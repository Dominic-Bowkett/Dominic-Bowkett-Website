A damp reading that creeps up near the skirting, a suspended timber floor with a joist end nobody can see, a loft hatch someone has painted shut over three redecorations — a striking amount of what a building survey turns on comes down to getting an edge under something and applying leverage without wrecking the thing you’re trying to look under. A damp meter tells you there’s a problem near the floor; a crowbar is what gets you close enough to see what it actually is.

It earns its place in the van for exactly that reason, not for demolition. Most of what gets asked of a crowbar on an inspection is a first, careful lift — a single floorboard to check a joist end, a section of skirting to look behind for damp, a stuck hatch rather than a stud wall. The difference between a good bar and a bad one shows up in precisely that moment: a thin, properly tapered blade slides under a board edge and lifts it cleanly, while a thick, blunt-ground import splits the tongue, cracks the board, or simply refuses to bite.

The list below runs from a bar shaped specifically around lifting floorboards through to a compact bar for tight voids, priced from a few pounds to genuine trade money. Read the buying-advice section even if you skip straight to the table — the difference between a drop-forged bar and a cast one isn’t marketing, it’s whether the tool bends the first time you actually lean on it.

How I judge a crowbar

Drop-forged, not cast. Forging works steel under pressure into a single dense, consistent piece; casting pours molten steel into a mould and can leave internal voids that stay invisible right up until the bar snaps or bends under load — usually at exactly the moment you’re relying on it not to. A listing that names “drop-forged” or “forged and heat-treated” is telling you something real; one that just says “steel” probably isn’t.

A properly tapered, thin blade. The hard part of most survey-related prying is getting a wedge started without a hammer — into a tongue-and-groove floorboard joint, or the gap behind a painted-shut skirting board. A bar ground to a fine taper slides in; a thick, flat-ground edge bounces off or splits the timber instead.

A claw that actually pulls nails. Look for a shaped, sharpened claw at one end, distinct from the flat chisel or lifting end — genuinely useful for pulling the fixings holding a board down, rather than levering blind against a nail head and snapping it off flush.

Length matched to the leverage you need. Most floorboard, skirting and hatch work calls for something in the 12–18in range, short enough to work in a tight loft or void. Anything much longer belongs to masonry and lintel work, not delicate first lifts.

One-piece construction over a bonded grip. A bar forged from a single piece of steel has no joint to fail under leverage; a moulded rubber grip is comfort, not structure — worth having, but not the thing to prioritise over the steel itself.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

“Forged” is a claim worth checking, not assuming. Drop-forged steel is worked under pressure into a dense, consistent piece; cast steel is poured into a mould and can carry internal voids invisible until the bar snaps or bends under load. If a listing doesn’t name the process, treat it as cast and price it accordingly — the cheapest bars on this list that do name “drop-forged” are still doing more for the money than an unlabelled import at the same price.

Tip thickness and taper over headline steel grade. A listing quoting a specific carbon-steel grade or tensile figure isn’t obviously better if the working edge in the product photos is ground thick and blunt. Look at the taper before the spec sheet — that’s what actually determines whether the bar bites cleanly under a board or bounces off.

Size matched to real jobs, not the biggest bar available. Most survey-related use is a first careful lift, not full demolition. A 12–18in bar covers the great majority of what comes up on an inspection; a 3ft-plus bar is a two-handed, both-feet-planted tool for masonry and lintels, and awkward to half-draw in a loft.

Permission and reinstatement, not the bar itself. What actually protects a report and a client relationship is asking before lifting anything structural or decorative, and being able to put it back afterwards. A line in the report — “floorboard lifted with the owner’s consent to inspect the joist below” — covers far more than any amount of extra spend on the tool that lifted it.

What the marketing pushes that you can skip. Paint colour, branded pouches and “heavy-duty” printed on a thin, unground edge are all decoration. None of it changes whether the taper bites cleanly or the steel bends first time you lean on it.

Questions I get asked

What’s the difference between a crowbar, a wrecking bar and a pry bar? In practice the names overlap heavily and manufacturers use them almost interchangeably. Where there is a distinction, “crowbar” and “wrecking bar” tend to describe a heavier, hexagonal or round-section bar built for demolition and general levering, while “pry bar” more often describes a flatter, thinner-bladed tool — like the Stanley Wonder Bar above — intended for delicate trim and nail work. Buy on the shape and taper of the working end, not the name on the listing.

What size crowbar do I need to lift a floorboard? For a single board in a domestic suspended timber floor, something in the 12–18in range with a thin, tapered end is usually enough — long enough for real leverage over a joist, short enough to work in a confined loft or stair void. Anything past about 24in is more leverage than most floorboard work needs, and becomes awkward to manoeuvre in a tight space.

Will a crowbar damage floorboards or skirting? Any bar can, if it’s the wrong shape or forced into the wrong place. A thin, properly ground taper worked gently into an existing joint — the tongue-and-groove edge of a board, or the gap behind a skirting board — does far less damage than a thick wedge hammered under the middle of a board. Where a floor covering or fitted skirting will be visibly marked, that’s worth flagging to the owner before lifting it, not just noting afterwards.

Is a drop-forged crowbar actually stronger than a cast one? Yes, meaningfully. Forging works the steel under pressure into a denser, more consistent grain structure, while casting pours molten steel into a mould and can leave internal voids that stay invisible until the bar fails under load — typically as a snapped tip or a bent shaft at exactly the moment you’re leaning on it. It’s the single biggest factor separating a bar that lasts years from one that doesn’t survive its first genuinely stuck board.

A crowbar rarely justifies its own line in an instruction, but the moment it comes out is often the moment a survey earns its fee — the difference between “floor covering prevented inspection” and an actual answer about the joists underneath.
