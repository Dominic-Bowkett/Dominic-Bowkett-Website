The best
site radios
for solo site work

A site radio earns its keep on the quiet jobs as much as the noisy ones — company through a long solo survey in an empty house, a hands-free way to take a call without balancing a phone on a joist, and one less charger fighting for a socket in the van. None of that needs a full PA system, just a unit that shares batteries with the tools already carried, actually picks up a signal where a phone's tuner won't, and survives being thrown in with the ladders. Here's what separates a site radio worth carrying from one that just takes up van space, and seven worth buying.

Most of a survey day is quiet by design — working room by room with a laser measure and a torch, waiting on an access appointment running late, or filling in notes at a kitchen table with the heating off. A radio isn't strictly necessary for any of that, but it's the difference between an unoccupied Victorian terrace feeling like a job and feeling like an unlit, echoing box for three hours straight.

The more practical use is taking a call hands-free while up a loft ladder or halfway through a roof void, without balancing a phone on a joist and hoping the connection holds. A site radio that shares batteries with the drill, torch or multitool already in the van saves carrying a second charger, and one with genuine DAB+ reception picks up a signal in places a phone's FM tuner or patchy 4G won't reach — useful on some of the more rural addresses this practice covers around Hartfield and the Weald.

How I judge a site radio

Battery platform before spec sheet. The best radio on paper is the wrong buy if it means carrying a second charger and battery format that doesn’t match anything else in the van. Whatever's already running the drill, torch or multitool usually decides the shortlist before sound quality gets a look-in.

DAB+ specifically, not just “DAB.” Newer stations increasingly launch on the DAB+ standard rather than the older format, and a radio that only lists “DAB” on the box may not decode them. Worth confirming DAB+ appears explicitly in the spec rather than assuming any digital radio covers it.

Bluetooth range that survives a stud wall, not an open field. Manufacturer figures are measured with a clear line of sight and drop off fast through brick, block or timber. What matters is whether a call holds from a loft to a phone left one floor down, not the number printed on the box.

A roll cage or a stated IP rating, not just a rugged-looking case. A radio living loose in a toolbox or the back of a van gets dropped and rained on eventually. Genuine impact protection and a proper ingress rating matter more day to day than an extra few decibels.

USB charging as a genuine bonus, not the reason to buy one. A port that tops up a phone or tablet on a long day off-grid is a real convenience, but it draws from the same battery running the radio — useful, not a substitute for a proper charger back at the van.

What actually matters — and what doesn't

Wattage and subwoofers matter for a crew, not one person in one room. The PACKOUT's 120dB and four-speaker spread suit a whole floor or an outdoor site with several trades working at once. For a single surveyor, a much smaller unit at a fraction of the price is genuinely enough.

DAB+ specifically is worth checking before buying. Newer stations increasingly launch DAB+ only, and a radio that lists just “DAB” on the box may not decode them — confirm DAB+ appears explicitly in the spec rather than assuming any digital radio covers it.

Bluetooth range on the box is measured in an open field, not through a house. A quoted 25–36m range drops sharply through brick, block or a few stud walls — expect a reliable connection across one or two rooms, not the full building.

A roll cage or a stated IP rating tells you more than the brand name. A radio living loose in a toolbox or the back of a van gets dropped and rained on eventually — the DeWalt's roll cage and the Makitas' IP65 rating are worth more day to day than extra decibels.

Buying into a new battery platform just for a radio rarely pays off. If the van's already running Makita, Milwaukee, DeWalt or Ryobi batteries, the radio on that same platform is the one that doesn't add a second charger — a slightly better radio on an unfamiliar platform is usually the wrong trade.

Questions I get asked

Does a site radio need to match my existing cordless tool battery?

Not strictly — every radio in this guide except the M18 RADDAB+G2-0 also runs from the mains lead. But buying on the same platform as the drill, torch or multitool already carried means one fewer charger competing for a socket in the van, and one less battery format to keep track of. Worth checking before buying if a battery platform's already established.

Will DAB+ reception work inside a stripped-out or basement property?

Patchily. DAB, like FM, is a broadly line-of-sight signal that struggles through dense brick, concrete or a below-ground level, and a stripped-out property with foil-backed insulation or metal studwork can shield a signal further still. A radio with a decent aerial near a window will usually do better than one buried in a back room — no model in this guide solves poor reception on its own.

Is Bluetooth range enough to take a call from a loft while the phone stays in the van?

Generally no, despite what the box says. Manufacturer figures of 25–36m are measured with a clear line of sight; a phone left in a van outside will usually drop the connection well before that, through brick, timber and a floor or two. Bluetooth on these units is more reliably useful for a phone left in the same room or one floor away, not across a whole property.

Can a site radio double as a phone or tablet charger on a long day?

Most of the units in this guide have a USB or USB-C port that will top up a phone, though it draws from the same battery running the radio rather than charging independently. On a long day with no mains socket nearby that's a genuine convenience — on a job with power available, plugging the radio into the mains and saving the battery for the tools is the better call.

A radio in the van doesn’t change what turns up in a report, but the long solo hours it makes more bearable are usually spent on exactly the kind of thorough, room-by-room inspection a building survey or EPC needs.
