The question I get asked in houses in multiple occupation is not whether emergency lighting is a good idea. It is whether this particular house needs it, and the honest answer is that the fire risk assessment decides. A two-storey shared house with a short, straight stair often does not. A converted Victorian with a long ground-floor corridor, a stair that turns, an internal lobby and no windows on the escape route often does, and so does anything a council licence names it for.

The legal footing is the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, which makes the responsible person assess the risk and keep escape routes usable, including when the lights go out. Local authority HMO licence conditions can then add specifics. The technical benchmark is BS 5266-1, the code of practice for emergency lighting, with BS EN 1838 setting the light levels and BS EN 50172 (BS 5266-8) covering testing. The fire risk assessment cost guide covers who does the assessment and what it picks up, and the HMO licence cost guide explains where licence conditions come in.

This is a features overview of seven products, not a design. Emergency lighting has to light the actual route, at the actual spacing, from the actual door to the actual street, and that is a layout question for an assessor or a competent designer. Bear in mind too that several cheap listings are 110V site-lighting units, which are no use in a UK house. I have left those out and every product below is a 230V mains fitting.

How I judge emergency lighting

Maintained or non-maintained? A non-maintained light is off until the supply fails. A maintained one is on all the time, as a normal light or a lit sign. In a shared house with a lit hall, non-maintained is normal. Maintained matters where people who do not know the building use it, which is more a licensed premises than a four-bed let.

How long does the battery last? The usual rating is three hours, and councils commonly ask for it where people sleep. A one-hour fitting is cheaper and sometimes acceptable, but only if your assessor says so in writing.

Is it built to the standard? A genuine fitting is designed to BS EN 60598-2-22 and the listing should say so. A listing that just says “emergency bulkhead” tells you the battery exists and nothing about whether it will light the floor.

Can it test itself? The standard asks for a brief functional test monthly and a full-duration test each year, recorded in a log. Self-testing units do this automatically and show a fault on a small LED. They cost more and save a surprising amount of effort.

Where does it go? IP65 for outside doors, cellars and damp porches. IP20 is fine inside a dry hall. Slimline suits a narrow stair. The weather rating is the thing people forget until a unit fills with condensation.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Position matters more than the fitting. The aim is that someone who has lost the lights can find the way to a place of safety. That means light over each exit, at changes of direction and level, at stairs, and outside the final exit. Seven well-chosen units in the right places beat twenty bought in a bundle.

Wiring matters. A fitting needs a permanent live supply so the battery keeps charging, taken from the lighting circuit it serves, so that when that circuit fails the light comes on. It is not a plug-in job. Ask the electrician to cover it alongside your periodic inspection; the EICR cost guide explains what that involves.

Testing and the log. Emergency lighting you never test is a battery you hope works. Test monthly, do the full-duration test annually, keep dates and results. Batteries typically need replacing every few years; the date is on the unit.

Emergency lighting is one part of the escape picture. Doors, alarms and the means of escape work together. See smoke and heat alarms, fire door closers and strips, key safes and the first-time landlord kit. If a power cut leaves tenants in a stairwell, a torch is a stopgap, not a plan; for personal kit, see rechargeable torches.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. Lumens figures with no standard behind them, colour temperature, and a fitting that looks smart. A battery light that glows in a hall for a minute is not emergency lighting.

Questions I get asked

Does my HMO need emergency lighting? Not automatically. It depends on the size and layout of the building, the length and complexity of the escape route, and your licence conditions. The fire risk assessment will say, and a council licence can require it where the assessment does not.

What is the difference between maintained and non-maintained? A maintained light is on all the time and stays on when the mains fails; a non-maintained light only comes on when the mains fails. Non-maintained suits most shared houses.

How often should emergency lighting be tested? BS 5266-8 describes a short monthly test and a full-duration test annually, with the results logged. Self-testing fittings automate the routine, but you still need to read the indicators.

Can I fit emergency lighting myself? The fittings are simple, but the supply is not. They need a permanent live and the right circuit, so use a competent electrician and ask for a certificate for the work.

Escape routes are one of the first things I look at in a shared house or a conversion, and a missing or dead emergency light is a quick fix before it becomes a licence or insurance problem. A buy to let survey will note them, and getting in touch is the way to arrange one. For the rest of the kit, there are more field-kit guides.
