waterproof jackets

A decent chunk of any building survey happens outside, and none of it waits for good weather: the elevation walk-round, the roof pitch and gutter line checked from ground level or by drone, the garden and outbuildings, the DPC line around the base of the walls, the external condition section of an EPC assessment. Rain doesn’t move the appointment, and neither does a cold wind off the Weald in February. Most kit in a survey bag gets reached for occasionally — a waterproof jacket gets worn most days between October and April, and plenty of the rest of the year besides.

That everyday use is exactly why a repurposed hiking coat or a bargain-bin cagoule tends to disappoint. A survey jacket needs pockets that actually fit a phone, a laser measure or a damp meter rather than just car keys, sleeves that move properly when reaching up a downpipe or crouching at a DPC line, and a hood that stays put in wind without blocking the view of a ladder’s footing. Some sites also specify hi-vis at the gate regardless of what’s underfoot, which is a different jacket to the one worn for a quiet residential homebuyer survey.

How I judge a waterproof jacket

A genuine waterproof rating, not a “shower resistant” softshell wearing the word loosely. A properly waterproof jacket carries a hydrostatic head rating — usually quoted in millimetres — tested against a column of water, combined with taped seams at every stitch line. A softshell treated with a durable water repellent (DWR) finish beads water off the surface, which is fine for light drizzle and nowhere near enough for standing in driving rain on a roof inspection.

Breathability, because most of the wet on a survey comes from the inside. Climbing a ladder, crouching at a DPC line or walking a long garden generates enough heat that a genuinely non-breathable shell leaves a surveyor wetter from sweat than the rain would have. It’s the reason the technical hillwalking-style shells in this guide earn their place over cheaper, thicker jackets that are waterproof on paper alone.

Room to move: reaching, crouching, kneeling, climbing. Pre-bent or articulated sleeves and a dropped back hem matter more on site than they do standing still, which is exactly the difference between a jacket designed for tradespeople and one designed for a dog walk. It’s also why a full day in a jacket cut for standing around gets uncomfortable fast once the actual job starts.

Hi-vis when the site asks for it, not worn as a default. Most residential survey work is better served by a plain jacket — hi-vis reads as unnecessary, occasionally alarming, on a domestic doorstep. It earns its place for managed sites, new-build developments and any job with a site induction that specifies it, which is worth keeping one jacket in the van for rather than buying as the everyday choice.

Small enough to live in the bag, for the day the forecast turns without warning. The best waterproof jacket is the one that’s actually there when an unplanned shower catches a garden walk-round, not the technical shell left at home because it takes up a third of the boot. What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The hydrostatic head number matters less past about 10,000mm than the seams and zips do. A jacket rated to 20,000mm is not meaningfully drier in normal UK rain than one rated to 10,000mm if the seams aren’t taped or the front zip isn’t storm-flapped — water finds the gap before it finds the fabric. Past that point, breathability and durability are the numbers worth comparing, not the waterproof rating printed on the label.

Breathability is not marketing padding for a survey jacket specifically. Ladder work, reaching into a downpipe or just walking a long garden generates enough heat that a genuinely non-breathable shell gets a surveyor wetter from sweat than most showers would. It’s the reason the Berghaus and the Mac in a Sac earn their place above cheaper, thicker jackets that are waterproof on paper alone.

Hi-vis is a site requirement, not a default upgrade. Most residential survey work is better served by a jacket that doesn’t read as a contractor’s to a homeowner opening the door, and the hi-vis jackets here earn their place for managed and commercial sites specifically, not as the one jacket worn for every job.

A softshell labelled “water resistant” is not a waterproof jacket, whatever the marketing implies. Softshells breathe brilliantly and move well, but most rely on a durable water repellent finish that beads water rather than a genuine waterproof membrane — fine for light drizzle, not for standing in driving rain on a roof inspection.

Packed size decides whether a jacket actually gets carried. The best waterproof jacket is the one that’s in the bag when the forecast turns without warning, not the technical shell left at home because it takes up too much of the van.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a hi-vis waterproof jacket for a residential survey?

For most homebuyer surveys and building surveys, no — a hi-vis jacket reads as unnecessary, occasionally slightly alarming, on a domestic doorstep, and a plain waterproof jacket does the job. It earns its place for managed sites, new-build developments, housing association instructions or anywhere with a site induction that specifies it regardless of the property type, which is worth keeping one in the van for rather than buying by default.

What’s the actual difference between a waterproof jacket and a water-resistant one?

Waterproof means a genuine membrane or coated fabric rated to a hydrostatic head, usually 5,000mm or higher, tested to resist a column of water without leaking, combined with taped seams at the stitch lines. Water-resistant, or a durable water repellent (DWR) finish, only makes water bead off the surface rather than stopping it penetrating — fine for light drizzle, not for standing in prolonged rain on an external elevation.

Is a hardshell or a softshell better for survey work?

A hardshell — the Berghaus, Snickers, Dickies and the hi-vis Portwest jackets here — is the safer default because it’s genuinely waterproof rather than just water-resistant. A softshell breathes and moves better and is worth carrying for a dry, cold day, but very few softshells sold as “waterproof” actually meet the same rating, so it’s a jacket for a forecast that’s merely threatening rather than one that’s already raining.

How long should a decent waterproof jacket last with regular site use?

A dedicated workwear shell like the Snickers or Dickies, built with heavier fabric and reinforced stitching, should manage several years of weekly use before the waterproofing needs reproofing or the seams start to fail. Lighter hillwalking-style shells like the Berghaus, or packable jackets, will need a wash-in reproofing treatment sooner — the DWR finish wears off with regular use well before the membrane itself fails, and a jacket that’s stopped beading water is usually a reproofing job, not a replacement one.
