Every drainage job, trial pit and trench starts with the same question: what is under the spade? A great many of the surveys I do involve a lawn, a driveway or a side passage that someone wants to dig up, and the honest answer is usually “I don’t know until it has been scanned”. The tool that answers it is a cable avoidance tool, a CAT, and the signal generator that goes with it, a Genny.

The first thing to say is that this is the category in this series where I would most often tell you not to buy. A professional CAT and Genny kit costs £1,000 or more, it needs training to use properly, and it is no guarantee: HSE guidance in HSG47, Avoiding danger from underground services, is clear that a scanner is one part of a safe system of work, alongside plans, a permit to dig and careful hand digging. If you dig twice a year, hire one, or pay someone with the kit and the training. The drainage survey cost guide and the topographical survey cost guide show where that work sits.

If you do dig regularly, or run a practice that sets out trial pits, the kit below is what I would look at. It is worth checking the listing and the supplier for each, because Amazon is not where most of this equipment is sold and stock on the marketplace comes and goes.

How I judge a cable avoidance tool

What it can actually find. A CAT detects two things passively: the 50Hz field from live mains cables, and radio signals re-radiated by long metallic services. Anything else, such as a dead cable, a water main, a gas pipe or a duct, needs a Genny to put a signal on it. A tool that cannot be paired with a generator leaves large gaps.

What it cannot find. Plastic gas and water pipes with no tracer wire, fibre optic cables with no metal, and anything deeper than the tool’s range. A clear scan is not a clear site. Treat a quiet reading as “found nothing”, never as “nothing there”.

Training and calibration. The tool is only as good as the person using it. Look for a supplier or course that provides operator training, and a service interval: a detector that has not been checked against a known signal is a gamble. This is why the professional range is bought from specialists, not casually.

Ease of use in a muddy trench. Large controls, a loud audible response and a simple layout matter more than a display. You will be wearing gloves.

Total cost. A detector without a generator is half a kit. Price the pair, the batteries and the training, then compare it with hire at roughly £30 to £60 a day.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Hire is the honest option. For most surveyors a tool hire shop or a utility locating contractor makes more sense than buying. You pay by the day, the kit is calibrated and often comes with a quick briefing. Only buy if you will use it enough to cover the cost and the training.

Plans first. Before scanning, get the statutory undertakers’ plans. A scan interprets what is there; the plan tells you what ought to be there, and the two together are safer than either alone. Dig carefully by hand near any indicated service, and use insulated hand tools.

Don’t pay for the extras. Data logging, Bluetooth and app connections are sold hard and are rarely what keeps a spade out of a cable. A reliable detector, a compatible generator and an operator who knows what the sounds mean are the whole specification.

When a CAT is the wrong answer. If you want to know where the drains run, a CAT will not do it; a set of drain rods or a CCTV drainage survey will. If you want to drill a wall, you want the cable detectors in the earlier guide.

Questions I get asked

What is a CAT and genny? A CAT is a cable avoidance tool, a hand-held detector that picks up the signal from live cables and some buried metal services. A Genny is the signal generator that puts a tone onto a cable or pipe so the CAT can find one that is not live. Used together, they cover more of what is under the ground than either does alone.

Can I buy a cable avoidance tool on Amazon? Some are listed, usually through marketplace sellers, and stock changes. The benefit of Amazon is mostly convenience, and the price is similar to a specialist’s. Check the seller, the model code and what is in the box before you order.

Is a cable detector the same as a cable avoidance tool? No. A wall scanner such as the Bosch D-tect looks for cables inside walls and floors; a cable avoidance tool looks for services underground before digging. The names get muddled in searches, but the jobs and the price are very different.

Do I need training to use a CAT? In practice, yes. HSG47 expects those using locating equipment to be trained and to follow a safe system of work. Most suppliers and training providers run a short course, and it is worth doing before the first dig.
