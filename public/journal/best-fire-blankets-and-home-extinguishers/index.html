By the time I’m asked to look at a landlord’s fire precautions, the kitchen fire has almost always happened somewhere else first — a scorched hob, a blackened extractor, a tenant’s account of what they smothered a chip pan with because there was nothing better to hand. Cooking is still the most common cause of accidental fires in UK homes, and the ones that get out of control fast are the ones where nobody had a blanket or an extinguisher near the kitchen door.

There’s no single national law that says every rented home must have either. What drives the paperwork is the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, which puts a duty on the “responsible person” — usually the landlord or managing agent — to risk-assess any shared or converted building and provide “appropriate” firefighting equipment where that assessment calls for it. Most councils still inspect licensed HMOs against the old LACORS housing fire safety guidance, which expects a fire blanket in every kitchen, shared or not, and often a rated extinguisher in the common parts. For a single self-contained let, neither is a strict statutory requirement — but it’s the kind of gap the Housing Health and Safety Rating System flags under “fire,” and the kind of thing an insurer or a letting agent’s checklist asks about regardless.

This guide covers both halves of the kit: the blanket every kitchen should have whatever else is fitted, and the four common types of portable extinguisher — powder, carbon dioxide, foam and water mist — each with a genuine BS EN 1869 or Kitemark-to-BS-EN-3 certification, not just a CE sticker an importer added themselves. Seven worth buying, from an £11 budget blanket to a marine-grade water mist unit, and a bundle if you’re equipping more than one property at once.

How I judge a fire blanket and extinguisher

The certification on the label, not the word on the box. “Fire safety” and “heavy duty” printed on cheap packaging mean nothing on their own. A fire blanket needs Kitemark certification to BS EN 1869; a portable extinguisher needs Kitemark certification to BS EN 3. Both are independently tested by BSI — a CE mark alone, which any importer can self-apply, isn’t the same thing.

The fire class actually matches the risk. A blanket, or a Class F-rated extinguisher, for cooking oil fires; a standard ABC powder or foam extinguisher for general combustibles; CO2 or water mist where live electrics are involved, because powder residue inside a consumer unit or a laptop is its own expensive problem afterwards.

Something a panicking person can operate with no training. A blanket needs none. An extinguisher needs the PASS sequence — pull the pin, aim at the base, squeeze the handle, sweep side to side — printed clearly enough that a tenant reads it for the first time while the fire is already burning.

Disposable domestic kit, sized for the room it’s protecting. Nearly everything here is a five-year-guarantee, buy-it-once unit, not the annually-serviced commercial extinguisher a fire risk assessor specifies for a shared stairwell under BS 5306-3 — fine for a single let, worth checking if you’re fitting out communal areas in a larger HMO. A 2kg extinguisher is plenty for a flat kitchen; the 6kg commercial units sold alongside them are heavier than most people can operate one-handed under stress.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Never water, never straight from the tap, on a fat fire. Water flashes to steam almost instantly on burning fat and can throw the fireball across the kitchen — the single most repeated warning in UK fire services’ cooking-fire guidance. A blanket, or a Class F-rated extinguisher, smothers it; nothing from the sink should go near a chip pan.

The class rating matters more than the kg or litre figure on the tin. A 2kg extinguisher rated 13A puts out a bigger test fire than a 3kg unit rated 8A. The letters and numbers are the tested performance; the weight is just how much agent is inside the canister.

CO2 doesn’t cool anything. It displaces oxygen and smothers the flame, but the material underneath stays hot enough to reignite once the gas disperses — the right call for an electrical fire you can then isolate at the fuse board, not a substitute for powder or foam on ordinary combustibles.

What the listings push that doesn’t matter. “Heavy duty” and a bigger kg number on an otherwise identical extinguisher are marketing copy, not a rating, and a CE-only sticker on a cheaper bundle isn’t the same as the BSI Kitemark. If a licence condition asks for BS EN 3 or BS EN 1869, check the listing states it in those words.

Questions I get asked

Do landlords have to provide a fire blanket or extinguisher by law? Not as a blanket rule for every rented home. The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 requires a fire risk assessment for the common parts of any shared or converted building and “appropriate” equipment where that assessment calls for it. Many councils inspect licensed HMOs against the old LACORS fire safety guidance, which typically expects a blanket in every kitchen and often an extinguisher in shared areas. A single self-contained let has no such statutory requirement, though it’s standard practice and something insurers and agents commonly ask about.

Powder, CO2, foam or water mist — which one for a rental property? Match it to the room. Powder is the most versatile for general combustibles and gas but corrosive to electronics and unpleasant to breathe in a small space; CO2 suits a consumer unit, boiler or any live electrical equipment because it leaves no residue; foam sits between the two, cleaner than powder on general fires; water mist covers the broadest range of fire classes safely, including near a person, but costs several times more than the others.

Can I use water on a chip pan or cooking oil fire? No — never. Water sinks below burning fat, flashes to steam almost instantly and can throw the burning oil across the room. A fire blanket laid gently over the pan, or a Class F-rated extinguisher, is the only safe response; turn off the heat and leave it covered until completely cool.

Where should a fire blanket or extinguisher be mounted in a kitchen? By the door, not the cooker — the escape route, not the hazard. A blanket mounted beside the hob is exactly the spot you can’t reach once that’s what’s on fire.

For the wider compliance picture, a fire risk assessment is usually where a fire blanket or extinguisher shortfall gets flagged, alongside interlinked smoke and heat alarms and a key safe for out-of-hours access.
