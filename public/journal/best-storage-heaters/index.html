A good few of the RdSAP assessments I do are on flats and cottages with no gas main anywhere near them — storage heaters from the 1970s or 80s, a single dial with no thermostat worth the name, and a D or E rating that the current heating system is dragging down almost on its own. A high heat retention storage heater is a genuine, no-rewiring upgrade for that specific house: same off-peak tariff, same two-cable arrangement, a proper SAP appliance category instead of an old resistance one. It won't turn an electric-only home into a heat-pump-rated property, and I'd rather say that up front than let the marketing imply it. Here's how I'd judge one, and six worth a look.

A storage heater does one simple thing: it uses cheap off-peak electricity overnight to heat a block of dense ceramic bricks inside its case, then releases that stored heat through the day as the room needs it. The 1970s and 80s versions most people picture — a crude dial, a manual damper flap, no real feedback on what's happening inside the case — store roughly what they're told to regardless of the weather and release it on their own schedule, whether anyone's home or not. A high heat retention (HHR) unit is a different appliance behind a similar-looking case: better insulation around the core, a controller that adjusts the overnight charge to the forecast and the room's actual temperature, and a retention rating of at least 45% of stored heat still available 24 hours after a full charge — the figure Lot 20 of the EU Ecodesign regulation sets as the category baseline.

On an EPC, that difference matters more than most homeowners expect. RdSAP scores a heating system partly on its own efficiency and partly on how well it's controlled, and an old manual storage heater with no thermostat scores badly on both counts — sometimes badly enough to be the single biggest drag on an otherwise reasonable property. Swap it for a modern HHR unit with electronic charge control and the software recognises a materially better appliance, not just a newer-looking box. That's a genuinely low-disruption fix on a property with no gas main and no realistic case for a heat pump yet: no new pipework, just confirming the existing Economy 7 or Economy 10 circuit suits the new unit.

The honest caveat, and the one most affiliate pages skip: a storage heater upgrade improves the heating-system score, not the fabric of the house. If the real problem is a solid wall with no insulation or a loft that's never been topped up, new heaters on their own will move the rating a little and leave the running cost mostly where it was. Worth knowing which one you're actually solving before spending the money — more on that below, after the six units I'd look at.

How I judge a storage heater

Retention percentage, not just the “HHR” label. Lot 20 sets 45% as the floor for high heat retention; several units below sit at 45–49%, a meaningful difference in how much of an overnight charge is still usable heat by the following evening. Ask for the figure rather than trusting the badge alone.

Electronic charge control that actually learns. The genuine gain over an old unit is a controller that adjusts the overnight charge against room temperature, not a fixed dial set once and left. A basic 24-hour timer without adaptive charging is a smaller step up than the marketing suggests.

What it needs from the existing circuit. Every unit here runs on a standard Economy 7-style off-peak supply through the same two-cable arrangement an old storage heater already used. Confirm the existing circuit and fuse rating suit the new unit's input wattage before ordering — a same-wattage swap rarely needs an electrician beyond a competent DIY changeover; stepping up in size might.

Fan-assisted or natural convection. A fan-boosted unit releases heat faster on demand, useful in a room only occupied in the evening, but it adds a moving part and running noise a purely convective HHR case doesn't have.

Smart control as a genuine feature, not a headline. WiFi or app control is useful on a property you don't occupy every day; on a heater someone lives with full-time, a clear on-unit display and a sensible weekly programme often matter more than an app nobody opens after the first week.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

It improves the heating system's EPC score, not the building fabric. RdSAP marks a heating system on its efficiency and its controls; a modern HHR unit scores meaningfully better than an old manual storage heater on both. It does nothing to a solid wall with no insulation, a cold loft, or single glazing that's actually driving the running cost. If that's the real problem, a storage heater upgrade will nudge the rating and leave the bill mostly unchanged — worth checking what's actually being marked down first, which is exactly what a full EPC assessment tells you (see what an EPC costs if you haven't had one recently).

Off-peak tariff eligibility is the whole economic case. None of this makes sense on a standard single-rate tariff, where electricity used overnight costs the same as electricity used at 6pm. Confirm an Economy 7, Economy 10 or comparable time-of-use tariff is actually available and switched on before buying; without it, a storage heater is simply a worse radiator than a direct electric panel heater.

Retention percentage is the number that separates "HHR" from "storage heater." A unit genuinely clearing 45%+ overnight-to-evening retention behaves noticeably differently from one that doesn't — less heat wasted mid-morning, more still available by the time people are home in the evening. Ask for the figure rather than trusting a general "energy efficient" description.

A heat pump isn't always the answer it's presented as. On a gas-free property with a small heat demand and a tight budget, a modern HHR storage heater can be the pragmatic near-term fix while a heat pump conversion is planned or saved for. That's a fabric-first question a PAS 2035 retrofit assessment answers properly, rather than guessing from a single appliance swap.

What the marketing pushes that matters less than it sounds. A colour app dashboard is pleasant to use but doesn't change how much heat the case actually retains. "Smart" and "WiFi-enabled" describe the control layer, not the thermal performance — check the retention percentage and warranty length before the app screenshots.

Questions I get asked

Will new storage heaters actually improve my EPC rating? Usually yes, often by more than people expect, because RdSAP scores both the heating system's efficiency and how well it's controlled. An old manual unit with no thermostat can be dragging the whole assessment down; a modern HHR unit is recognised as a materially better appliance. It won't touch the fabric side of the rating, though — insulation and glazing are separate line items.

Do I need an electrician to fit one? A straight swap for an existing unit of similar wattage, on the same circuit, is within reach of a competent DIY changeover for some models, but always check the existing wiring and fuse rating first, and have a qualified electrician make the connection if there's any doubt. Stepping up significantly in wattage needs a proper electrical assessment.

What's the real difference between HHR and a standard storage heater? Retention percentage and control quality. An HHR unit is built to keep at least 45% of an overnight charge available 24 hours later; a standard Lot 20 unit still has dynamic charge calculation over the crude dial-and-damper units of the 1970s and 80s, but doesn't claim the same retention figure, and costs noticeably less as a result.

Would I be better off with a heat pump instead? Possibly, but not automatically — it's a fabric-and-budget question, not a straight heater-for-heater swap. A heat pump needs a building that can run at a lower flow temperature efficiently, plus a much bigger upfront cost. For a poorly insulated home, the insulation usually needs sorting first regardless of which heating system follows it.
