A borescope is only as useful as the hole you can get it through, and most of the holes I would drill on a survey are 10mm, through a mortar joint or a brick face, into a cavity that may or may not be full of something. The drill gets the attention. The bit does the work. A blunt or wrong bit turns a thirty-second job into a hot, dusty argument with a Victorian wall.

There are two families. Round-shank masonry bits go in an ordinary chuck and suit a combi drill in hammer mode. SDS-plus bits lock into an SDS drill and let the hammer do the work, which is far faster in hard brick and concrete. Which you need depends on the drill you own, so start there.

Old walls make the choice harder than it looks. Soft lime-mortared brick takes any bit, hard engineering brick and dense Victorian stock wear a cheap bit out in a few holes, and a concrete lintel or a flint-faced wall will stop a combi altogether. I read the wall before I choose the bit, and I would sooner drill a mortar joint than a brick face wherever I have the choice, because the joint is softer and far easier to make good afterwards.

Before drilling anything, check for cables and pipes with a cable detector, wear safety glasses and a respirator, and get the owner’s permission. In a listed building, ask the conservation officer before you drill at all.

How I judge a masonry drill bit

Shank to match the drill. SDS-plus in an SDS drill, round shank in a combi. Putting a round-shank bit in a hammer drill is how bits get blunt and chucks get damaged. Decide the drill first.

The sizes you will use. For survey work that is mostly 6, 8, 10 and 12mm. A 10mm covers most borescope probes. A 50-piece box is mostly sizes that will never leave the case.

Carbide tip quality. The tip is what cuts. Branded bits use better carbide and brazing, so they stay on size and do not shed the tip in hard brick. A cheap bit goes blunt after a few holes and starts to wander.

Dust clearing. The flute design carries dust out of the hole. A bit that clogs overheats and slows. Pull it out regularly to clear the hole, whatever it says on the box.

Length. A cavity wall of 270mm to 300mm through brick, cavity and block needs a longer bit than a standard short one. Check the working length, not just the overall length.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Buy sizes, not piece count. A 40-piece set looks like value and is mostly sizes you will not use. Buy a small branded set and replace the 10mm when it wears.

Sharp beats new. A bit that has stopped cutting will not recover with more pressure. Replace it. Leaning on a drill with a worn bit heats the tip and ruins it faster.

Ignore multi-material claims. Bits sold for “wood, metal and masonry” do none of them well. Use a masonry bit for masonry.

Be wary of unbranded sets. Cheap sets quote impressive hardness and often fail on a hard Victorian brick. For a few holes a month the branded set is cheaper in the end.

When a bit is the wrong answer. If you only need to see into a cavity, a mortar joint is easier to drill and to make good than a brick face. In a listed or rendered wall, do not drill without advice at all.

Questions I get asked

What size drill bit for a borescope? Most inspection borescopes need 8 to 12mm, so a 10mm bit is the sensible default. Check your camera’s probe diameter in the borescope guide and allow a millimetre of clearance.

Can I use SDS bits in a normal drill? No. The shank is a different design and only fits an SDS chuck. Round-shank bits can go in an SDS drill only with an adaptor, which is not worth the bother.

Do I need a hammer action for masonry bits? Yes, in brick and block. Without hammer mode a masonry bit mostly grinds. Hammer mode is the switch on the drill, and you turn it off for timber and plasterboard.

Should I drill the brick or the mortar joint? The mortar joint, where you can reach it. It is softer, quicker to drill and easier to repoint invisibly. Brick faces chip and show the repair, so keep brick holes for when the joint is in the wrong place.

How long does a masonry bit last? It depends on the wall. A good bit may drill dozens of holes in soft brick and far fewer in dense engineering brick or concrete. When progress slows or the bit runs hot, replace it.
