Noise doesn’t leave a stain on a ceiling, which is probably why it gets underweighted on a lot of survey instructions. But it comes up constantly in the parts of the job that never make it into the photographs: a tenant convinced a neighbour’s television comes through the party wall, a seller disputing that a new junction layout has ruined the garden, a retrofit client certain the MVHR unit fitted last month is louder than the fan it replaced. In every one of those conversations, a number beats an opinion.

A handheld decibel meter is not the instrument that settles a statutory nuisance case — that matters more than which model you buy, and I come back to it below. What one is good for is turning “it’s really noisy” into “68dB(A) at the window with the extractor running, 42dB(A) with it off”: a fact a client or neighbour can work with. Read the buying-advice section even if you skip straight to the table. What separates a useful meter from a toy isn’t a flashy display — it’s honest weighting, a sensible response speed, and a windscreen that means you’re measuring the room, not the draught.

How I judge a decibel meter

A-weighting as standard. Human hearing isn’t equally sensitive across all frequencies, so dB(A) rolls the low end off to approximate what a person perceives — the scale almost every noise-nuisance standard is written around. dB(C) is flatter and matters for low-frequency thump — plant, bass, traffic rumble — so a meter offering both tells you more than one that only does A.

A real fast/slow switch. Fast response tracks a spike — a door slam, a passing van; slow response averages it out, closer to how a sustained nuisance actually reads. A meter that only does one speed only answers half the question.

Max/min hold. A single instantaneous reading is nearly useless for anything intermittent. Capturing a peak while you’re not staring at the screen — a passing HGV, an extractor kicking into boost — turns a meter into a record rather than a glance.

A windscreen, not an afterthought. Wind noise across a bare microphone adds several decibels of pure artefact, which matters enormously outdoors. A meter sold without one, or treating one as an optional accessory, is telling you how seriously outdoor use was considered.

Honesty about compliance. A handful of these genuinely meet IEC 61672 or EN 60651 Class 2; most budget imports simply don’t claim it, which is more honest than a vague “professional” sticker with no standard named. Knowing which camp a meter sits in tells you what its number is worth.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A reading is not a nuisance determination. This is the single most important thing on this page. Statutory nuisance under the Environmental Protection Act 1990 isn’t triggered by crossing a fixed decibel number — there isn’t one. An environmental health officer judges nuisance by effect: whether it unreasonably interferes with use and enjoyment of a property, considering time of day, duration, character and locality, using published guideline levels (WHO’s night-noise guidance, BS 8233) as context rather than a threshold. A £20–£150 handheld meter gives a genuinely useful figure for a conversation or a report. It is not, on its own, evidence a council will act on.

Where the evidence trail actually runs. Formal noise-nuisance investigation uses a calibrated, traceable instrument (often Class 1), a protocol that separates background noise from the specific source, and often continuous logging over several nights — usually carried out by the council’s environmental health team or an independent acoustic consultant. If a dispute is heading anywhere formal, a spot reading from any meter here is a starting point for the conversation, not a substitute for that process.

dB(A) is what you want almost always. Unless you’re chasing low-frequency plant hum or bass transmission, leave the meter on A-weighting — switching to C for a general reading just makes the number harder to compare against published guidance.

A windscreen outdoors is not optional. Wind noise across a bare capsule reads as several decibels of nothing. If a garden or roadside reading didn’t come from a meter with one fitted, don’t trust the number.

What the marketing pushes that you can skip. “Professional” on the box means nothing without a named standard behind it. Bluetooth “AI noise analysis” on a £25 meter is decoration on hardware that can’t back it up. Spend the money on the microphone, the windscreen and the weighting instead.

Questions I get asked

What decibel level counts as a noise nuisance? There isn’t a fixed legal number in England and Wales — statutory nuisance is judged on effect, not a threshold, by the local environmental health team. Guideline figures (such as WHO’s roughly 30–35dB LAeq for undisturbed sleep) are useful context, not a line that automatically triggers action. If you’re measuring for your own understanding, compare with and without the noise source in the same conditions rather than chasing an absolute number.

Can I just use a phone app instead of buying a meter? For a rough sense of whether something is loud, yes. Phone microphones are optimised for voice, aren’t weighted or calibrated, and vary wildly between handsets — studies comparing apps against reference meters typically find errors of several decibels either way, with no windscreen. Fine for “is that quieter than before”; not something to hand a neighbour as evidence.

What’s the real difference between dB(A) and dB(C)? A-weighting rolls off low and very high frequencies to mimic human hearing, which is why it’s the standard for nuisance work. C-weighting is close to flat, so it better captures low-frequency content — plant machinery, bass through a party wall, distant traffic rumble — that A-weighting underplays. A meter offering both tells you whether a problem is tonal or a low-end thump, which changes what the fix looks like.

Do I need a calibrated, certified meter for a survey report? Depends what the number is for. An indicative comment — “background road noise was noticeable at the front elevation” — doesn’t need laboratory calibration behind it. A reading that might end up in front of an environmental health officer or a court does, and at that point you’re commissioning a calibrated instrument and a competent acoustic consultant, not buying a meter off this page.
