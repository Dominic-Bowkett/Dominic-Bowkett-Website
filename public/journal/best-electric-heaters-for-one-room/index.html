The best
electric heaters
for heating just one room

When one room is cold and the rest of the house isn’t, the instinct is to reach for a portable heater rather than turn the whole system up. That’s often the right call — but only if the room needs occasional top-up heat rather than a permanent workaround for cold that a fifteen-minute check would explain. Half the calls I get about a house feeling too expensive to heat aren’t about the boiler — they’re about one specific room. A converted garage that never quite joined the heating system, a box room used as a home office three days a week, a bay-windowed lounge that a Victorian terrace’s single loop of radiators was never really sized for. The honest answer in a lot of those cases isn’t a bigger boiler or a system upgrade — it’s a portable heater doing a job the whole-house system was never asked to do.

What it isn’t is a like-for-like swap with gas central heating on cost. Electricity in the UK runs at roughly three to four times the price per kWh of mains gas, so an electric heater only comes out ahead financially when it lets you turn the rest of the system down or off — heating the one occupied room instead of the whole house through a cold evening, not running on top of a heating system that’s already on.

There’s also a persistent myth worth clearing up before the list: every plug-in electric heater here converts electricity to heat at close to 100% efficiency, whatever the box says about the technology inside. A 2kW oil-filled radiator, a 2kW convector and a 2kW infrared panel all cost exactly the same to run for an hour. What actually differs is how fast the room feels warm, whether the heat lingers after it switches off, and whether it’s warming the air or the people in it — which is what the picks below are ranked on, not invented efficiency claims. And if a room needs one running for hours every evening all winter just to be usable, that’s usually telling you something about the room itself, worth a look before treating the heater as the permanent fix — exactly what an EPC assessment turns up, and a decent draught-proofing pass is often cheaper than a winter of running one every evening.

How I judge an electric room heater

What it actually costs to run. Every heater here is a resistive electric heater, so the running cost is wattage times hours times the unit price of electricity — full stop. A 2kW heater on for three hours costs the same whether it’s an oil radiator, a convector or an infrared panel, regardless of what the marketing implies about “efficiency.” A plug-in energy monitor will confirm exactly what a specific heater is costing, for a few pounds.

Response time versus retained heat. A ceramic or convector heater warms the air fast and goes cold the moment it’s switched off; an oil-filled radiator takes longer to get going but keeps radiating for twenty minutes or more after it cuts out, which suits a room used for a whole evening rather than a quick top-up.

Whether it heats the air or heats you. Infrared panels warm surfaces and people directly rather than the air in the room, which matters in a draughty space, a conservatory, or anywhere air changes fast enough that a convector is just heating air that leaves through a gap in the frame.

A real thermostat, not just heat settings. “Low, medium, high” is a fixed output; a thermostat cycles the heater to hold a set temperature, which is what actually stops it running flat out all evening once the room’s warm enough.

Safety features for something left running. Tip-over cut-off and overheat protection matter more than most of the spec sheet if this is going in a room with pets, kids, or is likely to get left on while nobody’s watching it.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

“Efficient” infrared marketing is mostly noise. Every heater here converts electricity to heat at close to 100% efficiency at the point of use — that’s just how resistive electric heating works. Claims that infrared is inherently more “efficient” than a convector are really claims about how it’s used — heating people rather than a whole volume of air — not about the physics of the element.

Wattage sets the running cost, not the technology. A 2kW heater costs the same to run for an hour whether it’s an oil radiator, a convector or an infrared panel. A listing promising lower bills at the same wattage is making a marketing claim, not an electrical one.

A thermostat saves more than an extra heat setting ever will. A heater that cycles to hold a set temperature uses meaningfully less electricity over an evening than one left running on “high” with no way to tell it the room’s warm enough.

Double-check wattage against the listing title, not just the product name. A few infrared panels I looked at while researching this guide had listing titles and spec bullets that didn’t match — one seller’s higher-wattage listing reused the identical photo and copy of a lower-wattage model. Check the spec sheet or plug rating, not just the headline number.

If a room needs one running every evening all winter, that’s not really a heater problem. A portable heater bridging an occasional cold room, a converted outbuilding, or a home office on set hours is sensible. One running for hours every evening because a room is permanently cold points at the fabric of the house — draughts, missing loft insulation, single glazing — worth an honest look before accepting it as the new normal. That’s what an EPC assessment or a retrofit assessment is for.

Questions I get asked

Is it cheaper to heat one room electrically than run the whole central heating system? Only if it lets you turn the rest of the system down or off. Electricity costs roughly three to four times as much per kWh as mains gas, so an electric heater running on top of gas central heating that’s already on is adding cost, not saving it. It only wins financially when it replaces heating a house-full of rooms with heating the one that’s occupied.

Are infrared heaters actually cheaper to run than an oil-filled radiator? Watt for watt, no — a 400W infrared panel costs less to run than a 2000W oil radiator because it’s using a fifth of the power, not because infrared is inherently more efficient. Compare heaters of the same wattage and the running cost is identical; what differs is what the heat actually does once it’s produced.

Can I leave an oil-filled radiator on overnight? Check the manufacturer’s guidance for the specific model, since this varies. Look for tip-over cut-off and overheat protection as a minimum, and treat it like any other unattended appliance — nothing flammable nearby, and not relied on for months without checking it’s still behaving as it should.

Will a room heater help with condensation in a cold room? Indirectly, and only a little. Electric heaters don’t add moisture the way an unflued gas or paraffin heater does, so they’re not making it worse. But warming a room without addressing ventilation just moves where condensation happens rather than solving it — that’s a job for proper ventilation, not a heater. Worth a look at PIV units or a dehumidifier if condensation is the actual complaint.

None of these fix a house that’s expensive to heat — they manage one room, honestly and cheaply, while something bigger gets sorted, or if it never needs sorting at all. If the real question is why a room, or the whole house, needs this much help to stay warm, an EPC assessment is the place to start, or a full retrofit assessment if the aim is to bring the running costs down properly.
