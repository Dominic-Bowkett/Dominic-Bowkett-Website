Drones for
building surveys:
the 2026 rules

The UK drone rules changed on 1 January 2026. For surveyors, the biggest change isn’t the paperwork — it’s which drone you can legally fly over a residential street. Here’s what changed, what to check before you fly, and what a drone roof survey can and can’t honestly tell you.

What changed on 1 January 2026

The Unmanned Aircraft (Amendment) Regulations 2025 brought in three changes that affect every survey flight:

  • UK class marks. New drones carry a mark from UK0 to UK6 that tells you which subcategory they can fly in. Any drone without a UK or European class mark is now a “legacy” aircraft, and the rules for it depend on its weight.
  • Registration from 100 g. You need a Flyer ID (a free online test, valid for five years) to fly anything of 100 g or more. The business needs an Operator ID (£12.34 a year) for anything of 250 g or more, or of 100 g or more with a camera — which covers every survey drone.
  • Remote ID. UK1, UK2, UK3, UK5 and UK6 drones must broadcast Remote ID now. From 1 January 2028 it extends to legacy and UK0 drones of 100 g or more with a camera.

The A3 trap on residential streets

This is the one that catches surveyors out. In the A3 subcategory you must stay 50 m from uninvolved people and 150 m from residential, commercial, industrial or recreational areas — and the CAA counts villages, towns and cities as residential areas. If you’re standing in a terraced street, you’re inside that line, so A3 is out.

So an older, legacy drone of 250 g or more can’t legally do a typical street roof under A3. A legacy drone under 2 kg can fly in A2, but must stay 50 m from uninvolved people, which rarely works in a street either. The realistic options are a sub-250 g, UK0 or UK1 drone flying in A1, a UK2 drone in A2 with low-speed mode, or an operational authorisation in the Specific category.

That’s why I fly a sub-250 g aircraft in the Open category for residential roofs: it’s the class the rules allow closest to people.

When you need the Specific category

The standard route for survey work beyond the Open category is the PDRA01 operational authorisation from the CAA. It lets you fly within 150 m of built-up areas, costs £524 a year and lasts 12 months. It doesn’t remove the 50 m distance from uninvolved people (except during take-off and landing), and you’ll need an operations manual and a remote pilot competence certificate. The GVC is issued until the end of 2027; after that, the new remote pilot certificates replace it.

Before you fly

  • Airspace. Protected aerodromes have Flight Restriction Zones: the aerodrome traffic zone plus runway protection zones 1 km wide that run 5 km out from each runway end, up to 2,000 ft. Inside one, you need permission from air traffic control. The CAA says the primary source for restrictions is the UK Aeronautical Information Publication and the NATS restriction zones, and warns that apps can be out of date.
  • Prisons. In England and Wales there are no-fly zones of around 400 m around closed prisons.
  • Landowner permission to take off and land. If the drone comes down on someone else’s land, you need their permission to retrieve it.
  • Insurance. Third-party liability insurance is required for any flight that isn’t purely recreational — the CAA’s own Drone Code gives surveys as an example.
  • Privacy. The ICO’s guidance on drones asks you to assess whether a drone is the right tool, avoid recording people and property you don’t need, tell people, and keep images for the shortest time necessary. Its own example is a surveyor who keeps the camera on the roof.
  • Weather. Cold batteries drain faster, and manufacturers advise pre-heating below about 5°C. Gusts around parapets and chimneys are where small drones get into trouble.

Getting images you can report from

The number that matters is the ground sample distance: how much surface one pixel covers. For a common 20-megapixel, 1-inch camera it rises in a straight line with distance:

120 m3.29 cm180 m wide

For defects you want to be at the top of that table — a few metres off the roof, not up at mapping height. Take a wide shot before every close-up so the reader knows where they’re looking, and don’t rely on straight-down images: the faces of chimney stacks, abutments and eaves only show in oblique shots.

What a drone can and can’t tell you

A drone roof survey is a visual inspection, not a structural assessment, and the report should say so. Where it earns its keep is in telling you where to look next — a plant growing out of a hopper head means water has been overflowing onto the wall below, so you check the brickwork and the room inside for damp.

Thermal imaging from the air

Thermal surveys of buildings follow BS EN ISO 6781-1:2023, which replaced BS EN 13187. Conditions matter more than the camera: commonly cited guidance is a temperature difference of at least 10 K between inside and outside, wind below about 5 m/s, no sun on the building, and dry surfaces. Resolution matters too — at 20 m, a 640×512 sensor can reliably measure something about 6 cm across, while a 160×120 sensor needs about 41 cm. And bare metal and glazing reflect their surroundings, so lead flashings and windows can look hot or cold for reasons that have nothing to do with heat loss.

My own drone is a compact camera aircraft, not a thermal rig, so I don’t offer aerial thermography — but if you’re commissioning one, these are the questions to ask.
