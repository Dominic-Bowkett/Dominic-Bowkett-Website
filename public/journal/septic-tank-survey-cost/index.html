Septic tank survey
cost in 2026

A proper septic tank survey — someone opening the chambers and checking compliance, not a look from the garden — typically costs £250–£300 for a basic inspection in 2026, or £350–£600 for a full written report, rising past £700 with CCTV work or emptying. It's one of the few checks a standard building survey genuinely doesn't cover, on exactly the property where it matters most.

Published 17 Sep 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A proper septic tank survey — someone opening the chambers, checking the tank's actual condition and confirming the discharge is legal, rather than a glance from outside — typically costs £250–£300 for a basic visual inspection with verbal feedback in 2026, rising to £350–£600 for a full written report with photographs. Add CCTV work on the pipework, tracing a hidden drainage field, or emptying the tank to inspect it properly, and the total moves past £700.

The number isn't really the point here, though. What matters is that a septic tank is one of the very few things on an off-mains rural property that a standard homebuyer or building survey doesn't meaningfully cover — on exactly the kind of house, older and out past the mains sewer, where something wrong with it is both plausible and expensive to put right.

I'm a building surveyor, not a drainage or wastewater specialist, so I don't survey septic tanks myself — that's a job for a specialist contractor with genuine small sewage treatment system experience, not a general drainage firm or a plumber. What follows is 2026 pricing checked against current UK specialists' published rates, what a proper survey actually covers, and why it's worth commissioning separately on any of the off-mains cottages and farmhouses scattered through my own Kent, Sussex and Surrey patch.

The average most people find quoted sits around £250–£300, which buys the basic visit. If you actually want something you can hand to a solicitor or use to negotiate — condition, photographs, a stated view on compliance — budget for the higher end of that range instead.

What drives the price

Verbal versus written. A site visit and a spoken opinion costs less than a proper written report with photographs and findings you can actually act on, or show to someone else.

Whether the tank needs emptying to inspect it properly. A genuine look at the internal condition sometimes means emptying first, typically around £150, usually charged separately from the survey fee itself.

Access and system age. A modern plastic tank with a visible, unobstructed lid is a quick job. An old brick or concrete tank buried under decades of undisturbed garden, with covers that have to be located and dug out, takes longer and costs more.

CCTV and tracing. Camera work through the pipework, or tracing a drainage field that's never been mapped, is what pushes the better-equipped surveys past £700.

Travel and rurality. The properties that need this survey are, by definition, off the mains network and often some distance from the nearest specialist firm, so a call-out charge is more common here than on most other trade quotes in this series.

Why your building survey doesn't cover this

A RICS Level 2 HomeBuyer Report or Level 3 Building Survey inspects drainage visually, at whatever's reachable from an accessible manhole cover — the same limitation I've covered in drainage survey cost for mains-connected houses. On an off-mains property, that limitation matters more: a septic tank or small sewage treatment plant is a sealed system, usually buried, and a surveyor standing over a lifted manhole simply can't tell you whether it's the right size for the household it serves, whether it's discharging legally, or how close it is to failing.

The seller's Property Information Form (TA6) is supposed to fill that gap. It asks specifically whether the property has a septic tank or treatment plant, when it was last emptied, and for a description of the system and how it's maintained. In practice, plenty of sellers answer those questions vaguely or leave them blank, particularly on a system installed by whoever owned the house before them and never properly explained.

Then there's the compliance angle, which is where this genuinely differs from most other “do I need this” questions in this series. General Binding Rules, in force since 2015, set the standard a septic tank or small sewage treatment plant has to meet without an individual environmental permit — including a hard rule that a plain septic tank can no longer discharge untreated straight into a watercourse, a ditch, stream or river. That needs a small sewage treatment plant with secondary treatment instead, or the effluent rerouted to a drainage field in the ground, which is unaffected by the rule unless it sits close to a watercourse. Anyone with an existing system discharging to a watercourse had until 1 January 2020 to sort it. That deadline has been and gone. A tank still discharging untreated into a ditch today isn't a problem due soon — it's already non-compliant, and once you own the property, putting it right becomes your problem, not the seller's.

What it actually checks — and the tells worth spotting yourself

A proper survey opens the tank's chambers, not just the outer lid, checks the physical condition of the tank itself, confirms where the discharge actually goes, assesses whether the drainage field is doing its job, and checks the system against General Binding Rules requirements — output, distance from buildings and boundaries, distance from any watercourse.

Some of what it's looking for you can spot yourself before paying for anything. A drainage field that's permanently waterlogged, has an unusually green or lush patch of grass over it even in dry weather, or smells of sewage, usually means it's failing or overloaded. Gurgling drains or slow-draining fixtures throughout the house, especially after rain, point the same way. None of that tells you exactly what's wrong or what it'll cost to fix — that's what the survey is for — but it's a reasonable steer on whether to commission one at all, or to press the seller harder before you do.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Ask specifically whether it's a verbal opinion or a full written report — the two get confused on the phone more than almost any other trade in this series.
  • Check whether emptying is included or charged as an extra if the tank needs it to inspect properly.
  • Ask whether the surveyor assesses General Binding Rules compliance specifically, not just physical condition — a tank can be structurally sound and still discharging illegally.
  • Get the seller's TA6 answers and any maintenance records in writing beforehand, and ask the surveyor to check them against what they actually find.
  • Use a firm with septic and small sewage treatment plant experience specifically, not a general drainage contractor whose day job is unblocking mains-connected drains.

Questions I get asked

How much does a septic tank survey cost in 2026? A basic visual inspection with verbal feedback typically costs £250–£300. A full written report with photographs runs £350–£600. Add CCTV, tracing or emptying and the total moves past £700; emptying alone, where it's needed, is roughly £150, usually charged separately.

Does my building survey or HomeBuyer Report cover the septic tank? Not meaningfully. A RICS Level 2 or 3 survey inspects drainage visually at accessible points only, and a buried, sealed septic tank or treatment plant isn't something a general practice surveyor opens up, empties or assesses for compliance. Book a specialist septic tank survey separately if the property's off mains.

What if the septic tank discharges into a ditch or stream? Under the General Binding Rules, that's no longer allowed without secondary treatment — a plain septic tank needs upgrading to a small sewage treatment plant, or rerouting to a drainage field instead. The compliance deadline was 1 January 2020, so an untreated watercourse discharge today is already non-compliant, and once you own the property, it's your problem to fix.

What does it cost to replace a septic tank or drainage field if the survey finds a problem? It depends what's wrong. A straightforward tank replacement typically costs £4,000–£10,000. Upgrading to a small sewage treatment plant, the usual fix for a non-compliant watercourse discharge, runs closer to £6,000–£12,000. A drainage field on its own is usually £2,000–£5,000, and a full, more complex replacement can reach £15,000. Finding this out before you exchange makes it a negotiating point rather than a bill you inherit.
