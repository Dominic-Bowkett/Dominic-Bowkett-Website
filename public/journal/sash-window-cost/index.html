Sash window
cost in
2026

Draught-proofing a sash window typically costs £200–£400, a full restoration £400–£1,200, and a new timber sash £1,000–£2,500 in 2026. Repair usually wins; here is when it doesn’t.

Published 8 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Your sash windows rattle, stick, let the wind through, or the sill has gone soft, and somebody has told you the answer is new windows. In 2026 the honest range is wide: £200–£400 to draught-proof an existing sash, £400–£1,200 to restore one fully, and £1,000–£2,500 to supply and fit a new timber sash window, with specialist or conservation-spec joinery going above that.

It depends on what is wrong with the window you have, and whether the building’s planning status limits what you may replace it with. Most sash windows I see are repairable. Most quotes I see for replacing them are priced on the assumption that they are not.

I’m a building surveyor, not a joiner or a window installer. I don’t restore or fit windows, so I have no stake in which route you choose. What follows is the 2026 pricing and my view of when repair is the sensible spend.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 8 October 2026 against Trade2Base’s sash window repair and restoration guides (published May–June 2026) and MyJobQuote’s trader-quoted prices (August 2026), with Everest’s sales-led figures as the high outlier. The sources agree well on repair work and disagree widely on replacement, which tells you something in itself. Kent, Sussex and Surrey quotes tend to sit toward the top of each band.

A worked example: a Victorian terrace in Sussex with eight original sashes in fair condition would typically cost £4,000–£10,000 to restore throughout, against £8,000–£20,000 to replace every window with new timber sash. The second figure is my arithmetic on the per-window range, not a quoted price, and both assume a fairly standard property.

MyJobQuote’s trader responses show how far apart quotes can land: £250–£350 for basic work, £1,000–£1,200 and up for double glazing, £1,500–£2,000 for full refurbishment with double glazing. Everest quotes £3,000–£5,000 for a wooden sash, well above the other sources and more likely a premium installer package than a typical price. Treat it as a ceiling.

What drives the price

Condition of the timber. This decides everything. A sound sash with tired cords is a small job. One with a rotten sill and cracked bars is not. Replacing a rotten sill typically adds £150–£350 per window.

How much of the window you keep. Draught-proofing and cord repair work with the existing frame. Replacement means removing the box, making good the reveal and often redecorating internally.

Glazing specification. Slim double-glazed units in a timber sash cost more than single glazing with secondary glazing behind it. A broken single pane is a minor cost: £80–£200 each.

Size and number. Larger sashes cost more, and the more you order the cheaper each window becomes. Whole-house restoration of 8–12 windows typically runs £4,000–£12,000.

Listed or not. Specialists working on listed buildings may charge 15–25% above standard rates for the documentation and conservation requirements.

Access. Upper floors on a terrace may need scaffold or a tower, which isn’t usually in the window price. See my scaffolding cost guide.

Should you restore or replace?

This is where a surveyor’s eye earns more than the numbers. A sash window isn’t worn out because it rattles. Rattling usually means worn cords, dried-out putty or gaps round the frame, all cheap to fix. Replacement is often sold on “draughty, can’t open it, can’t clean it”, and all three are repair problems.

The performance case is better than most people expect. English Heritage tested 1880s sashes in 2009 and found that draught-proofing a sash in good condition cut air leakage by as much as 86%. Adding good secondary glazing brought the U-value to 1.7 W/m²K, and 1.6 with closed shutters, inside the 2.0 W/m²K target in the Building Regulations. Their conclusion was that original sashes can match modern double glazing with the right upgrades. I’d read that as what a well-done restoration can achieve, not a promise for every window.

Replace when: the frame is rotten beyond repair rather than just the sill; the windows are later, poorly made replacements that don’t suit the building; or the joinery has been so altered that restoring it costs more than a good new sash. If the quote says every window must go and nobody has probed the timber, ask why.

A caution from my side of the fence: draught-proofing makes a house tighter, and a tighter house holds more moisture. If you already see condensation or mould on cold surfaces, sort out ventilation at the same time as the seals, or you move the problem rather than fixing it. My damp survey cost guide covers how a diagnosis separates condensation from real damp, and rising damp treatment cost explains why the two get confused. Also check that any open-flued appliance in the room still gets the air it needs.

Planning, listed buildings and the rules

Whether you can replace at all depends on the building. In an ordinary house, replacing windows is generally permitted development. In a conservation area covered by an Article 4 direction that right is withdrawn and you need planning permission, and a switch from timber to uPVC is the change councils most often resist. Heritage-style uPVC with authentic joints and sash horns is sometimes approved, but it is the conservation officer’s call, not a given.

A listed building will almost always need listed building consent to replace windows, and repair is the default expectation. My listed building consent cost guide covers the process. Unauthorised works to a listed building are a criminal offence, so ask first.

Separately, replacement windows are notifiable under the Building Regulations. The usual route is an installer registered with a competent person scheme such as FENSA, who self-certifies. The standard for replacement windows in an existing dwelling is a U-value of about 1.4 W/m²K, with a relaxed figure where the appearance of a listed or conservation-area building can’t change. Ask for the certificate: you’ll need it when you sell. The glazing gain on an energy rating is smaller than most people hope; see my double glazing cost guide and EPC cost guide.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • Has anyone looked at the timber? A blanket “replace everything” quote with no condition check is a sales quote.
  • Repair and replace priced separately. Ask for both on the same windows. If restoration isn’t offered at all, find someone who does it.
  • What the price includes. Glazing, painting inside and out, making good plaster, disposal of the old frames, and the FENSA certificate.
  • Frame material and joints. Timber species, how the joints are made, and whether the sash profile matches the original. Mock sashes with fake horns don’t.
  • Planning status. The installer should ask whether the house is in a conservation area or listed. If they don’t, you carry the risk.
  • “Today only” discounts. Window sales carry some of the biggest gaps between the first price and the real one. Get at least three written quotes.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to restore a sash window? In 2026 a full sash window restoration typically costs £400–£1,200 per window, depending on how much timber needs replacing. Draught-proofing alone is £200–£400, and replacing sash cords £150–£300. A whole house of 8–12 windows typically costs £4,000–£12,000.

Is it cheaper to repair or replace sash windows? Repair is usually cheaper. Restoring a sound timber sash typically costs £400–£1,200, against £1,000–£2,500 to supply and fit a new timber sash. Replacement makes sense when the frame is rotten beyond repair or the existing windows are later, unsympathetic replacements.

Do I need planning permission to replace sash windows? Often not for an ordinary house, but you do in a conservation area covered by an Article 4 direction, and a listed building will almost always need listed building consent. Check with your local planning authority before ordering anything.

Can sash windows be as warm as double glazing? Close. English Heritage testing in 2009 found draught-proofing cut air leakage through a sound sash by as much as 86%, and good secondary glazing brought the U-value to about 1.7 W/m²K, inside the 2.0 target. A draught-proofed, well-fitted sash can approach double glazing without losing the original window.
