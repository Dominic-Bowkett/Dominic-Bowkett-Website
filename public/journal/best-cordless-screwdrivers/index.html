A survey is a long list of small fixings, not big ones. The meter cupboard door that has to come off to read a serial number and go back on square. The loft hatch batten that was screwed rather than nailed. An inspection cover held by four cross-head screws that have not turned in a decade. A bath panel that needs releasing to check for a leak behind it. None of these is a job for a full-size drill-driver, and none of them is fast with a hand screwdriver either — not when there are six or eight of them on a single visit and a full day of properties still to get through.

That gap is what a compact cordless screwdriver fills. It is smaller and quieter than pulling an 18V drill out of a case for two screws, it fits a jacket pocket rather than a tool bag, and it does the job in the time a hand driver takes to find the right bit. It is also a genuinely different category of tool to the SDS drills and combi drivers covered elsewhere on this site — those are for making holes in masonry; this is for taking screws in and out of thin ply, UPVC and softwood, repeatedly, without stripping the head or cracking the panel.

The range on Amazon runs from pocket units under £25 to 7.2V drill-driver hybrids well over £100, and most survey work sits well inside the cheaper end of that range — the expensive units earn their keep only if drilling a pilot hole is a regular part of the job, not an occasional one. How I judge a cordless screwdriver

One-handed control, because the other hand is usually holding something. A hatch cover, a torch, a bath panel that wants to fall on you — on a survey the screwdriver is very rarely the only thing in your hands. Weight, balance and a start method that does not need a second hand to brace the tool all matter more here than they would on a bench.

Torque control, not raw power. Meter cupboard doors, inspection covers and bath panels are thin ply, UPVC or softwood, and the screws holding them are small self-tappers or machine screws, not coach bolts into a joist. What matters is a clutch or torque setting fine enough to seat a screw and stop, not a motor strong enough to strip the head or crack the panel if you are not paying attention.

A light source on or near the tool. Meter cupboards, under-stairs voids and loft hatches are almost never lit, and finding the screw slot by torchlight held in your teeth is exactly the kind of small friction a built-in LED removes.

Charging that does not depend on the van. A micro-USB or USB-C top-up from a power bank between properties beats a proprietary mains dock when the working day is spent away from a socket. It is a bigger factor in day-to-day usefulness than the headline battery capacity.

Bit compatibility for the odd oddball fixing. The great majority of survey-relevant screws are PZ2 or PH2, but security or captive screws turn up occasionally on service hatches and consumer unit covers — a 1/4″ hex drive that accepts standard third-party bit sets is worth more than a large but proprietary bit set that cannot be topped up.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Torque headline numbers are mostly irrelevant for this job. These tools get compared on Nm figures as if they were fixing joists, but nothing in a meter cupboard, an inspection cover or a bath panel needs serious torque — it needs control. A stepped clutch that stops cleanly at the right setting beats a bigger maximum-torque figure you will never use and would strip a screw head if you did.

Battery capacity marketing matters less than charge method. “Up to 190 screws” or a headline mAh figure sounds precise, but in practice a compact screwdriver only gets short bursts of use across a working day, and it is very rarely flat. What matters more day to day is whether it tops up from a micro-USB cable and a power bank between properties, or needs a proper mains charge back at the van — the Draper here is the one exception in this list on that point.

A 50-piece bit set is mostly padding. The genuinely useful bits on a UK survey are PZ2 and PH2, with a flat driver and occasionally a Torx or hex bit for the odd fixing. A large bit set is not a downside, but it is not the deciding factor either — a smaller set with the right handful of bits does the same job as a case of fifty you will use four of.

A drill mode is worth paying for only if you are actually drilling. The Makita in this comparison earns its higher price by genuinely doubling as a drill for pilot holes; if that is not a regular part of the job, it is extra bulk and cost for a capability that sits unused, and a dedicated compact screwdriver does the actual job faster.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a drill or just a screwdriver for survey work?

A screwdriver, for almost everything covered in this guide. Meter cupboard doors, inspection covers, loft hatch battens and bath panels are held by screws going into material that is already pre-drilled or soft enough not to need it — a compact cordless screwdriver does the job faster and with less bulk than a drill-driver. The exception is if you are regularly making your own pilot holes, in which case a hybrid like the Makita DF012DSE, or a separate SDS drill for masonry, covers that specific need better than a screwdriver ever will.

What torque do I need for a meter cupboard or access panel?

Less than the spec sheets suggest. These are small self-tapping or machine screws into thin ply, UPVC or softwood, and 4–6Nm with a stepped clutch is comfortably enough — several of the tools in this comparison sit in exactly that range. What you actually want is the ability to stop at a low setting for the delicate fixings, not a higher maximum figure, since the risk on this kind of work is stripping the head or cracking a UPVC panel, not running out of power.

Will a cordless screwdriver handle security or captive screws?

Usually yes, provided the tool takes standard 1/4″ hex bits and you carry a security bit set separately — the bit sets bundled with most of these tools cover the common drive types (Pozidriv, Phillips, flat, sometimes Torx) but rarely tamper-resistant variants. A cheap third-party security bit set fills that gap for the odd service hatch or consumer unit cover that uses one, without needing a different tool.

Micro-USB charging or a mains charger — does it matter?

For a tool that spends the working day away from a socket, yes. Micro-USB or USB-C charging means a top-up from the same power bank you carry for a phone or a work light, which matters more in practice than the headline battery capacity. A mains-only charger with a UK plug, like the Draper here, is not a dealbreaker for a budget tool used a few times a week, but it means planning a proper charge back at base rather than topping up mid-round.

A cordless screwdriver gets a meter cupboard door or an inspection cover back together in seconds; opening one up safely and reading what is behind it is what a building survey or an EPC visit is actually for.
