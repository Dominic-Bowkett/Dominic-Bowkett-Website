The best
disposable coveralls
for dusty work

A loft top-up throws loose mineral wool fibres over everything within reach, a suspended timber floor lifted for inspection lets out a century of settled dust in one go, and a strip-out on an old terrace coats clothes in plaster and paint dust that doesn’t brush off in the van. A disposable coverall over normal clothes is the cheap, unglamorous answer — and the Type 5/6 rating on the good ones is the same standard HSE points to for the dusty, non-licensed end of work where a suspect material might turn up. Here’s what the rating actually means, what doesn’t, and seven worth keeping folded in the van.

A disposable coverall keeps loft insulation, old plaster and decades of settled grime off skin and clothes rather than out of the air the way a respirator mask does. The two are a pair, not alternatives — a mask filters what gets breathed in, a coverall stops the same dust working down a collar over a long loft or crawl-space visit. None of this is exotic: loose-fill insulation during an EPC top-up, a suspended floor lifted to check for rot, or a Victorian strip-out throwing plaster and paint dust into an unventilated room are a normal Tuesday, and a folded paper boilersuit generally isn’t rated for any of it.

Type 5/6 matters here for a specific reason. HSE’s own Asbestos Essentials task sheets, written for the non-licensed end of work, are explicit that a disposable Type 5/6 coverall with a hood is essential PPE where dust might be disturbed and a suspect material could be present. That’s a long way from saying this kit is asbestos-removal equipment — licensed asbestos work uses fully taped, sealed suits, higher-grade respiratory protection and a decontamination unit on site, and nothing here is any of that. A Type 5/6 coverall is for the ordinary survey scenario: stop the moment something looks suspect, get it assessed, and keep dust off clothes in the meantime, the way HSE recommends for any non-licensed dusty task.

How I judge a disposable coverall

Type rating that actually matches the job. Type 5 (BS EN ISO 13982-1) covers solid airborne particulates — dust, fibres, loft insulation. Type 6 (BS EN 13034) covers light liquid splash. Almost every trade coverall now carries both together, the right minimum for dry dust work; Type 3 or 4, with taped seams, only earns its keep where there’s standing water or genuine chemical contamination too.

Fabric technology, not just the brand on the front. Tyvek’s flash-spun HDPE sheet and the SMS/SMMS nonwovens used by Portwest, 3M and Ansell both hit Type 5/6, but behave differently — Tyvek generally holds up better against tearing on rough timber and brick, SMS breathes better on a hot loft.

Seam construction. Stitched seams (visible thread) are adequate for dry dust and cover most of this list. Sewn-and-taped seams are what buys the jump to a liquid-tight Type 3/4 rating — worth knowing so a taped suit isn’t bought on spec when a stitched one would do the job for less.

A hood that seals against a respirator, not just the head. An elasticated hood opening does the basic job; a two or three-panel hood that closes nearer the chin seals better against an FFP3 mask edge, which matters more for keeping dust off skin than the headline Type rating.

Cost per suit at the quantity actually bought. A single Amazon suit and the same suit bought by the case from a trade supplier can be three or four times apart in price — check both before assuming the single-unit price is the real cost of stocking up.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Type 5/6 is dust and light-splash protection, not asbestos-removal equipment. HSE’s Asbestos Essentials guidance names Type 5/6 coveralls as sensible PPE for the non-licensed end of dusty work — the ordinary survey scenario, where the response to a suspect material is to stop and get it properly assessed. Licensed asbestos removal is a different job entirely, using fully taped and sealed suits, higher-grade RPE and an on-site decontamination unit. Nothing here is rated or intended for that.

Type 5/6 marking and a CE Declaration of Conformity are the non-negotiable check. Cheap “disposable overalls” in the same search results are sometimes just a basic polypropylene boilersuit for mess protection, with no Type 5/6 marking or EN standard mentioned anywhere — a different product category, whatever the price implies.

Fabric technology decides comfort and toughness more than the brand. Tyvek’s HDPE sheet and SMS/SMMS fabrics both meet Type 5/6, but don’t behave the same — Tyvek generally holds up better against rough surfaces, SMS breathes better in the heat.

Reuse within a shift is allowed, and worth doing. HSE’s Approved Code of Practice for CAR 2012 confirms a coverall can be reused across a shift if undamaged and taken off without contaminating the wearer — a genuine saving over binning one after every loft hatch.

What marketing sometimes pushes that doesn’t matter: terms like “hazmat suit” or vague “asbestos stripping” claims on a basic stitched-seam listing. A Type 5/6 coverall meets the standard HSE names for routine dusty work; it isn’t evidence it’s rated for licensed removal.

Questions I get asked

Is it safe to wear one of these if I think I’ve found asbestos?

The suit itself is fine to wear, but the right response to a suspected asbestos-containing material is to stop disturbing it and leave the area, not carry on working in a Type 5/6 coverall. These suits are dust and light-splash protection for routine, non-licensed work where fibre release is expected to be low — not the sealed, taped system used for licensed asbestos removal. If a material’s suspect, the next step is a specialist assessment under the Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012, not a tighter suit from this list.

What’s the actual difference between Type 5 and Type 6?

Type 5 (BS EN ISO 13982-1) is tested against fine airborne solid particulates — dust, fibres, loose insulation — by pumping talc-like powder into a chamber with the suit inside and checking what gets through. Type 6 (BS EN 13034) is a light liquid-splash test, low-pressure spray rather than a jet or submersion. Nearly every trade coverall now carries both together, so the real choice isn’t Type 5 versus Type 6 — it’s Type 5/6 versus the higher Type 3/4 ratings that need taped seams for genuine liquid-tight protection.

Can a disposable coverall be reused, or does it get binned after one job?

It can be reused within the same shift if undamaged and taken off without contaminating whoever’s wearing it — HSE’s own guidance for CAR 2012 says as much explicitly. Across different jobs, or once it’s torn, snagged or visibly dirty, a fresh suit is the sensible choice.

Do I need taped seams for loft and crawl-space work?

No. Dry dust from loft insulation, old timber and masonry only needs a Type 5/6 suit with ordinary stitched seams — six of the seven suits above use exactly that. Taped seams and the higher Type 3/4 rating earn their place where there’s also standing water, an active leak or genuine chemical contamination, not for dry dust alone.

A coverall that meets a stated standard is a small, cheap check against a bigger problem — the same diligence that matters across a full building survey or a loft inspection ahead of a retrofit assessment.
