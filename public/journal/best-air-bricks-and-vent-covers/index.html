The best
air bricks
and why a blocked one rots the floor

Half the timber-floor defects I write up on a suspended ground floor trace back to the same cause: an air brick that got painted over, pointed up in a re-render, buried under a raised patio, or simply never cleared after the last lot of landscaping. Sub-floor ventilation is one of the cheapest things to get right and one of the most expensive things to have ignored — a joist that's sat in still, damp air for a few years is a very different repair bill to a five-minute vent swap. Here's what actually needs venting, what a telescopic vent and a periscope vent each solve, and seven worth buying.

An air brick is the grille set into an external wall that lets outside air reach the void under a suspended timber ground floor — the gap between the earth and the underside of the joists. That void needs to stay dry and moving, because timber sitting over still, humid air is exactly the condition wet rot and dry rot need to get started. The air brick isn't decorative and it isn't optional: it's the only thing keeping that void ventilated once the floor's been laid, and on most pre-1980s houses it's the entire strategy for keeping the joists alive.

What actually goes wrong is rarely the air brick itself failing. It's almost always something done to the wall around it, years after the house was built: a driveway or patio raised to meet a new door threshold and left covering half the brick; cavity wall insulation blown in without a vent sleeve fitted through it, silting the airway shut; a repaint or render job that skims straight over the grille because nobody thought to mask it; flower beds banked up against the wall. None of these are dramatic events. They're all small, well-intentioned bits of work that happen to switch off the ventilation without anyone noticing for years, and by the time it shows up as a bouncy floor or a musty smell it's a joist-replacement job, not a vent-swap job.

Where I do find rot or persistent damp at floor level and the sub-floor void turns out to be the cause, the honest next step is usually a proper look with a borescope through the floor before anything gets lifted — a borescope tells you how far the damage has spread without taking the floor up speculatively. If what's driving the problem is rising damp in the wall itself rather than a blocked void, that's a different job entirely; rising damp treatment costs covers what that actually involves and when it's genuinely needed rather than oversold. How I judge an air brick or vent cover

Equivalent free area against what the void actually needs, not just a like-for-like swap with whatever was there. Approved Document C's long-standing guidance, carried through NHBC technical standards and every major manufacturer's own literature, asks for at least 1,500mm² of ventilation per metre run of external wall or 500mm² per m² of floor area — whichever is greater — on at least two opposite external walls.

Telescopic adjustment range, so the vent reaches from the outer wall face to the actual sub-floor void without needing the brickwork re-cut. Cavity depths and brick courses vary more between houses than most people expect.

A built-in vermin grille, or a separate cover fitted over the top. An open airbrick is an open invitation to mice; a cover that clogs with debris just moves the blockage problem rather than solving it.

Material and finish matching the wall — clay-effect terracotta and buff read as brick, black and white plastic read as a retrofit, and on a period property or anywhere the front elevation is visible that distinction matters more than it should.

Whether the job needs an accessory, not just a bigger vent. A raised path or a deep cavity isn't fixed by a longer straight sleeve on its own — see the section below on periscope vents and when a plain telescopic vent genuinely isn't the answer.

Telescopic vents, periscope vents, and when a straight-through vent isn't enough

Every product above except the G962 accessory is a straight-through vent: air travels in a more or less direct line from the outer wall face to the sub-floor void, telescopic or not. That works for most houses. It doesn't work where the ground outside has been raised above the original airbrick line, which is common on older terraces where a road, pavement or patio has crept upward over decades of resurfacing — Georgian and Victorian properties fronting a public highway are particularly prone to it. Forcing a straight vent through in that situation either sits it below finished ground level, where surface water runs straight into the void, or leaves it uselessly high above where the void actually is.

That's the job a genuine periscope vent is built for: a Z-shaped or stepped duct that takes air in at a usable height above the raised ground and routes it down and across to the void, rather than running in a straight line. Rytons and Cast Iron Air Bricks both make dedicated periscope units for exactly this scenario, specified through builders' merchants and installers rather than sold as a fixed-price retail SKU I could verify in stock on Amazon at the time of writing — which is why there's no periscope vent with a buy button in this guide. The G962 remote void sleeve above is the closest thing to a periscope fix available as a straightforward online purchase: it won't clear a full raised-ground scenario on its own, but it handles the more common case of a path or slab that's crept over one side of the opening.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A blocked air brick is rarely deliberate. Nobody sets out to seal a sub-floor void. It happens one render job, one raised patio and one flower bed at a time, which is exactly why it's worth a five-minute check from outside before assuming the floor itself is at fault.

Painting over the grille is worse than it looks. A coat of masonry paint that bridges the slots can cut the free area to almost nothing while leaving the airbrick looking untouched from a distance — it's one of the quicker checks on a survey precisely because it's so easy to miss from the pavement.

More air bricks isn't automatically the fix if the total area is already there. The guidance figure is a total equivalent area across at least two opposite walls, not a rule that every wall needs its own vent regardless of size.

Cavity wall insulation and air bricks need to be specified together. A cavity that's had insulation blown in without a vent sleeve fitted through it can silt the airway shut from the inside, invisibly, which is one of the less obvious things worth asking an installer about before work starts.

On a listed building or in a conservation area, swapping a clay airbrick for plastic is a visible change. It's a small alteration but exactly the kind a conservation officer can have a view on for a front elevation. Check before assuming a like-for-like plastic swap is uncontentious.

Questions I get asked

Can I just block up an air brick I don't like the look of? No — permanently sealing one reduces the total ventilated area below what the void needs, and on an older suspended timber floor that's a direct route to wet or dry rot. If it's the appearance rather than the airflow that bothers you, a colour-matched cover or a smaller, better-placed vent is the fix, not sealing it.

What do I do if the outside ground level has been raised above my air bricks? That's exactly the situation a periscope vent is designed for — see the section above. Don't force a standard telescopic vent through at the wrong height; get a periscope unit specified through a merchant or installer instead.

Do I need Building Regulations approval to add or move an air brick? Straightforward like-for-like replacement generally doesn't need sign-off. Adding new sub-floor ventilation, or altering it as part of other works, falls under Approved Document C and is worth raising with your local authority or a competent-persons-scheme installer rather than guessing.

How do I know if my sub-floor void has enough ventilation? Count the airbricks, estimate their equivalent area from the manufacturer's figures, and check it against 1,500mm² per metre run of wall (or 500mm² per m² of floor area, whichever is greater) across at least two opposite walls. If that doesn't add up, or you can't tell because some vents are hidden behind planting or paving, that's a reasonable thing to flag on a survey.
