Most moving-house lists stop at the removal van. That is the easy half. The half people regret is the first night and the first week: a bed that will not go back together, a torch at the bottom of an unmarked box, and a house that nobody has looked at properly since the survey. I spend my working days in other people’s lofts and crawl spaces, and the same handful of items turns up in every sensible move.

This guide is grouped by job rather than ranked as one category: the packing, the sealing and labelling, the furniture, the first night, and then a short first-week section of cheap checks that a surveyor would run on a house they had just taken on. None of it is expensive, and most of it earns its place again after the move.

If you are still at the stage of choosing a house rather than boxing one up, the house viewing kit is the earlier guide. And if the survey has not happened yet, it should come before any of this: see what a house survey costs and the HomeBuyer survey.

The one I’d buy first

Really Useful 64 Litre storage box

If I could only buy one thing for a move, it would be a handful of lidded crates, because they are the only item here that is still useful in a year. They stack in the van, shrug off a damp garage and become loft storage afterwards, whereas cardboard is a one-trip product. Buy a few for the heavy and fragile things, and use cheap cardboard for everything else.

How I judge a moving kit

Does it survive the stairs? A move is mostly carrying. Anything that fails under load, whether a box bottom, a thin tape or a handle, costs more than it saved. Weight is the real limit: pack heavy items small and light items large.

Does it work after the move? Crates, label makers, tool kits and torches earn their money again. Packing tape and cardboard do not, so buy those cheap.

Is it findable on the night? The first-night items go on the van last and come off first, in a box marked as such. A kit packed at the bottom of a pile is a kit that does not exist.

Does it protect the house? Floors, door frames and stair walls take the damage in a move. Sliders, corner protection and a clear route cost little against a gouged hallway.

Boxes and packing

01

Really Useful 64 Litre storage box

Best overall — reusable, stackable and unbothered by a damp garage

Cardboard is cheap until it spends a week in a garage or a hallway with a wet coat nearby. A lidded 64-litre crate is the opposite: it stacks, it carries with two hands, and it comes out the other end as long-term storage for the loft. The manufacturer’s specification gives a 64 litre capacity with clip-on handles and a lid that locks, and owner feedback is consistent that the lids stay put and the boxes nest when empty. Fill one with books and it becomes a back injury, so split heavy items between boxes.

For

Against

Sealing and labelling

03

Scotch Heavy Duty packaging tape

Best for sealing — a proper heavy tape rather than the cheapest roll

Box bottoms fail before anything else, and the usual cause is thin tape rather than a thin box. A heavy-duty polypropylene tape, run along the seam with a second strip across each end, holds a loaded box that a cheap roll lets go of on the stairs. Buy a multipack, because moves use more tape than anyone expects, and a hand dispenser is worth the extra few pounds for your wrists.

Best for labelling — legible labels that survive being stacked

Labelling is the job that decides how the first week feels. Writing the room on the top of a box is no use once boxes are stacked, so label two sides, and name the destination room, not the contents. A small handheld label maker prints a clear label that does not smudge, and the same machine labels the fuse board, the stopcock and the consumer unit circuits afterwards, which is the useful part. Moving the furniture

05

X-Protector furniture sliders

Best for heavy furniture — protects the floor you have just bought

Dragged furniture is how a good oak floor becomes a scratched one, and on a suspended timber floor it is how you start a conversation about the joists below. Sliders let a wardrobe or a sofa move across a floor with little effort and no scoring. Use the plastic type on carpet and the felt type on hard flooring, and never push a loaded unit across a timber floor with a known soft spot. For stairs and flats a sack truck is the better tool; see the sack trucks guide.

The surveyor’s first week

08

Stopcock key (telescopic)

Best cheap first-week check — know where the water turns off before you need to

The classic first-week emergency is a burst pipe and no idea where the stopcock is. Find it, turn it off and on, and note what happens, because a stopcock that has not moved in ten years often will not move at all, and it is better to find that out on a calm Saturday. A telescopic key reaches a stopcock sitting in a deep box or under a sink. This is a generic category search, as models are interchangeable; check the key length on the live listing. If it will not turn, or weeps from the spindle when you try, that is a plumber’s job rather than a force job.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Labels matter more than boxes. Good boxes badly labelled make a worse move than mediocre boxes labelled by room. Name the room it is going to, on two sides, and put the one box you need first in plain sight.

Take meter readings on the day. Note the gas, electric and water meter readings when you get the keys, ideally with a photo, because the first bill is the one that is argued about.

Skip the moving-specific gadgets. Branded wardrobe boxes, vacuum bags for everything and multi-piece “moving kits” are mostly padding. A roll of tape, a marker and some blankets do most of it.

Do not skip the safety check. The one item I would not leave to day ten is the CO alarm if anything burns fuel, along with knowing where the stopcock and the fuse board are. Everything else can wait.

This is not a survey. A socket tester and a hygrometer show symptoms. If something odd turns up in the first week, such as a damp patch that appears after rain or a crack that was not obvious before, it is a question for a surveyor, not a gadget. A building survey is the route for a house you already own.

Questions I get asked

What should I pack last when moving house? The first-night box: a kettle and mugs, bedding, a torch, a basic tool kit, phone chargers, toilet roll and medication. It goes on the van last so it comes off first, and it is labelled so nobody puts it in the wrong room.

What should I check in the first week of a new house? The stopcock and the fuse board, so you know where to turn things off; the sockets, with a cheap tester; the smoke and CO alarms; and the meter readings. Then watch for damp or condensation through the first few weeks, particularly in bedrooms and bathrooms.

How many boxes will I need to move a house? A rough rule is ten to fifteen boxes per room for a family home, fewer for a flat, and more if you own a lot of books. Order a little over, as spare boxes are easier to recycle than a last-minute run to the shop.

Is it worth buying boxes or borrowing them? Borrowed or second-hand boxes are fine if they are double-wall and dry. New ones are worth it for heavy items. The cost is minor next to a van and a day off work, so do not let it be the weak link.

A move is the one moment you have every cupboard open and every room empty, which is the best chance you will get to look at the house properly.
