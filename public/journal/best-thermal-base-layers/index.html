A survey is not a steady-temperature job. You start in a warm kitchen, climb into a loft that is a few degrees above the street, lie on a suspended timber floor void, and finish on a roof edge or in a back garden with the wind coming across a field. Your body goes from sweating to shivering several times in a morning, and the clothing that copes with that is not the outer jacket. It is the layer next to the skin.

The base layer has two jobs: stay warm when it is slightly damp, and dry out before you cool down. Cotton does neither, which is why a cotton T-shirt is the worst thing to wear into a cold loft. The choice is between merino wool, which is naturally warm when damp, and synthetic fibres, which dry faster and cost less. Neither is wrong, and the right one depends on how you work.

How I judge a thermal base layer

Fibre: merino, synthetic or a blend. Merino stays warm when damp and resists odour, so it suits long days and repeat wearing. Synthetics such as polyester and polypropylene dry faster, wear harder and cost much less. Blends try to get a bit of each.

Weight. Brands label weights by grams per square metre, often 120, 150, 200 and so on. A 120 is a light layer for mild days or people who run warm. A 200 is a proper winter layer. Weights are not standardised across brands, so compare the number within a brand rather than across them.

Fit. A base layer works by sitting close to the skin. Too loose and it does not wick, too tight and it restricts movement on a ladder. Look for flat seams, because a seam across the shoulder under a tool bag strap or a harness will rub by lunchtime.

Durability and washing. Knees, elbows and cuffs take the wear in a crawl space. Merino is more delicate and often needs a cooler wash. Synthetics tolerate being thrown in with the work trousers.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Merino versus synthetic is a trade, not a contest. If you wear the same top three days running between jobs, merino wins on smell. If you wash after every job and sweat heavily on ladders, synthetic wins on drying and price. Plenty of people end up with one of each.

Ignore the temperature claims. Warmth ratings and “heat-retention technology” labels are marketing, not measurement, and there is no common test behind them. Look at the fibre and the weight.

Do not buy cotton, and do not buy the fashion version. A “thermal” top in a cotton blend is warm when dry and cold when it is not. Fashion-brand thermals are fine for a cold commute, but they are not built for a ladder.

Layer the rest properly. A base layer cannot make up for trousers that are too thin or boots that let the cold in. The work trousers guide covers the legs, and the safety boots guide and wellington boots guide cover the feet. For wet gardens, the base layer sits under a waterproof shell and not instead of one.

Dust and insulation are a laundry problem. A day in a loft puts fibres and dust into everything you are wearing, and merino in particular can hold them. A base layer you can wash at 40°C is worth more than one you have to nurse.

Questions I get asked

Is merino better than synthetic for a base layer? It depends on the job. Merino is warmer when damp and stays fresher over a day or two. Synthetic dries faster, lasts longer and is far cheaper. For surveys, where you sweat and then cool down, I would lean merino for the top and not worry about the bottoms.

What weight base layer do I need for winter? A midweight, around 200 in merino terms, suits most winter days in a cold house or garden. A lighter 120–150 suits October and March, or anyone who overheats. Treat the numbers as a guide within a brand.

Can I wear a base layer under a shirt on site? Yes, and it is the sensible way to do it. A thin crew neck under a work shirt, with a fleece or softshell over the top, lets you strip down in the warm kitchen and add back in the loft.

How often should I wash a merino base layer? Less often than a synthetic one. Merino resists odour, so airing it between wears is normally enough, with a cool wash when it needs it. Follow the care label, because hot washes and tumble dryers are what shorten its life.
