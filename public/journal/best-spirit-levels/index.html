A spirit level sounds like a builder’s tool, and mostly it is. But on a survey it earns its place as a measuring instrument. When a client says a door has started catching, or a marble rolls across the lounge, or the extension “feels like it’s going downhill”, the level is what turns a feeling into a figure. One door frame 10mm out of plumb is a note in a report. Every frame on the same side of a Victorian terrace leaning the same way is a chapter — and possibly a conversation about movement.

The same instrument checks whether a kitchen worktop was set level or the units are heading for the corner, whether a damp-proof course line actually holds its 150mm above ground the whole way round, whether a bathroom floor has any fall towards its drain, and which way a suspended timber floor is dipping when a sleeper wall has settled. None of that needs laser kit. It needs a straight, rigid length of aluminium with a vial you can trust.

And trust is the whole game. The difference between a £12 level and a £90 one is not that one shows a bubble and the other doesn’t — it’s whether the bubble is telling the truth, whether it still will be after a year in the van, and whether you can prove it in thirty seconds. That last part matters most, and it’s where this guide starts and ends.

How I judge a spirit level

A published accuracy figure, in mm/m. Decent levels state their vial accuracy as millimetres of error per metre of length — 0.5mm/m is the professional standard, and Stabila, Milwaukee and the better trade brands all publish it. Across a 1.2m level that’s 0.6mm of permissible error: less than the thickness of a pound coin, and far tighter than the construction tolerances of any house I survey. A level that doesn’t publish an accuracy figure at all is telling you something. And treat “0.029°” on a spec sheet as the same claim in different clothes — it’s 0.5mm/m converted to degrees.

Length — more than you think. A level reads the two points its ends happen to sit on, so a short level on a lumpy surface reports the lump, not the structure. For anything structural — door frames, walls, lintels — 600mm is the sensible minimum. For floors, worktops and DPC lines you want 1200mm, and 1800mm if falls across whole rooms are the question. A torpedo level has its uses in a survey bag (pipe runs, gully falls, appliance feet), but it is not the tool for judging a building.

It must pass the flip test. Any level can be checked for calibration in thirty seconds without any reference surface: set it down, note precisely where the bubble sits against the lines, then turn it end-for-end onto exactly the same spot. A true level shows the identical reading both ways; any difference is twice the vial’s error. A level that fails is not a level — it’s a straightedge with delusions.

Readability in bad light. Survey work happens in unlit hallways, cellars and lofts, reading vials at skirting level and above head height. Vial magnification, high-contrast ring markings and a bubble you can read from above rather than straight-on all sound like brochure fluff until you’re squinting at a worktop in a north-facing kitchen in December.

Rigidity — because a level is also a straightedge. Half the diagnostic value of a 1200mm level is the gap under it. Bridge a floor and the daylight beneath tells you hump or dip, and roughly how much once a tape is against it. That only works if the level itself is straight and stays straight, which is why box-section profiles beat I-beams and why the reinforced-spine designs exist. A level that’s spent a year diagonal under a van shelf needs the flip test more often.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The flip test is the only spec you can verify yourself. Every claim on the packaging reduces to one question you can answer in the shop: does it read the same both ways round? Do it before you buy, again after any serious knock, and to the old level in the shed before trusting it with anything that matters. Vials drift and adhesives age, and a level that has quietly gone out of true is worse than no level — it hands you confident wrong answers.

Digital earns its keep when the number must be written down. For a builder, level is a yes/no question and a bubble answers it. For a surveyor, the magnitude is the finding. “The ground floor falls approximately 20mm per metre towards the rear left corner” is a sentence that supports a conclusion about settlement; “the floor is a bit off” is not. A digital level gives you that figure directly, in mm/m or percent, and the display photographs well as file evidence. If your work never requires the number, save the money and buy the better vial.

Falls are smaller than you think. A wet-room floor laid to a 1-in-80 fall — a common design figure — slopes at 1.25%, or about 0.7°. You cannot judge that by eye, and a standard vial only barely declares it. This is exactly the territory where a digital readout stops an argument between a client and their tiler, in either direction.

What the display stand pushes that you can skip. Vial counts, mostly — a third or fourth vial at 45° is decoration for house diagnostics. Rubber “comfort” grips, painted-on angle scales, and combined laser-and-vial gadgets that do neither job well. Magnets are genuinely useful if you regularly meet steel and an irrelevance if you don’t — and they collect every screw and filing in the van. Spend the marginal pound on the vial, the box section and the brand’s calibration reputation, in that order. One honest caveat the other way: a digital level with a user-zero function can be zeroed badly as easily as well, so learn its calibration routine and check it against the flip test rather than trusting the button.

Questions I get asked

How accurate is a spirit level? A professional-grade level is rated at 0.5mm/m — over a 1.2m level the bubble is permitted to be wrong by 0.6mm, far tighter than any tolerance that matters in an existing house. Budget levels either meet the same figure (the Stabila 70-2 does), quote a looser one, or say nothing at all — which usually answers the question. In practice the spec matters less than the condition: an expensive level that has been dropped can read worse than a cheap one that hasn’t, which is why the flip test beats the brochure.

How do I check if my spirit level is accurate? The flip test, and it costs nothing: put the level on a firm surface that’s close to level, note exactly where the bubble sits against the graduation lines, then rotate the level 180° end-for-end and set it back on the same spot. The two readings should be identical; if they differ, the vial is out by half the difference. Check the plumb vial the same way against a door frame, flipping face-for-face. Digital levels usually include a two-position calibration routine that automates the same idea — run it on a solid surface, not a wobbly table.

What length spirit level do I need? One length rarely covers everything, but if forced to choose I’d take 1200mm: long enough to make honest judgements about floors, worktops and DPC lines, short enough to travel. Go no shorter than 600mm for anything structural — door frames, walls, lintels — because a short level reads local bumps rather than the element. For whole-floor falls, 1800mm or a 1200mm level on a straightedge is better still. Torpedo levels (around 230mm) are for pipework, appliance feet and gully falls, not buildings.

Are digital spirit levels worth it? If you only ever need to know whether something is level, no — a good vial answers that faster and never needs batteries. They earn their price when you need the actual value: recording a floor’s fall in a survey report, setting or checking a designed gradient (a 1-in-80 shower fall, a ramp, a drainage run), or photographing evidence of a lean. The professional option is the Stabila TECH 196 DL; the Huepar covers the same jobs at a budget, with more diligence required on calibration. Either way the vial levels stay in the bag — digital supplements a good level, it doesn’t replace one.
