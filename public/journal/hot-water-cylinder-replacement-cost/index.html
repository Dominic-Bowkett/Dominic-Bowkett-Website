Hot water cylinder
replacement cost
in 2026

A new hot water cylinder typically costs £500–£1,400 fitted for a vented one and £1,500–£3,500 for an unvented one. Before you pay either, check whether you only need a £150 element.

Published 15 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Replacing a hot water cylinder typically costs £500–£1,400 fitted for a like-for-like vented cylinder, and £1,500–£3,500 for an unvented, mains-pressure one. Those are 2026 figures, and Kent, Sussex and Surrey sit toward the top of each band.

But the first question is whether the cylinder needs replacing at all. A failed element or thermostat is far more common than a failed tank, and costs a fraction. And the second question, vented or unvented, is often answered by what your house is like, not by what an installer would rather fit.

I am a building surveyor and energy assessor. I do not install cylinders, so I have no stake in which one you buy. This is what the 2026 figures say and what I would settle before you accept a quote.

The short answer

Prices were checked on 15 October 2026. BookaBuilder, Trade2Base (published 14 June 2026) and BestBuilders (updated 8 September 2026) all publish 2026 guides, and their bands overlap well on the main jobs. They differ most on the unvented low end, where some quotes assume a small cylinder swapped like for like.

A worked example: a three-bed semi with four people, moving from a loft tank and a copper cylinder to a 210-litre unvented one. Trade2Base prices the cylinder at £650–£950 and a first-time install at £700–£1,400 labour, with pressure reducing valve, expansion vessel and discharge pipework on top. That lands inside £1,500–£3,500, and in the South East I would plan on the upper half. BestBuilders and BookaBuilder both put the regional premium at roughly 10–25%.

Do you actually need a new cylinder?

Check the cheap faults first. If the water is lukewarm or runs out quickly, but the cylinder is not leaking and is not corroded, the usual suspect is the immersion heater element or its thermostat. Replacing an element is typically £150–£280 fitted, with the part itself costing little. A thermostat alone is cheaper again.

A cylinder that is weeping from the shell or a seam, or is corroded around the fittings, is the case for replacement. A leak at a joint or valve can be repaired. If you are unsure, ask the plumber to show you where the water is coming from rather than accepting “it’s past it”.

If the cylinder works but you are heating it all day on an immersion, a timer costs far less than a new cylinder (see my immersion heater timer guide).

Vented or unvented?

A vented cylinder is fed from a cold tank in the loft, so the pressure is whatever gravity gives you. An unvented one is fed straight from the mains, which gives much better flow to showers and lets you run more than one outlet. It also removes the loft tank and its freezing risk.

It is not an upgrade for its own sake. Unvented systems cost more to buy and fit, need discharge pipework to a safe place, and need to be serviced every year. One cost guide puts the annual service at £80–£150, though that is a single source and I would ask any installer what theirs charges. Your own pipework also has to cope with the higher pressure; BookaBuilder lists it as one of the main reasons an unvented job grows in cost.

So my advice is simple. If the pressure and flow you have are fine, and you do not have a second bathroom, a like-for-like vented replacement is the cheapest answer and is perfectly respectable. If you are short of loft space, or have two or more bathrooms and a weak shower, unvented is worth the extra.

What drives the price

Size. BestBuilders sizes unvented cylinders by household: 120–150 litres for one or two people (£900–£1,600 installed), 180–210 litres for three or four (£1,200–£2,200) and 250–300 litres for five or more (£1,700–£3,000). Trade2Base supplies show the same pattern, from £500–£750 for 150 litres to £850–£1,200 and over for 250 litres and above.

Like for like, or a conversion. Trade2Base puts a like-for-like unvented swap at £400–£700 of labour, about a day, against £700–£1,400 and a day and a half to two days for a first-time install.

Pipework and extras. BookaBuilder gives £200–£1,000 or more for rerouting pipes, £150–£400 for electrical upgrades and up to £500 for disposal of the old unit. Awkward loft access and a cylinder in a first-floor cupboard push labour up.

Emergency or planned. Booking a replacement before the old one fails avoids the weekend call-out premium and gives you time to get three quotes.

The paperwork: G3 and notification

An unvented cylinder is a pressure vessel, which is why it is regulated. Part G3 of the Building Regulations requires it to be installed by someone competent to do so, which in practice means an installer holding a recognised unvented qualification, and the work has to be notified to Building Control. Trade2Base describes a qualified installer as able to self-certify through a competent person scheme, which avoids a separate Building Control inspection fee. One training provider quotes Gas Safe notification at £3.34 plus VAT for 2026/27, against Building Control fees that can run to hundreds of pounds.

The same provider lists replacing a vented cylinder over 15 litres as usually notifiable too. I would not rely on one source for that, so ask your installer, and ask for the certificate. It matters when you sell: a buyer’s solicitor will ask for evidence on an unvented system, and a missing certificate is the sort of thing I note in a survey. My building control fees guide explains what happens when paperwork is missing.

If a heat pump is coming

A heat pump heats water more slowly and at a lower temperature than a boiler, so it needs a cylinder with a much larger coil. One cylinder guide puts a heat pump coil at roughly 3.0–4.5m² against 0.8–1.2m² on a standard boiler cylinder. A heat-pump-ready cylinder is typically £1,100–£2,200.

If a heat pump is a realistic plan in the next few years, do not buy a boiler-era cylinder now and replace it twice. My air source heat pump cost guide covers the main job, and the new boiler cost guide compares the alternative honestly.

Insulation and the EPC

When I assess a home for an EPC I record whether the cylinder is insulated and how thickly, because it affects the rating. A modern cylinder comes with factory foam insulation; an old one often has none, or a thin jacket. The Energy Saving Trust’s guidance is that a jacket on an older cylinder should be at least 80mm thick. If you are keeping the old cylinder, a proper jacket and insulation on the first metre or so of the pipework are among the cheapest things you can do. My EPC cost guide explains what the assessment covers.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • A diagnosis. A quote for a new cylinder should say what is wrong with the old one. If it is the element or thermostat, ask for that price.
  • Vented or unvented, and why. If the installer proposes unvented, ask what problem it solves in your house.
  • Size and make stated. The litres, the model and whether it is a heat-pump-ready cylinder, written on the quote.
  • G3 evidence. The installer’s unvented qualification and a commitment to notify the work and give you the certificate.
  • Everything itemised. Cylinder, safety kit, pipework, electrics, disposal and the annual service, so you can compare three quotes like for like.
  • Written quotes. At least three, and check the installer’s registration (Gas Safe for gas-fired systems).

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to replace a hot water cylinder? Typically £500–£1,400 fitted for a like-for-like vented cylinder and £1,500–£3,500 for an unvented one, including the safety kit and notification. A failed immersion element alone is usually £150–£280.

Is an unvented cylinder worth the extra cost? Only if you need mains-pressure hot water, for example for more than one bathroom, or have no loft space for a tank. It costs more to fit, must be installed by a G3-qualified engineer and is serviced annually. If your vented system works and the pressure is fine, there is no need to change.

How long does it take? A like-for-like swap is about a day. Converting from vented to unvented is one and a half to two days, because pipework, discharge routes and the safety components all need altering. Expect the hot water to be off for that time.

Do I need a new cylinder for a heat pump? Usually, yes. A heat pump needs a much larger coil than a boiler-era cylinder, and a heat-pump-ready cylinder is typically £1,100–£2,200. If a heat pump is on the cards, choose the cylinder with that in mind rather than replacing it twice.
