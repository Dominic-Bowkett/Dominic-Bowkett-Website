A pocket tape measures a room. A laser measure measures a room faster. Neither is much use when the job is the rear garden, the full width of a terrace elevation or the run of a boundary wall, and that is when a long open-reel tape comes out of the van. Plot sizes, extension footprints, how far the drain run is from the back door: half the outdoor numbers in a survey notebook come off one of these.

It is a quiet category, and the search volume is small next to ladders or torches, but the wrong tape is an expensive mistake of the quiet kind. A stretched blade or a worn hook adds centimetres to a plan, and those centimetres turn up later as a wrong area on an EPC or a mismatch against the drawings. The pocket tape guide covers the everyday 5m to 8m tape; this one is for the reel that goes in the back of the van.

If your outdoor measuring is mostly paths and drives, a measuring wheel is faster and a laser measure is better indoors. The long tape earns its place on plots, set-out lines and anything where you want a number you can check by eye on the ground.

How I judge a long tape measure

Fibreglass or steel. Fibreglass is the default: it does not rust, it shrugs off wet grass and, if you kink it, it usually survives. It stretches a touch under pull, so keep the tension light and consistent. Steel does not stretch, which is why it is the choice for set-out and precise work, but it rusts, kinks and conducts electricity, so it is the wrong thing to carry into a meter cupboard or near an overhead supply.

Accuracy class. Tapes sold in the UK carry an accuracy class, usually Class II or Class III, which sets how many millimetres of error are allowed over a given length. Over 30m the difference is a few millimetres, not centimetres, but a tape with no class printed on it is telling you something. For rough plot sizes it hardly matters. For a drawn plan it does.

The hook and the first 100mm. The end hook is the weak point of every tape. A bent or loose hook shifts every reading by the same amount, and on a long tape that error is easy to miss. Check the hook against a known length when it arrives and again after a drop. On an open-reel tape the zero is often at the hook face or the ring; know which.

Frame and winder. Open-reel frames are light and shed mud, but the blade is exposed and picks up grit. Closed cases are cleaner but heavier and cannot be hosed off. A folding handle and a smooth winder matter more than they sound after the fifth wind-up of the day.

Legibility. Large, high-contrast numbers every metre and clear markings every 10cm save a lot of squinting in poor light. This matters more than any spec on a rainy afternoon at the bottom of a garden.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A second person beats a better tape. A long tape is a two-person tool, and the cheapest accuracy upgrade is someone to hold the zero. Working alone, hook the end over a gatepost, a brick corner or a peg, and keep the blade off the ground: a tape lying on grass dips and reads long.

Sag and slope. Over 20m an unsupported tape sags in the middle and reads long. Keep it taut and level where you can, and on a sloping plot measure in level steps rather than along the ground. A plan shows horizontal distance, and a tape pulled up a slope does not.

Don’t pay for the extras. Magnetic hooks, backlit blades and Bluetooth readouts are sold hard and add little to a tape you will use outdoors a few times a week. A readable blade, a solid hook and a frame that survives a drop are the whole specification.

When a long tape is the wrong answer. Indoors, a laser measure is quicker and one person can use it. For a long run of path, drive or road frontage, a measuring wheel is faster. For anything that needs real accuracy over distance, such as a legal boundary or a set-out for a structure, the answer is a topographical survey, not a better tape.

Questions I get asked

What is the best tape measure for measuring a garden? A 30m fibreglass open-reel tape. It is long enough for most gardens, light enough to carry and unbothered by wet grass. If your gardens are rural or large, a 50m tape saves joining two readings. Hold the end on a fixed point and keep the blade off the ground.

Fibreglass or steel for a long tape? Fibreglass for most site work, steel when the dimension must be exact and conditions are dry and clean. Fibreglass stretches a little under pull and steel does not, but steel conducts electricity and rusts. If you work near live equipment, fibreglass is the safer default, though a wet blade is never a good idea near a supply.

How accurate is a 30m tape measure? Accuracy depends on the class printed on the tape. A Class II tape allows an error of a few millimetres over 30m under standard conditions of tension and temperature. In practice your technique, the hook and the sag contribute more error than the blade. For rough plot sizes it is more than good enough; for drawings, take care.

Can I use a laser measure instead? Indoors, yes, and it is quicker. Outdoors in bright sun many laser measures struggle to find their dot at distance, and they need a target to bounce off. A laser measure with a target plate helps, but a tape on a plot is still the most reliable way to a number you can check on the ground. The laser measure guide goes into the range figures.
