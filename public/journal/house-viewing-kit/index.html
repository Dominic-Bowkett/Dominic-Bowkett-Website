The house
viewing kit

Most second viewings are spent falling further for a house rather than checking it — the light in the kitchen, where the sofa would go. None of what follows fixes that, but a handful of cheap tools in a coat pocket narrows the gap between “I liked it” and “I know roughly what I’m dealing with,” before an offer goes in. Eight things I’d take, one job each, and what each one actually finds.

A first viewing is for deciding whether to go back. A second viewing is the one worth taking a few minutes' longer over, once the estate agent's patter and the staged photos have worn off enough to actually look at the building rather than the styling. None of what's below is a substitute for a proper survey — it's what narrows down what to ask the agent, what to photograph for later, and occasionally what to walk away from before paying for one.

Everything here fits a coat pocket or a small bag. Nothing needs charging overnight, pairing to a phone, or a manual to operate — a viewing is not the moment to be fiddling with an app while an agent waits by the front door. Most of it earns its place doing a second job well beyond this one house, which is the honest reason to actually buy rather than borrow it.

How I’d prioritise a viewing kit

A reading is a flag, not a diagnosis. A high number on a damp meter means “ask why,” not “damp proofing needed” — it can’t tell you if it’s rising damp, a leaking gutter or condensation from a shower with no extractor fan. Working out which is what a survey is for.

Discretion beats capability. A lab-grade moisture meter or a full-size pair of binoculars looks odd pulled out in front of an agent and the sellers. Everything here fits a coat pocket, takes seconds to use, and doesn’t need explaining.

One reading rarely proves anything alone. A single damp reading, one room’s dimensions, one compass bearing — treat each as a data point worth mentioning to a surveyor later, not evidence to negotiate on by itself.

None of this replaces a survey. A second viewing with a damp meter and a torch might tell you a house is worth pursuing, or worth walking away from — it won’t tell you about the roof structure, the foundations or what’s behind a wall. That’s what a HomeBuyer Survey is for, once there’s an offer worth protecting.

Damp and staining

01

Brennenstuhl Moisture Detector MD

Best overall — the one I’d take first; my top pick above

Fresh paint in one corner of a tired-looking room, a slightly musty smell under a bay window, a patch of wallpaper that’s a shade too new — a damp meter turns a hunch into a reading in the time it takes to press two pins against the surface. It won’t say what’s causing a high reading, only that one’s there, which is enough to make a mental note or a photo before moving on. More detail on pin versus pinless meters, and the professional-grade alternatives, is in the damp meters guide.

Which way does the garden face

06

Suunto A-10 NH Compass

Best for orientation — does the garden get the sun the listing photos implied

Listing photos are shot for the best light of the day, not the light a garden gets most afternoons. A bearing off the back of the house takes under a minute and settles whether “south-facing garden” in the particulars is accurate, or whether a conservatory actually faces north and will be gloomy most of the year. A basic orienteering compass with a rotating bezel and printed scale — the same tool used for map reading, rather than a phone app that a steel front door or a fridge freezer can throw off by tens of degrees.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A number is only useful with context. A damp meter reading of “high” against a skirting board with no other clue means little on its own — note where, and mention it to a surveyor rather than treating it as a verdict.

Don’t let a good reading talk you out of a survey. A dry damp meter reading and a square compass bearing are reassuring, not conclusive — they check a handful of spots, not the roof structure, the foundations or what’s inside a wall.

Cheap and simple beats capable and slow. Everything here is chosen for the ten minutes of a viewing, not professional daily use — a pin-type meter over a lab-grade pinless one, a basic compass over a phone app fighting a steel door.

Ask before you test. Pressing a damp meter’s pins into paintwork, however small the mark, is worth a quick “do you mind if I check this wall” first. Most sellers and agents don’t mind — it’s a courtesy, not an assumption.

Questions I get asked

Do I need all eight of these for a first viewing? No — save this kit for a second viewing on a house being seriously considered, once first impressions have worn off enough to actually check things rather than fall for the light in the kitchen.

Will a damp reading at a viewing tell me if I need a survey? No — treat any reading, high or low, as one data point for a surveyor rather than a verdict either way. Even a dry reading in one spot says nothing about damp elsewhere in the building.

Can I just use my phone instead of half of this? A phone covers photos reasonably well, but its compass reads erratically near steel doors and appliances, and a laser measure or damp meter has no phone equivalent worth trusting.

What should actually make me walk away at a viewing? Nothing here should decide that alone — a high damp reading, a leaning chimney through binoculars or a failed socket test are reasons to ask harder questions and book a proper survey, not reasons to rule a house out on the spot.
