Radon is a radioactive gas produced when the trace uranium in rock and soil decays. It seeps up through the ground, finds its way into buildings through floors, gaps and service penetrations, and accumulates — particularly in the well-sealed, under-ventilated rooms we spend most time in. The UK Health Security Agency puts it behind more than a thousand lung cancer deaths a year, which makes it the second-biggest cause after smoking. It has no smell, no taste and no colour, so the only way to know whether your home has a problem is to measure it.

And this is not just a Cornwall issue. The UKHSA publishes a radon map at ukradon.org showing the designated Affected Areas — places where at least one home in a hundred is expected to exceed the action level — and pockets of the South East sit squarely in them: stretches of the chalk Downs, parts of the Weald, corners of Kent and Sussex that surprise people. I see it surface most often in conveyancing, when an environmental search flags the Affected Area and a buyer rings me in a mild panic. The honest answer is always the same: the map is a probability, not a measurement. Two identical semis can differ wildly; you find out by testing the actual house.

There is a retrofit angle too. Every time we make a house more airtight — new windows, draught-proofing, insulation done properly under PAS 2035 — we reduce the dilution that used to happen by accident, and radon concentrations can drift upwards. A radon number, measured properly, belongs in that conversation.

How I judge a radon detector

The long-term average is the product. Radon levels swing constantly — hour to hour with the weather, and season to season, typically peaking in winter when windows stay shut and the heating drives the stack effect that pulls soil gas in. A single afternoon’s reading is weather, not evidence; UK guidance is built around a three-month average for exactly this reason. So the first thing I look for is a device that measures continuously, averages honestly, and puts the long-term figure front and centre rather than a twitchy live number.

Units and thresholds that match UK guidance. Radon is measured in becquerels per cubic metre — Bq/m³, one becquerel being one radioactive decay per second. The UK action level is 200 Bq/m³ as an annual average, with a target level of 100 Bq/m³ below which you’re doing well. Several detectors sold here are American-market units reading in picocuries per litre; 1 pCi/L is 37 Bq/m³, so a reassuring-sounding “2.7” is actually knocking on the target level. Buy something that speaks Bq/m³, or at least check the settings before you compare numbers to guidance.

Time to a first honest number. Passive detectors take three months by design. Digital monitors vary enormously — some give a usable indication within a day or two, others need a week before the number stops wandering. If you’re checking whether a mitigation fix has worked, or screening a cellar before you commit to a purchase, response time is worth paying for.

Where the number ends up. A figure on a screen suits living with the question. A logged history in an app suits watching seasons and proving a fix. A certificate from a UKHSA-validated laboratory suits conveyancing, landlords and anything with legal weight. They are different jobs, and no single device does all three.

Power and placement discipline. The protocol wants the detector at breathing height in a regularly occupied ground-floor room, away from draughts, windows and radiators, for months at a stretch. A battery-powered unit goes where the measurement should be; a mains-powered one goes where the socket is. That sounds trivial and decides more readings than the sensor does.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Protocol beats hardware. Whichever device you buy, the measurement that means anything is a long one, in the right place: main living room and a bedroom, breathing height, away from windows and radiators, left alone for three months — ideally including winter, when levels peak. UKHSA applies a seasonal correction to three-month results for exactly that reason. A £170 monitor read after a breezy summer afternoon tells you less than a £60 passive pack done properly.

Digital and passive are complements, not rivals. The sensible pattern for a house in an Affected Area is a lab-analysed passive test for the definitive, on-paper answer, and a digital monitor afterwards to live with — watching winter, confirming mitigation keeps working, catching drift after building work changes the airtightness. If you can only buy one: passive for a transaction or a formal record, digital for curiosity and ongoing peace of mind.

High numbers are fixable, and fixing them is ventilation work. A radon sump — a small void formed under the floor slab with a pipe and a quiet fan — actively depressurises the ground beneath the house and routinely cuts high levels by 90 per cent or more. For moderate levels, positive input ventilation (a loft-mounted unit gently pressurising the house with filtered air) often brings the average under the target, and helps condensation into the bargain. Sealing floor penetrations and improving sub-floor ventilation to suspended timber floors support either. What doesn’t work: air purifiers. Radon is a gas; a HEPA filter does not touch it.

What the sales pages push that you can skip. Decimal places, mostly. Consumer radon sensors are counting sparse random decay events, which is why every honest spec sheet quotes accuracy that improves with averaging time — a live number to one decimal place is statistical theatre. The same goes for smart-home integrations that announce your radon level to the kitchen speaker, and for multi-gas dashboards on units whose radon sensing is the afterthought. Spend the money on the sensor and the averaging; if you want the full air-quality picture there are better tools for that job.

Questions I get asked

What is a safe radon level in the UK? There is no wholly safe level — risk scales with exposure — but UK policy draws two lines as annual averages: 200 Bq/m³ is the action level, above which you should take steps to reduce it, and 100 Bq/m³ is the target level to aim for when you do. For context, the UK indoor average is around 20 Bq/m³. A long-term reading in the tens is unremarkable; sustained triple digits deserves attention, not alarm — remediation is routine building work, not an exorcism.

How do I check the radon map for my house? The UKHSA’s interactive map at ukradon.org shows the probability band for any postcode, and a definitive address-level report costs a few pounds. It’s the same dataset a conveyancing environmental search draws on when it flags an Affected Area. Remember what the map is: the chance that a home in that spot exceeds the action level, based on geology and measurements nearby. It cannot tell you about your house — construction, floors and ventilation swing the actual figure enormously — so a flag on a search is a prompt to test, not a defect in itself.

How long does a radon test take? The standard UKHSA measurement is three months with two passive detectors, one in the living room and one in a bedroom, with a seasonal correction applied to estimate the annual average. Digital monitors will show you numbers within hours or days, and those are genuinely useful for spotting patterns — but treat anything under a month as provisional. Radon can vary several-fold between a windy week and a still one, and by a factor of two or more between summer and winter in the same house.

Do I need a radon test when buying a house? If the search flags an Affected Area, the pragmatic route is to ask whether the property has been tested before and whether any sump or fan system exists — then test yourself after completion, because three months rarely fits a conveyancing timetable. Price negotiation on the flag alone is usually disproportionate; a full radon sump installation is modest money against most defects a survey turns up. If the house already has mitigation, check the fan is actually running — they get switched off “because of the noise” more often than anyone admits.
