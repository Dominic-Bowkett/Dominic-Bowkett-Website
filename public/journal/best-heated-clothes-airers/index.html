The best
heated clothes airers
for drying laundry without feeding the condensation

A clothes horse parked in front of a bedroom window, or a wash draped over every radiator in the house with the door shut, is behind a fair share of the condensation calls I get through autumn and winter. A heated airer doesn’t remove that moisture from the house — it just gets it into the air faster and cheaper than leaving it to evaporate off cold radiators overnight. Used well, it’s a genuinely useful, low-cost tool. Used behind a closed door with the window shut, it’s doing exactly what causes the problem it’s meant to help with. Here’s how I’d judge one, and the eight I’d look at.

Indoor drying is often unavoidable in a British winter — nobody’s hanging a wash outside in November, and not every home has a tumble dryer or somewhere to vent one. How it’s done matters more than most households realise. A heated clothes airer puts the same few litres of water held in a wash load into the air as any other method of drying indoors, just gently and steadily from a low-wattage element running through each rail, rather than in one hot blast from a tumble dryer or slowly and passively off cold radiators overnight. Run in a room with somewhere for that moisture to go — an extractor fan on, a trickle vent open, the door onto a ventilated hallway — it’s a genuinely useful, cheap way to dry a load without the electricity draw or the venting a tumble dryer needs.

Run behind a closed bedroom door with the window shut and nothing else moving air, it’s doing exactly what produces streaming windows and black mould in the corner. If condensation is already a whole-house problem — windows misting every morning, damp patches migrating between rooms rather than sitting under one obviously wet load of washing — buying a better airer won’t fix it; that’s a ventilation question, and a PIV unit, a ventilation assessment, or in an especially airtight retrofit MVHR is the more useful next step. For drying a normal wash load in a room that can actually shed the moisture, here are the eight airers I’d look at.

How I judge a heated clothes airer

Wattage set against drying space, not looked at alone. A headline power figure means little without knowing how much rail or bar length it’s heating. Two airers rated within 50W of each other can dry noticeably differently once drying space and arm design are factored in.

A cover that’s actually doing something. A cover traps warm air around the fabric and can meaningfully cut drying time, but only where ventilation is also happening elsewhere in the room — otherwise it just holds the moisture closer to the clothes rather than letting it disperse anywhere useful.

Load capacity matched to an actual wash, not a marketing kg figure. A family wash with bedding and towels needs the 18–24kg end of this list; a single person’s laundry doesn’t need the biggest unit here, and paying for capacity that never gets used is money better spent elsewhere.

A timer or thermostat, not just a plug and a good memory. Leaving several hundred watts of bare heating elements running unattended overnight, or forgetting about it entirely, is the kind of thing that ends badly. A built-in timer is worth paying for on that basis alone.

Storage depth and weight. Folding flat to 8cm behind a door is a different proposition to a unit that’s effectively a permanent fixture in the utility room once it’s up. Check the folded dimensions against where it’s actually going to live.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A heated airer doesn’t remove moisture from the house. It just gets it into the air faster than draping wet washing over a radiator. Running one in a closed bedroom overnight with the door shut and no extractor or window open is exactly the pattern that produces the condensation call the following week.

The running-cost comparison against a tumble dryer is real, but the timeframes aren’t equivalent. At these wattages a full drying cycle typically costs somewhere in the region of 15–40p across 4–8 hours, against upward of £1 for a vented or condenser tumble dryer cycle that finishes in under an hour. It’s genuinely cheaper per load, not cheaper per hour — it dries slower for less.

A cover is worth the extra cost, but it doesn’t turn an airer into a tumble dryer. It speeds drying by trapping warm air around the fabric, not by extracting moisture out of the room. The water evaporating off the clothes still has to go somewhere, cover or no cover.

Wattage isn’t a reliable proxy for drying speed on its own. The number of heated rails or bars and how the arms are angled to give clothes airflow matters as much as the raw power draw. Two airers rated within 50W of each other can dry noticeably differently.

What the marketing pushes that matters less than it sounds. A headline maximum temperature figure (40–55°C on some of these) says little on its own; it’s only useful alongside the airflow design and the room it’s actually drying in. None of it substitutes for drying space and capacity matched to the wash it’s meant to handle.

Questions I get asked

Will a heated clothes airer cause condensation or mould? Used in a room with somewhere for the moisture to go — an extractor fan running, a trickle vent open, the door onto a ventilated hallway — it’s no worse than any other method of indoor drying, and considerably better ventilated than washing draped over radiators behind a closed door. Used in a sealed room, it adds to the same moisture load that causes both.

Is a heated airer actually cheaper to run than a tumble dryer? On a straight cost-per-load basis, yes — typically 15–40p across 4–8 hours at these wattages, against upward of £1 for a vented or condenser tumble dryer cycle. It takes considerably longer to finish a load, so it suits a household that can leave washing drying overnight rather than one that needs it back in under an hour.

Do I need to leave a window open while it’s running? Not necessarily a window specifically, but the room needs somewhere for the moisture to disperse — an extractor fan, a trickle vent, or a door left ajar into a ventilated hallway. A sealed room with the airer running is the wrong setup regardless of which unit it is.

Is a cover worth buying separately if it isn’t included? For a cold room or a larger load, yes — it meaningfully speeds drying by holding warmth around the clothes. For a small load in an already-warm utility room, it’s a nice-to-have rather than essential.

I don’t sell or install clothes airers myself, but if indoor drying is turning into a condensation problem rather than solving one, that’s usually a ventilation question rather than a laundry one — a ventilation assessment shows what’s actually moving air through the house, and a PIV unit or MVHR installation covers the whole-house version of the fix. A working bathroom extractor fan matters just as much as where the washing dries. If the pattern on the wall doesn’t behave like condensation at all, see rising damp treatment costs instead.
