Triple glazing
cost in
2026

Triple glazing typically costs £600–£1,100 per uPVC casement and £5,000–£11,000 for a semi in 2026, 20–40% more than double. For most houses the extra rarely pays back; here is where it does.

Published 9 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

If you are pricing new windows and a salesperson has mentioned triple glazing, the 2026 numbers are these: £600–£1,100 for a standard triple-glazed uPVC casement, £5,000–£11,000 for a typical semi, and a premium of 20–40% over the same window in double glazing. Aluminium and timber frames sit above that.

The number worth pausing on is what the extra buys. Upgrading from good modern double glazing typically saves £30–£60 a year, so on bills alone it will not pay for itself in your ownership of the house. There are cases where it is still the right call, and they are about comfort, exposure and new-build specification rather than money.

I assess and rate buildings for a living, and I do not sell or fit windows, so I have no stake in which way you go. What follows is the pricing, the honest arithmetic, and the cases where I would pay the premium.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 9 October 2026 against Trade2Base’s triple glazing guide (June 2026) and BestBuilders’ cost guide (updated August 2026), with Book a Builder and GFD Homes as cross-checks on the per-window range. Kent, Sussex and Surrey quotes tend to sit toward the top of each band.

The sources agree on the shape and differ on the edges. BestBuilders puts a medium casement at £700–£950 and eight windows at £4,800–£9,500; Trade2Base puts a standard casement at £600–£1,100 and a semi at £6,500–£11,000. GFD Homes quotes a lower £500–£700 per window, which looks like the cheap end of small openings rather than a typical figure. On the premium, Trade2Base and BestBuilders both say 20–40% over equivalent double glazing, and Book a Builder says £100–£300 per window. One large national installer, quoted by TheEcoExperts, puts it at 10–20%, which is the figure I would expect from someone selling the product.

A worked example: a three-bed semi with nine windows already in decent double glazing would pay roughly £900–£2,700 more to specify triple instead. That is my arithmetic on Book a Builder’s per-window range, not a quoted price.

What drives the price

Frame material. uPVC is the baseline. Aluminium typically adds 20–35% and timber 40–70% or more. Timber is often the only material a conservation officer will accept.

Window style. A casement is the cheap reference point. Sash windows, bays and large fixed lights cost more because of the weight, the mechanism and the extra panes.

Glazing specification. Not every triple-glazed unit is equal. Low-E coatings, argon fill and warm-edge spacers decide the real U-value, so ask for the figure rather than the word “triple”.

Frame weight. Three panes of glass are heavier than two. Hinges, sash balances and frame sections need to be rated for it, which is part of why sash windows jump in price.

Access and making good. Upper floors, bays needing a new lintel, and rotten reveals add labour well beyond the glass. See my scaffolding cost guide if the work is above ground-floor height.

Region. Kent, Sussex, Surrey and London run above the national averages quoted here.

Is triple glazing worth it?

Start with the performance. Good modern double glazing delivers a U-value of about 1.2–1.4 W/m²K; triple typically reaches 0.8–1.0 W/m²K. That is a real improvement, but the glass is already a small part of a house’s heat loss once the walls and roof have been dealt with. BestBuilders estimates the saving against good double glazing at around £30–£60 a year. TheEcoExperts, comparing an A-rated double-glazed house with an A++ rated triple-glazed one, gets £195 against £235 a year, a gap of about £40. Two sources, same answer: tens of pounds a year.

Set that against a premium of roughly £900–£2,700 for a typical semi and payback runs to decades. BestBuilders says 20–40 years or more on bills alone. At the most favourable end of both ranges the arithmetic is about 15 years, still beyond the life of many frames and seals. The honest conclusion is that triple glazing is a comfort product, not a financial one.

Where I would still pay for it:

  • Cold or exposed rooms. A north-facing room or a house on an exposed hillside feels the difference. The inner pane stays warmer, which reduces cold radiation off the glass and the condensation that forms on it.
  • Single glazing going in. If you are replacing single glazing and will not touch the windows again for 25 years, the incremental cost of the better unit is smaller than the incremental benefit.
  • Self-builds and extensions aiming at very low heating demand. Triple glazing is standard in Passivhaus-style design because the building is already so well insulated that the glass becomes the weak point.

Where I would not: a house with reasonable double glazing, solid single-skin walls and no loft insulation. Do the loft first. My double glazing cost guide covers the replace-or-repair question for ordinary windows, and the order in which to spend money is something a retrofit assessment settles properly.

Noise, condensation and your EPC

Noise. People assume a third pane means a quieter house. It does not follow. Sound insulation depends on glass thickness, differing pane thicknesses and laminated glass, not the count. BestBuilders says plainly that triple is “not automatically better” for sound, and a good acoustic double-glazed unit can beat a standard triple. If noise is the reason you are buying, ask for the acoustic rating, and consider measuring the problem first.

Condensation. This is the benefit that is real. The inner pane stays warmer, so there is less internal condensation. But streaming glass is also a symptom of too much moisture indoors, and better glass moves the problem rather than curing it. My damp survey cost guide covers telling the two apart.

EPC. The gain from double to triple is small. EPC Advisor puts the extra at only 1–2 SAP points over modern double glazing, and I would treat that as an adviser’s estimate rather than a published figure, because the assessment software uses fairly coarse window categories. If an EPC rating is the reason you are spending the money, loft insulation will do far more for the same outlay. See my EPC cost guide.

Building Regulations, sashes and listed buildings

Replacement windows in an existing dwelling in England must meet a U-value of 1.4 W/m²K or a Window Energy Rating of band B. Any competent triple-glazed unit clears that easily, but so does good double glazing, so the regulation does not push you toward triple. Use a FENSA-registered installer and keep the certificate.

On a period house the question changes. Triple-glazed units are thicker and heavier, and in a sash window the visible sightlines and weight are exactly what conservation officers object to. Sealed triple units in sashes have drawn planning refusals in conservation-area and listed cases, and a listed building will need consent for any change to its windows. Slim vacuum units, which vendors quote at around 1.2–1.3 W/m²K at a thickness close to single glazing, are what heritage installers tend to propose instead. See my sash window cost guide and listed building consent cost guide before ordering anything.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • The U-value or Window Energy Rating stated, not just “triple glazed”. A poor triple unit can underperform a good double.
  • Both options priced on the same windows. Ask for double and triple side by side so you can see the premium, not just the total.
  • A claim of big savings or big EPC gains. The independent figures are tens of pounds a year and a point or two.
  • Frame and hardware rated for the weight. Ask what the hinges and balances are rated for, particularly on large casements and sashes.
  • The planning position. The installer should ask whether the house is listed or in a conservation area. If they do not, you carry the risk.
  • What is included. Making good, disposal, FENSA certificate and an insurance-backed guarantee. Get at least three written quotes, and no signing on the day.

Questions I get asked

How much does triple glazing cost per window? In 2026 a triple-glazed uPVC casement window typically costs £600–£1,100 supplied and fitted. Sash windows are £900–£2,500 and bays £2,000–£4,500. Aluminium adds roughly 20–35% and timber 40–70% or more.

How much more does triple glazing cost than double? Most 2026 sources put the premium at 20–40% over an equivalent double-glazed unit, or roughly £100–£300 per window. One large installer quotes 10–20%. The gap is smaller in a new build, where the frames are specified once, than when replacing windows on an existing house.

Is triple glazing worth it in the UK? Rarely on energy bills alone. Upgrading from good modern double glazing typically saves around £30–£60 a year, so payback runs to decades. It is worth considering for a cold, exposed or north-facing room, a self-build aiming at very low heating demand, or when you are replacing single glazing and want the best available result.

Does triple glazing reduce noise more than double glazing? Not automatically. Sound insulation depends mainly on pane thickness, different glass thicknesses and laminated glass, not on the number of panes. A well-specified acoustic double-glazed unit can outperform a standard triple-glazed one.
