A clamp meter answers a question a multimeter can’t ask safely on a live domestic circuit: is this actually carrying current right now, with nothing broken open and no probe near a live terminal. A storage heater that’s stone cold, an immersion heater that never quite warms the tank, an extractor fan that looks switched on but isn’t moving air — the multimeter guide elsewhere in this series covers checking continuity and resistance once something is already off. A clamp meter answers a different, earlier question: is this circuit actually carrying current right now, with the appliance switched on and nothing disturbed. Clip the jaw around a single conductor — a meter tail, a length of twin and earth, an appliance flex — and read the current flowing through it, without breaking the circuit open or putting a probe anywhere near a live terminal.

That non-contact reading is the whole appeal. A multimeter measures current in-line, which on a domestic circuit means breaking into the wiring and inserting the meter itself into the loop — not something to attempt casually on a live circuit, and often simply impractical mid-survey. A clamp meter reads the same figure by sensing the magnetic field the current generates, so the conductor stays intact and the check takes seconds: is the storage heater actually drawing its rated load, is an immersion element pulling current at all.

It sits alongside the multimeter and socket tester already covered in this series rather than replacing either — a socket tester checks one 13A outlet’s wiring pattern, a multimeter reaches wherever a probe can go, and a clamp meter answers how much current is actually flowing, live, without opening anything up. How I judge a clamp meter

Jaw size that matches what’s actually being clamped. A slim single cable slips into almost any jaw, but a meter tail or a bundle of twin and earth needs real clearance — check the stated jaw opening in mm before assuming a compact meter will fit what’s on site.

True RMS for anything that isn’t a clean sine wave. The same distortion that catches out an averaging multimeter — a variable-speed fan controller, an LED driver, a modern heat pump’s inverter — catches out an averaging clamp meter too. True RMS is cheap enough now on most models that there is little reason to buy without it.

AC and DC current only if the work actually calls for it. Most domestic electrical fault-finding never sees anything but AC, but solar PV strings, EV charger circuits and battery storage systems run DC, and an AC-only clamp meter simply reads zero on them.

A CAT rating printed plainly on the casing, not implied by the price. CAT III 600V is the sensible minimum for fixed wiring and distribution circuits within a building; CAT IV extends that margin to the supply side of the consumer unit and the meter tails themselves.

Auto-ranging, plus a proper resistance and continuity function. A clamp meter that also does voltage, resistance and an audible continuity check earns its place in the bag twice over — useful as a backup multimeter when the dedicated one is elsewhere.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A four-figure current range is rarely the deciding factor. Domestic MCBs run 6–32A and even a single-phase meter tail rarely exceeds 100A, so a meter rated to 1000A buys headroom for a distribution board or three-phase installation, not for the circuit checks most surveys involve — not worth paying extra for on a purely domestic job.

True RMS is the genuine dividing line, not the display resolution. As with a multimeter, a modern LED driver, dimmer or inverter-driven heat pump can make an averaging clamp meter read a current figure that is simply wrong. True RMS is inexpensive enough now that there is little reason to buy a meter without it, budget or premium.

AC-only is fine for most domestic work; DC only matters if the job calls for it. A plain consumer-unit or appliance current check never touches DC, but solar PV strings, battery storage and EV charger circuits do — check which side of that line a job sits on before assuming an AC-only meter, like the Fluke 323 above, covers everything.

A stated CAT rating is not the same as a certified one. A genuine CAT III or CAT IV marking is printed on the casing and stated plainly in the listing; on budget meters it is sometimes closer to a printed claim than an independently tested one, which matters more for anything worked near a consumer unit’s supply side than for a downstream appliance check.

Jaw size is a physical constraint, not a spec-sheet afterthought. A meter with a tight 17–20mm jaw is genuinely more portable, but it will not close around a bundle of twin and earth or a thick meter tail — check the jaw opening against what’s actually going to be clamped before choosing size over capability.

Questions I get asked

What can a clamp meter do that a multimeter can’t?

A clamp meter reads current without breaking the circuit open — clip the jaw around a conductor and it senses the current from the magnetic field around it. A multimeter measures current in-line, which means physically inserting the meter into the circuit, impractical on a live domestic circuit mid-survey. For voltage, continuity and resistance the two overlap, but for a live current reading a clamp meter is the only sensible tool of the two.

Is a clamp meter safe to use to prove a circuit is dead before work starts?

No. As with a multimeter, HSE guidance on safe isolation calls for a dedicated two-pole voltage tester that meets GS38, proved on a known live source, used to test dead, then proved again afterwards. A clamp meter is a diagnostic tool for reading current on a circuit that is already known to be live or already switched off — not a safe-isolation device.

Do I need a clamp meter that measures DC current, or is AC enough?

For the great majority of domestic electrical fault-finding — heaters, fans, immersion elements, general circuit checks — AC current is all that is ever measured, and an AC-only meter like the Fluke 323 covers it completely. DC only becomes relevant once solar PV, battery storage or EV charging is genuinely part of the job, at which point the Martindale or Amprobe picks above take over.

What CAT rating do I actually need for survey work?

CAT III 600V is the sensible baseline for fixed wiring and distribution circuits within a domestic building, covering most of what a surveyor would clamp onto. CAT IV extends that margin to the origin of the installation — the meter tails and supply side of the consumer unit — a genuine extra safety margin rather than a bigger number on the box, though CAT III 600V is adequate for most checks in this guide.

A clamp meter earns its place for the same reason as the rest of the kit in this series — it turns “doesn’t seem to be drawing power” into a number worth writing down. On a retrofit assessment or an EPC, that is often the difference between a vague note and a clear finding an installer can act on.
