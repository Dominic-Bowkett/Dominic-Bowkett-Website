Fitting a loft ladder into an existing hatch typically costs £250–£500 in 2026, and £500–£900 if the hatch has to be enlarged or moved. The ladder is the cheap part. The cost, and the risk, is in what gets cut to make room for it.

Published 4 Nov 2026 Reading time · 8 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

If you already have a loft hatch of a usable size, the loft ladder installation cost in 2026 is typically £250–£500 supplied and fitted, for an ordinary aluminium sliding ladder and two to three hours of a carpenter's time. If the hatch is too small, in the wrong place, or you want a new one, the job becomes £500–£900, because someone now has to cut and trim the ceiling structure as well as hang the ladder.

That second job is the one to be careful with. Most quotes read as though a loft ladder is a product you buy and bolt up. In practice it is a small structural alteration to a ceiling with a ladder attached, and the difference between a good installer and a poor one shows up in how the opening is framed, not in which ladder is on the box.

I don't fit loft ladders — I survey houses, and I see a fair number of loft hatches that have been enlarged by someone with a saw and no plan for the joist they just cut through. So this is a straight read on what it costs in 2026, what moves the price, and the points I'd check on a quote before anyone starts cutting.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 4 November 2026 against current MyJobQuote and Book a Builder cost guides, which agree on the headline ranges to within a few tens of pounds. The South East and London sit toward the upper end of the ranges, as carpentry day rates here run roughly £200–£300 against £140–£200 in the North.

Labour alone is much smaller than the totals suggest. For a ladder going into an existing opening, fitting is roughly £120–£180; the ladder itself is the bulk of the bill, from £150–£200 for a basic aluminium one to £450–£550 for a telescopic. If you are comfortable in a loft and the hatch is already big enough, supplying the ladder yourself and paying only for the fit is the obvious saving.

What drives the price

Whether the hatch changes. This is the whole story. Fitting into an existing, adequate opening is a few hours. Widening or moving it adds roughly £150–£450 for straightforward trimming and making good, and more where it gets involved. A structural engineer, if one is needed, is a further £250–£600.

Ladder type. A sliding aluminium ladder is the cheap, ordinary choice. Concertina and telescopic ladders cost more but fold smaller, suiting a tight hatch or a ceiling height outside the usual range. Standard ladders suit floor-to-ceiling heights up to about 2.8m; above that, you need a longer or telescopic one. Most domestic ladders are rated for 100–150kg, so check the rating against whoever is carrying a box of Christmas decorations up it.

Hatch quality. A basic hatch is a board dropped in a lining. An insulated, draught-sealed hatch costs more and is worth paying for, for reasons covered below.

Light and power. A switched loft light is a separate electrical job, roughly £80–£200 on top, and it has to be done to the wiring regulations. It is worth doing. A ladder with no light means people climb into the dark with a torch in their teeth.

Making good. Plaster and paint around a new opening, and taking away the offcuts, can add £150–£350 if it isn't in the quote. Ask.

The part of the job that matters: what gets cut

Most houses have ceiling joists running one way across the loft, typically at 400–600mm centres. A standard hatch opening sits between two of them, and a ladder fits through it with no structural work at all. That is the cheap job.

An enlarged hatch is different. If the opening has to be wider than the gap between joists, a joist gets cut, and the load it carried has to be picked up by doubled trimmer joists either side, joined with proper hangers. Done properly it is routine carpentry. Done badly — a joist simply sawn through and the hatch lining screwed to the ends — the ceiling sags, the plaster cracks along the line of the cut, and it often goes unnoticed for years until someone stores something heavy up there.

In a house with a modern trussed roof (the W-shaped timber frames used in most houses built since the 1960s), the rule is simpler: you do not cut the truss members. The diagonal webs and the bottom chord are doing structural work. A hatch has to sit between trusses, and where that doesn't give you a big enough opening, the answer is a smaller hatch with a different ladder, or an engineer's design, not a saw. If you can see a quote that simply says "enlarge hatch" with no mention of how the opening will be framed, that is the quote to question.

On the regulations: fitting a ladder to an existing opening in a storage loft generally needs no approval. In England and Wales, cutting a ceiling joist or truss member, or altering the joists to take more load, is where building regulations start to apply, so a competent installer will either deal with building control or tell you why it isn't needed. A ladder is for storage access only. If the loft is to be used as a room, it needs a fixed staircase, which is a different job entirely and sits in the territory of my loft conversion cost guide.

Three things I see missed

The hatch is a hole in your insulation. A thin plywood hatch with no seal sits right in the line of warm, damp air rising from the landing, and it is among the coldest points in the ceiling. Put a ladder on it and it is also the point the hatch gets opened most. An insulated hatch with a compression seal is the cheap fix, and it matters most if you have recently done the loft insulation. Warm moist air leaking into a cold loft condenses on the cold side of the roof, which is how I end up looking at wet roof timbers above a hatch that doesn't seal.

Swing space below. A sliding ladder needs clear floor where it lands, and a hinged hatch needs room to drop open. On a narrow landing both can collide with a door. It sounds trivial and is the most common reason a ladder gets moved a second time. Check where it lands, and where the door swings, before any cutting.

The landing is a means of escape. On a two-storey house the landing is the route out in a fire. A ladder in its stowed position should leave it clear, and a hatch is not a fire door. This does not stop you fitting one, but it is one reason a ladder is for occasional storage access and not a route to a habitable room.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • How the opening will be framed, if the hatch is changing. Look for doubled trimmers and joist hangers, or an engineer's sign-off in a trussed roof.
  • The ladder named, with its length and load rating, not just "loft ladder supplied and fitted".
  • An insulated, sealed hatch, or a clear statement that the existing one stays.
  • Making good and rubbish removal included or priced, so it doesn't appear on the invoice.
  • Whether VAT is in the figure, which the cost guides themselves don't always make clear.
  • Loft light and switch priced separately, by someone competent to do the electrical work.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to fit a loft ladder? About £250–£500 supplied and fitted into an existing hatch in 2026, with MyJobQuote averaging around £350. Add roughly £150–£450 if the hatch needs enlarging, and expect £500–£900 for the whole job where it does.

Can I fit a loft ladder myself? If the hatch is already a suitable size, yes, and it is the cheapest route. The ladder is usually fixed to the hatch frame with a handful of bolts and the instructions are decent. The weight is the problem, so get a second pair of hands. Stop where joists need cutting; that is where a DIY job goes wrong.

Do I need building regulations approval for a loft ladder? Not for a ladder into an existing opening in a storage loft. If you cut a ceiling joist or any roof truss member to enlarge the hatch, building regulations can apply in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own routes. Ask your installer how they'll deal with it.

How big does a loft hatch need to be for a ladder? A common opening is around 520mm by 700mm, though many are smaller. A larger opening is easier and safer for carrying things through. What fits depends on the ladder, so check the manufacturer's minimum opening before you buy rather than after.
