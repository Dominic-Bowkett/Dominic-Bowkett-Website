A great deal of survey work is looking into places that were never designed to be looked into. The meter cupboard under the stairs, the consumer unit behind a coat rack, the boiler in a tall kitchen cupboard, the void under a suspended timber floor, the back of a loft eaves space. They are dark, they are cramped, and you need both hands: one for the moisture meter or the camera, one to hold a door, a board or the torch itself.

That is what a magnetic inspection light is for. It is a slim lamp with magnets in the back, so it sticks to the steel door of a consumer unit, a pipe clip, a joist hanger or the side of a boiler casing, and lights what you are looking at while your hands do something else. A normal torch ends up held in the teeth, which is a poor way to read an RdSAP boiler plate or photograph a stopcock.

This guide is for the slim lamps rather than big floodlights or a head torch. If you want a lamp for a whole room, see the work lights guide; for hands-free lighting on your head, see head torches. The lamps below sit in between: small enough for a jacket pocket, bright enough to be useful, and magnetic.

How I judge a magnetic inspection light

Magnet strength and position. A magnet only helps if it holds on the surface you have. Look for magnets on the back and, better, a second one at the end or on a clip, so the lamp can hang, stand or lie flat. Most consumer unit doors and boiler casings are thin steel, and a weak magnet slides off. Stainless steel, aluminium and plastic will not hold a magnet at all.

Shape. The best inspection lights are flat or slim. A bulky torch will not slide into the gap behind a meter. A wide flood beam lights a cupboard evenly; a narrow beam lights a spot and leaves shadow.

Real output, not the headline figure. Makers print the maximum lumens, which many lights can only hold for seconds before they step down. The useful number is the output on the working setting and how long it lasts. For close inspection 200–400 lumens is plenty, and a flood is more useful than a spot.

Power. A rechargeable lamp is convenient until it is flat in the van. Lamps with USB-C or a replaceable cell are easier to live with than ones with a fixed micro-USB socket. AA cells are heavier but can be swapped from a spare pack.

Safety around electrics. A lamp that sticks to a consumer unit is handy, but the magnet is not a reason to touch anything live. If I am looking inside a consumer unit, I am looking, not testing; testing is for an electrician. Keep the lamp clear of exposed terminals, and do not leave a heavy one perched where it could fall onto them.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The magnet matters more than the lumens. A 1,000 lumen light that will not stay put is less use than a 200 lumen one that clings to the door while you read the label. Check where the magnets are before you look at anything else.

Colour rendering is worth a look. Damp stains, efflorescence and the difference between a brown water mark and a harmless dusty one show up differently under a very blue-white LED. A warmer or adjustable light, like the Ledlenser, makes staining easier to judge. It is a small point, but it affects what goes in a report, and a damp meter reading should always back up what the eye thinks it sees.

Ignore the strobe and SOS modes. They are on almost every light and are never used on a survey. Fewer modes is a better design; a single button that cycles low, medium and high is all you need.

Waterproof ratings are not an invitation to wade in. IP54 and IPX7 are fine for splashes and a damp void. They do not make a lamp a sensible thing to leave in standing water, and a flooded sub-floor is a reason to stop and report rather than carry on.

Have a backup. A light that fails in a loft is a light that fails when you cannot see the hatch. Keep a cheap AA lamp in the bag in addition to a rechargeable. If you want a camera as well as a light for the same space, a borescope carries its own lamp.

Questions I get asked

What is the best magnetic torch for a trade or survey bag? For most people a flat rechargeable pocket light with magnets, such as the Milwaukee L4 FL-301, is the best compromise. If you do a lot of loft and roof-space work, add an area light like the Scangrip so you can see the whole space, and keep a head torch for walking about.

Will a magnetic light stick to a consumer unit? Usually, because most consumer unit doors and enclosures are steel, though some modern units have plastic enclosures that will not hold a magnet. Check the surface with the lamp before you rely on it.

Is a work torch the same as an inspection light? The names overlap. An inspection light is usually flat or slim, magnetic and aimed at close work; a work torch tends to be a longer torch with a pivoting head. Either can do the job, and the Fenix above is a good example of a work torch that is useful for long voids.

Are LED work lights safe in a damp void? Battery lights are low voltage and generally fine in damp places, which is a good reason to use them rather than a mains lamp on a lead. Check the ingress rating on the listing, keep the charging socket dry, and leave a flooded space alone.

Good light is the first part of a good inspection, and the cupboards and voids it reveals are where a lot of the findings in a report come from.
