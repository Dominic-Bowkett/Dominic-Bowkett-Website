Black spots on a bathroom ceiling, a grey bloom behind a wardrobe on an outside wall, a speckled line along the top of a window reveal. Half the enquiries I get from homeowners begin with a photograph of one of those. The first question is nearly always the same: what do I spray on it? The second, which people ask a few weeks later, is why it has come back.

The honest answer is that mould remover is a cleaning product. It takes the visible growth off a surface. It does not remove the water that grew it, and mould is a moisture problem long before it is a cleaning problem. If a room stays humid for long periods, or a cold wall keeps dropping below the dew point, the spores that are in every home will settle and grow again. I treat a spray as step two: find and fix the moisture first, then clean, then keep an eye on it.

With that said, a good spray is still worth having. A bathroom with silicone that has gone black, or a patch on a cold corner you are now ventilating properly, is a perfectly reasonable thing to clean. Here is how the products differ, and six worth considering.

How I judge a mould remover

Bleach or fungicide. Most sprays on the shelf are bleach-based. Bleach is good at removing the dark stain from hard, non-porous surfaces such as tile, glazed grout, shower screens and silicone. On porous materials such as plaster, paint or wallpaper, the bleach tends to whiten the surface while the growth beneath survives. Fungicidal washes, and kits that follow cleaning with a sanitiser, are designed with the porous surface in mind. Neither is a repair. Both deal with what is left after the cause is fixed.

The surface it is meant for. Read the label for the surfaces it names. A tile-and-grout spray on wallpaper will bleach the paper and leave the mould. A wall-and-ceiling product on silicone may do very little.

Smell and ventilation. Bleach sprays are unpleasant in a small bathroom. Gloves and an open window are the minimum, so you need to be able to ventilate while you use one. Never mix bleach with any other cleaner, particularly anything ammonia-based.

Prevention claims. Several products promise to stop mould returning. Treat that as a cleaning aid, not a promise. A protective film on a cold, wet wall will not outlast a wet winter.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Fix the moisture first. Find out where the water is coming from. Is it condensation from showers, cooking and laundry drying indoors, rain getting in through a failed gutter or pointing, or damp rising from below? A hygrometer tells you the room humidity, and a damp meter separates a surface-damp wall from a wet one. Mould on an outside corner or behind furniture is usually condensation on a cold surface; a dehumidifier and a proper extractor fan are the usual first steps, and a window vac deals with the wet glass in the meantime.

Clean it safely. Wear gloves and ventilate. Do not dry-brush mould, because that puts spores into the air. Spray, leave for the contact time on the label, wipe with a damp cloth and bin the cloth. If the growth covers a large area, keeps returning after the cause was dealt with, or someone in the house is asthmatic, immunocompromised or very young, get the cause looked at properly rather than keep spraying.

When it is the wrong answer. If the plaster is wet to the touch, if there is a tide mark with salts, or if the mould sits on an external wall below a leaking gutter, no spray will help. The damp survey cost guide covers what a proper diagnosis involves, and rising damp treatment cost covers the work if it turns out to be real rising damp. Painting over the stain is the other common mistake; I cover why in the damp proof paint guide.

What the labels push that does not matter. “Kills 99.9 per cent of bacteria” is a disinfection claim and says nothing about whether mould will come back. “Long-lasting protection” is the same. Pick on the surface, the smell you can tolerate and the price.

Questions I get asked

Is bleach or a fungicide better for mould? Bleach is fine on hard, sealed surfaces such as tile, grout and silicone, and it is cheap. On plaster, paint or wallpaper it tends to whiten the stain without reaching the growth beneath, so a fungicidal wash or a two-step kit is the more suitable choice. In both cases the moisture has to be dealt with, or the mould returns.

Why does mould keep coming back after I clean it? Because the conditions that grew it are still there: warm, humid air meeting a cold surface, or a damp wall. Mould spores are present in every home. Cleaning removes the growth, not the cause.

Can I just paint over mould? I would not. Paint over a damp wall tends to blister and fail, and hides what is going on behind it. Clean, dry out, fix the cause, and only then decorate.

Is black mould dangerous? Any significant mould growth is worth taking seriously, particularly for children, older people and anyone with asthma or a weakened immune system. For a small patch, clean it safely and fix the cause. For a large area, or if anyone in the house is unwell, get the building looked at and speak to your GP about symptoms.

If the mould keeps returning and you cannot see why, that is a job for an inspection rather than another bottle: a building survey or a ventilation assessment will tell you whether it is the air, the fabric or a leak.
