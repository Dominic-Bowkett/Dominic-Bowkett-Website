Mould removal
cost
in 2026

Professional mould removal typically costs £150–£600 a room and £725–£2,000 for a whole house. Removing it is the easy half; finding the moisture is the job.

Published 13 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Professional mould removal typically costs £150–£600 for one room, £725–£2,000 for a whole house, and £1,500–£5,000 or more where it has got into plaster and timber. Those are 2026 figures, and London and the South East run 20–30% above them.

But the removal is the cheap, easy half. Mould is a symptom of moisture, and if nobody finds where the moisture is coming from, you are paying to clean the same wall again in six months.

I am a building surveyor and retrofit assessor. I do not clean mould or sell treatments, so I have no stake in the answer. What follows is what the 2026 figures say and what I would want settled before you pay anyone.

The short answer

Prices were checked on 13 October 2026. MyJobQuote (published 11 September 2026, drawn from quotes on its platform), Fantastic Services and a Kent and Surrey damp-and-mould specialist all published 2026 guides, and they agree on the pattern even where the numbers differ. They disagree most on single rooms, which is a real spread rather than a sourcing error: a light surface clean and a room with damaged plaster are different jobs with the same name.

One older source, dated April 2025, puts a large room as low as £50–£250. I would treat that as the best case for a labour-only wipe-down, not a figure to plan around.

What drives the price

The surface. Mould on tile, glazing or silicone wipes off. Mould in porous plaster, wallpaper or timber is a different job, because the growth goes below the surface.

How established it is. A thin film is a morning. Thick, long-standing growth across a ceiling takes preparation, containment and repeat visits.

Access. Lofts, behind fitted furniture and high ceilings add labour. One guide puts a loft or roof void at £300–£650.

What comes with it. Fungicidal treatment is quoted at about £15–£30 per m². Anti-mould paint is £20–£50 of materials a room, and professional redecoration £150–£400 a room. Replastering is £300–£850 a room.

Where you live. The South East premium is 20–30%, so budget toward the top of every range in Kent, Sussex and Surrey.

Why it comes back

The government’s own guidance on damp and mould is blunt: simply removing surface mould will not prevent it reappearing. Landlords are told to revisit at least six weeks after treatment, which tells you how often it returns.

Much of what gets called “damp” in the homes I inspect is condensation. Warm, moist air from cooking, showers and drying washing meets a cold wall, and mould grows in the corners behind furniture and on north-facing walls. A chemical spray deals with the growth. It does nothing about the moisture, so I would not pay a premium for one.

Before you pay for removal, settle which kind of moisture you have. Condensation is cured with ventilation and heat. A leaking gutter, a bridged damp course or a failed seal around a window is cured by repair. I explain how to tell them apart, and what a survey costs, in my damp survey cost guide. If it does turn out to be rising damp, the numbers are in rising damp treatment cost, and I would read damp proofing cost before accepting a chemical DPC quote.

Do you need a professional at all?

Often not. Government guidance says mould and mildew products should be used in preference to bleach for health and safety reasons, with a mask, gloves and goggles, doors shut to stop spores spreading, and a window open. For a few square feet of surface mould on tile, sealant or paint that is a job for a spray and a cloth, and my guide to mould removers covers what is worth buying.

I would pay a specialist where the area is large, where it is in plaster or insulation, where it keeps returning, or where someone in the house has asthma, a weakened immune system or a young baby. That last case is the one that matters.

The sensible order is survey, fix the cause, then remove the mould. Paying for removal first is the commonest way to spend money twice.

Renting? The landlord duty

Awaab’s Law has applied to social housing in England since 27 October 2025, with fixed deadlines for investigating damp and mould once a tenant reports it. It does not yet apply to private tenancies. On 9 October 2026 the government opened a consultation on extending it, which closes on 18 December 2026, and no start date has been set. Private landlords are not off the hook meanwhile, because they still have to keep a home fit to live in.

The practical point for a tenant is to report the mould in writing and keep the date. For a landlord, the cheapest response is to find the cause quickly, because a cleaned wall that grows mould again looks like neglect.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • A diagnosis. A firm that quotes without asking where the moisture comes from is selling a clean, not a cure.
  • “Guaranteed” results. A guarantee on a wall that is still damp is only as good as the firm.
  • Treatment type. Ask what is being applied and why. Sprays and sealants are not substitutes for fixing ventilation.
  • Scope. Does it include removing contaminated plaster or insulation, disposal and re-instatement, or only the clean?
  • Ventilation advice. A good firm tells you about extract fans (£150–£300 fitted, see my extractor fan guide) and heating, not just the clean.
  • Written quotes. At least three, saying what area and what treatment.

Questions I get asked

How much does professional mould removal cost? Typically £150–£600 for one room of surface treatment, £725–£2,000 for a whole house, and £1,500–£5,000 or more where mould has spread into plaster and timber. London and the South East run 20–30% higher.

Can I remove mould myself? Usually, yes, for a small patch of surface mould on tile, sealant or paint. Government guidance says to use a mould and mildew product in preference to bleach, wear a mask, gloves and goggles, and keep the door shut. But cleaning does not stop it coming back.

Why does mould keep coming back after it is removed? Because the moisture is still there. Most household mould is condensation: too much moisture, not enough ventilation or heat. Clean it and fix nothing, and it returns, often within weeks.

Is my landlord responsible for mould? Often, if the cause is the building: a leak, rising damp or a defect. A landlord can argue otherwise where it is lifestyle condensation, but they still have to keep the home fit to live in. Awaab’s Law applies to social landlords in England now and is being consulted on for private tenancies.
