How much
does an EPC
cost in 2026?

Most domestic Energy Performance Certificates cost £60–£120 in 2026 — more for a large or unusual property, more again in London. The bigger question, with landlords now staring down a 2030 deadline, is what that fee actually buys you.

Published 21 Jul 2026 Reading time · 8 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

I quote a fixed EPC fee to most callers within the hour, and the thing that surprises people isn’t how high the number is but how much it varies between assessors for what looks like an identical job. There’s no set national price for an Energy Performance Certificate — assessors set their own fees — and in 2026 the honest range for a standard domestic property is roughly £60–£120, with a small flat nearer the bottom and a large detached home, or anything in London, nearer the top or beyond it.

What moves that price is mostly straightforward: size, location, how many rooms and heating systems there are to record. What should also move it, and often doesn’t, is how carefully the assessor treats a property that doesn’t fit RdSAP’s default assumptions. That distinction matters more this year than it has for a while, with a confirmed 2030 deadline pushing rented homes from an EPC E to a C.

This is my own trade, so I’ll declare that upfront and get on with an honest answer: what a certificate typically costs, what drives the fee up or down, when you genuinely need one, and what a cut-price quote can cost you later.

The short answer

Prices below were checked on 21 July 2026, cross-referenced against Checkatrade, the HomeOwners Alliance and current UK cost-guide data. As with most trades, my own patch of Kent, Sussex, Surrey and London tends to sit at the upper end of the national range, particularly inside the M25.

Commercial EPCs, produced under the fuller SBEM methodology rather than RdSAP, cost more again — typically upward of £150 depending on floor area and use class — and sit outside the scope of this guide, which covers domestic properties only.

What drives the price

Size and room count. RdSAP requires the assessor to measure and record every habitable room, plus construction details for walls, roofs, floors and windows, and the specifics of the heating and hot-water system. A one-bed flat with a combi boiler is a short visit; a rambling five-bed house with an extension, a loft conversion and two heating zones is a genuinely longer one, and the fee reflects that.

Location. Assessors in London and the South East generally charge more than those further north, for the same reasons any trade does — higher overheads, higher demand, more time lost to travel and parking in dense areas.

Turnaround. Most assessors can lodge a certificate the same day as the visit without a surcharge, but a genuinely urgent same-day booking — squeezed in ahead of a completion date — sometimes carries a small premium. Ask when you book rather than assuming either way.

Accessibility and paperwork. A property where the loft hatch is boarded shut, the boiler manual has vanished and nobody can say when the windows were replaced takes longer to assess properly than one where the owner has the original building control certificates and product data sheets to hand. Good paperwork genuinely saves time, and time is what you’re paying for.

Age and construction. Older, solid-wall or non-standard properties take longer to assess accurately, because more of the construction has to be judged rather than read off a modern spec sheet. This is also where corners get cut on cheap quotes, which I’ll come back to.

Do you actually need one?

You'll generally need a valid EPC to:

  • Sell. Required before a property can be marketed — agents are supposed to check one exists before listing.
  • Let to a new tenant. Required before the tenancy starts, and the property currently needs to be rated E or above to be let lawfully unless a registered exemption applies.
  • Complete a new build. Needed for Building Control sign-off and final occupation.
  • Remortgage or refinance. Many lenders now ask for a valid EPC even where the regulations technically don’t require one.
  • Apply for retrofit funding. ECO4, the Great British Insulation Scheme and the Boiler Upgrade Scheme all use the EPC as their baseline assessment.

Before paying for anything, check the register at findenergycertificate.service.gov.uk. Certificates last ten years, and a surprising number of owners pay for a duplicate of one that’s still valid.

The 2030 deadline that's changing the maths for landlords

On 21 January 2026 the government published its Warm Homes Plan response, confirming a single compliance date of 1 October 2030 for privately rented homes in England and Wales to reach at least an EPC C, replacing the current minimum of E. The spending cap landlords can be held to also rises, from £3,500 to £10,000 per property (or a 10% property-value cap for homes worth under £100,000), and the maximum fine for non-compliance from that date rises to £30,000 per property, up from £5,000 now.

Underneath that, the EPC methodology itself is being overhauled. A new Home Energy Model-based assessment is due to launch from October 2026, running alongside the current RdSAP system until October 2029, when the legacy rating is retired. Properties that already hold a C or better under the current system before then are expected to be treated as compliant until that certificate expires. Worth flagging: this is a confirmed policy direction, not yet law — the actual MEES legislation is expected to go through Parliament in 2027, so some detail may still shift. Scotland and Northern Ireland run their own standards.

The practical takeaway if you're a landlord: don't wait for the new metric to find out where you stand. An up-to-date, properly assessed EPC now tells you whether you're already close to C, what would close the gap, and whether that's cheap (loft top-up, draughtproofing) or expensive (solid-wall insulation, a heat pump) — four years is enough time to plan sensibly, and not enough time to leave it until 2029.

Where the cheap quote can cost you later

RdSAP is a reduced data method by design — it’s built to work without opening up walls or floors, which keeps assessments quick and non-intrusive. Where the assessor can’t see or evidence something — wall insulation behind plaster, the age of a boiler, loft insulation depth under stored boxes — the software falls back on conservative default assumptions. Those defaults are deliberately cautious, and on an older or previously-upgraded property they can understate what’s actually there.

An assessor who takes the extra ten minutes to photograph the loft insulation, ask about a cavity-fill certificate, or note a previous EPC’s data can often lift a property’s rating a genuine band without a single extra job being done to the house — purely by replacing an assumption with evidence. An assessor working to the lowest possible fee has no incentive to take that time, and the property owner is the one who pays for it later: in a lower asking price, a failed MEES compliance check, or a retrofit grant application that starts from an unfairly pessimistic baseline.

The fix costs you nothing beyond a bit of preparation: before the visit, dig out any building control certificates for windows, boilers or insulation, any FENSA or cavity-wall-insulation guarantee, and the previous EPC if one exists. Hand it over regardless of which assessor you use.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Confirmed accreditation. The assessor should be registered with a recognised scheme — Elmhurst, Quidos, ECMK and Stroma are the main ones — and you can check any assessor's registration on the government register before booking.
  • A fixed fee, agreed before the visit. Be wary of a headline “from £45” price that grows once the assessor has actually seen the property. Ask for the number in writing against your address and property type.
  • A willingness to look at evidence. If you have paperwork for insulation, glazing or heating upgrades, a good assessor will want it. One who isn't interested is telling you something about how they'll treat the assumptions that can't be seen.
  • A realistic time on site. A genuine RdSAP visit for an average two-to-three-bed home runs 30–60 minutes. A fifteen-minute walk-round on an older or extended property isn't gathering enough to trust the result.
  • Same-day lodgement, if you need it. Most assessors can turn a certificate around the same day as the visit — say so when you book if you're working to a deadline.

Questions I get asked

How long does an EPC last? Ten years. Check the register at findenergycertificate.service.gov.uk before paying for a new one — a valid certificate from a previous sale, let or new-build completion may still apply.

Can I get an EPC for free? No. A new certificate has to come from an accredited Domestic Energy Assessor. What's free is finding out whether you already have a valid one on the register, which happens more often than people expect.

Do I need an EPC to sell my house? Yes, before the property is marketed. It's cheaper and quicker to sort it upfront than to have a sale held up mid-transaction while one is arranged.

How much does it cost to actually improve a poor rating? Too variable to give a single figure honestly — it depends entirely on what's driving the low score. Loft insulation and draughtproofing are cheap and often grant-funded through ECO4 or the Great British Insulation Scheme; solid-wall insulation or a heat pump (partly offset by the Boiler Upgrade Scheme) cost a great deal more. A proper EPC with an honest read of what's realistic for your specific building is the right first step, not a guess.
