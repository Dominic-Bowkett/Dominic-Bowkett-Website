Half the jobs that end with a screwdriver near a consumer unit start somewhere ordinary — a socket that’s stopped working, a switch plate that needs to come off to see what’s behind it, a meter cupboard door held shut by one stubborn screw. The circuit involved should already be dead by the time a screwdriver goes anywhere near it, proved so with a proper voltage tester, but “should be” is exactly why the tool doing the opening needs its own margin of safety. An ordinary screwdriver, however good the steel, has no insulation rating at all — the shaft is bare metal from tip to handle collar.

VDE is the German standard body behind IEC 60900, the international standard for hand tools rated for work near live electrical parts. A genuine VDE screwdriver is insulated to 1000V AC/1500V DC and individually tested — typically to 10,000V per tool — with that testing marked on the shaft itself, not just claimed on the packaging. That is a different thing entirely from a screwdriver with a moulded plastic handle that merely looks insulated: nothing about the look of a handle tells you what voltage it was actually tested against, or whether it was tested at all.

It sits alongside the voltage tester, multimeter and socket tester already covered in this series rather than replacing any of them — a VDE screwdriver doesn’t prove a circuit is dead, it’s the tool used to get the cover off in the first place, and again afterwards once the test has been done. How I judge a VDE screwdriver set

A genuine VDE/IEC 60900 mark on the tool, not just “insulated” as a marketing word. Any plastic-handled screwdriver looks insulated. Only one individually tested — typically to 10,000V — and marked to IEC 60900 is actually rated for work near live or assumed-dead electrical parts; check the marking is on the shaft, not just claimed in the listing copy.

Slotted and Pozidriv coverage before Phillips. UK consumer units, switch plates and socket faceplates are overwhelmingly slotted and Pozidriv fixings. A set weighted toward Phillips is solving a problem this particular job doesn’t have, however complete it looks on paper.

Slim, reduced-diameter shafts for recessed screws. A screw sunk into a back box or tucked behind a faceplate rim needs a blade thin enough to reach it — a standard-diameter driver simply won’t fit the recess, no matter how good the tip.

Interchangeable blades against fixed handles. A handle-and-blade system packs more profile coverage into a smaller kit-bag footprint; a fixed set is faster mid-job because there’s no separate blade to find and lock in. Neither is wrong — it depends what else is already in the bag.

A case or pouch that keeps the set together. A loose VDE screwdriver rattling round a tool bag both risks the insulation and gets mixed in with untested ones — worth as much as the tool itself over a few years of van life.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A genuine VDE/IEC 60900 mark beats a screwdriver that’s merely “insulated.” Any plastic-handled screwdriver looks insulated; only one individually tested — typically to 10,000V — and marked to IEC 60900 is rated for work near live or assumed-dead electrical parts. Check the marking is actually on the tool, not just claimed in the listing.

Pozidriv matters more than Phillips for this specific job. UK consumer units, switch plates and socket faceplates are overwhelmingly Pozidriv and slotted; a set weighted toward Phillips, like the Silverline pick above, is solving a problem this job doesn’t have.

A 10,000V test rating doesn’t mean the tool is safe to use on a live circuit deliberately. VDE insulation is a safety margin for accidental contact while working on a circuit believed to be dead, proved dead first with a proper voltage tester — not permission to work on something known to be live.

Torx and PlusMinus profiles are genuinely useful only if the work actually meets them. The Wiha set above earns its extra pieces on jobs that cross into general trade work; on electrical-only tasks they’re dead weight in the roll.

Interchangeable blades save space; fixed handles save time. A handle-and-blade system like the CK or Wera fits more coverage in a smaller bag; a fixed set like the Draper or Bahco is faster mid-job because there’s no separate blade to find and lock in.

Questions I get asked

What does VDE actually mean on a screwdriver?

VDE is the German standard body (Verband der Elektrotechnik Elektronik Informationstechnik) whose testing underpins IEC 60900, the international standard for hand tools used near live electrical parts. A genuine VDE tool is individually tested — typically to 10,000V — and rated for safe use up to 1000V AC/1500V DC, marked on the tool itself rather than just claimed on the packaging.

Are VDE screwdrivers safe to use on a live circuit?

No — VDE insulation is a safety margin against accidental contact while working on a circuit that should already be dead, not a licence to work on one that’s known to be live. As with the voltage testers and multimeters covered elsewhere in this series, the standard sequence is to prove dead with a proper GS38-compliant tester before any cover comes off, VDE tools or not.

Do I need Pozidriv, Phillips, or both?

Pozidriv, for almost everything in a UK consumer unit, switch plate or socket faceplate — Phillips is more of a general trade profile than an electrical one. A set weighted toward Phillips over Pozidriv, like the budget Silverline pick here, is missing the size most surveys actually need.

How often do VDE screwdrivers need re-testing or replacing?

There’s no fixed UK statutory interval for a hand tool the way there is for portable appliance testing, but the insulation is a physical coating that degrades with wear, cuts and chemical exposure — any visible damage, a cracked handle or exposed metal near the tip is a retire-it signal, not a wait-and-see one. HSE guidance on insulated tools points to a visual check before each use, with anything damaged taken out of service immediately.
