Extractor fan
installation cost
in 2026

Swapping a bathroom extractor fan typically costs £120–£350 fitted; a new fan with ducting runs £300–£850, and a whole-house PIV unit £700–£1,600. The duct matters more than the fan.

Published 12 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Fitting an extractor fan typically costs £120–£350 if it replaces an existing one, £300–£850 if it is a new fan that needs a duct to outside, and £700–£1,600 for a positive input ventilation (PIV) unit that treats the whole house. Those are 2026 figures, and the South East runs 10–25% above them.

But the fan is the cheap part. Labour, ducting and access are what move the bill, and a fan that is fitted cheaply but discharges into the loft can cost you far more than it saves.

I am a building surveyor and retrofit assessor. I do not fit fans or sell ventilation products, so I have no stake in which you choose. What follows is what the published figures say and what I would check before hiring anyone.

The short answer

Prices were checked on 12 October 2026. Book a Builder and MyJobQuote both published 2026 guides (MyJobQuote dated 14 June 2026) and they broadly agree on the pattern, although MyJobQuote sits lower. I would budget the Book a Builder ranges for the South East and treat the lower figures as best case.

For a kitchen, Book a Builder puts labour at £80–£250, the fan at £20–£300 and ducting at £20–£150, so a ducted kitchen fan lands in the same band as a new bathroom one.

What drives the price

Whether there is already a duct. A like-for-like swap uses what is there. A new position means a core hole through brick, a grille, and cable, which is where the cost climbs.

Where the air goes. Out through a wall is cheapest. Through a loft to the roof or eaves adds duct, insulation and access. An internal bathroom with a long run needs an inline fan, which is why those quotes start at £600.

Fan type. Book a Builder puts a basic axial fan at £20–£60 and a centrifugal fan at £60–£150. The extra buys pressure, which you need on longer runs.

Labour and region. Electricians charge about £45–£70 an hour or £220–£350 a day, and the South East is typically 10–25% higher, so budget toward the top in Kent, Sussex and Surrey.

Making good. Retiling, replastering or a scaffold tower for a high outlet are extras. MyJobQuote puts making good at £100–£300 where tiles are disturbed.

The duct matters more than the fan

Approved Document F sets the extract rates: 15 litres per second for a bathroom, and 30 litres per second for a kitchen fan beside the hob or 60 litres per second elsewhere. A fan only delivers that if the duct lets it. Book a Builder notes that each 90 degree bend can cut airflow by up to 20%, so keep the run short and straight, in rigid duct where you can, since flexible foil performs badly.

The point I would press hardest is the outlet. A fan that discharges into the loft rather than outside is a defect I would flag in a survey. The moisture settles on cold roof timbers and insulation, and you get condensation, staining and eventually rot, all in a space you do not look at. Ducting through a cold loft should be insulated for the same reason. If a quote says “fan to loft”, it is not a ventilation quote.

Bathroom fans also need a suitable IP rating (IPX4 as a minimum for most positions) and the isolator switch sits outside the bathroom. That is the electrician’s job to know, and a good reason not to hand it to someone who is not one.

Do you actually need a new fan, or a PIV unit?

Fitting a fan is the right answer when a bathroom or kitchen has none or a dying one. But a lot of “damp” I see is condensation from moisture with nowhere to go, and a fan is only part of that. If a fan runs but the room still streams, check the fan’s real airflow, that the duct is not blocked or kinked, and that it runs long enough after a shower. A humidistat model that keeps going until the humidity drops usually fixes more than a bigger fan.

PIV is a different tool. It blows filtered loft air into the house, pressurising it slightly so moist air leaves through gaps in the fabric. Homebuilding (updated July 2024) puts the installed cost at £700–£1,000 on average and Book a Builder at £700–£1,600, with running costs of about £20–£60 a year. Both are clear on the limits: it does not replace kitchen and bathroom extract, it is a poor fit for flats and open-flued appliances, and installed in a damp loft it simply pushes that damp indoors.

If you are unsure which problem you have, a proper damp survey tells you whether you are looking at condensation or something else before you spend. If the house is being insulated or draughtproofed, ventilation has to keep pace, which is why I measure it in a retrofit assessment. The same logic applies to heat-recovery systems, covered in my MVHR cost guide.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • Where the duct ends. It should terminate outside, with a grille. Not the loft, not a soffit void.
  • Duct type and length. Rigid and short beats flexible foil and long.
  • The airflow rating. Ask for the fan’s l/s against the 15 or 30 you need, not just its brand.
  • Electrics. Registered electrician, RCD protection, an isolator outside the bathroom and a certificate. Guides disagree on whether a like-for-like swap must be notified under Part P, so ask your electrician to confirm what they will issue.
  • Making good. Is the core hole sealed and the plaster or tile repair priced?
  • Written quotes. At least three, naming the fan model and the duct route.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to fit an extractor fan? Typically £120–£350 to replace or upgrade a bathroom fan, £300–£550 for a new fan with a short wall duct, and £450–£950 where it has to be ducted through a loft. South East prices run 10–25% above the national figures.

Can I fit an extractor fan myself? Swapping a fan like for like is within reach of many DIYers, but bathrooms are special locations under the wiring regulations, with zone and IP rating rules. For anything beyond a straight swap I would pay a registered electrician and keep the certificate.

Does an extractor fan need to vent outside? Yes. A fan that discharges into the loft puts the moisture into the roof void, where it condenses on cold timbers and insulation. Ducting to outside air, with a short, straight, insulated run, is what works.

Is a PIV unit worth it instead of extractor fans? Not instead. PIV treats whole-house condensation by gently pressurising the home and does not replace extract in a kitchen or bathroom. It suits a draughty but otherwise sound house with no open flues; it is a poor fit for a tight flat or a damp loft.
