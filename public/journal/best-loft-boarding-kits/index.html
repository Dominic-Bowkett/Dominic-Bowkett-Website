The best
loft boarding kits
that don’t crush the insulation

Half the lofts I go up into have been boarded at some point by someone with a stack of chipboard and a weekend — and more often than not the boards are sitting straight on the joists, with whatever insulation was underneath squashed down to fit. It works, in the sense that there's now somewhere to put the Christmas decorations. It also quietly undoes a meaningful chunk of what the insulation was there to do. Here's what a raised boarding kit actually buys you over doing it the traditional way, six products worth considering, and the load and ventilation limits worth knowing before you start.

Most houses built from the 1990s onwards have around 270mm of mineral wool or an equivalent up in the loft, laid loose or as rolls between and over the joists to hit current Building Regs U-value targets. The joists themselves are typically only 100–150mm deep, because they were sized to carry a plasterboard ceiling below, not a storage floor above and 270mm of insulation. Board straight across the top of the joists and you're either compressing that insulation down to the joist depth, or leaving a gap where the boards don't quite sit flush — neither of which is what the person doing the job usually intends.

Raised boarding kits solve this by lifting the deck clear of the insulation entirely, on plastic legs or an interlocking support system screwed to the joists, so the full depth of insulation stays exactly as thick as it was designed to be underneath a floor that's now up above it, not on top of it. The alternative — and it's a perfectly reasonable one for a lighter storage load — is timber battens run across the joists at right angles, which raises the deck by less but costs a fraction of the price. Below is how the two approaches compare, what a compressed 270mm of mineral wool actually loses in performance, and the six products I'd look at.

Worth saying before the picks: none of this turns a loft into a room. These are storage-load systems, not a structural floor — boxes and suitcases, not a home gym.

How I judge a loft boarding kit

Whether it actually clears the full insulation depth. A system rated to lift the deck 279mm clear does nothing useful over 100mm mineral wool that's already been topped up to 270mm — check the raise height against what's actually in your loft, not the deepest depth the product is capable of handling.

Independent certification versus the manufacturer's own claims. A load rating published on a brand's own site or Amazon listing is a starting point, not proof — BBA certification is the one genuinely third-party sign-off in this category.

Whether it leaves an air gap, not just a storage gap. Raising the deck also stops moisture from the room below condensing against the underside of cold boards — a system that packs the void solid defeats that even while avoiding compression.

Fit against standard joist centres. Most of what's sold here assumes 400mm or 600mm spacing; an older or non-standard roof is worth measuring before ordering.

Cost against what you're actually storing. A BBA-certified system earns its price for regular storage duty. For a handful of boxes twice a year, timber battens do the same basic job for a fraction of the outlay.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The compression figure is real, but check who's behind it. A widely-cited National Physical Laboratory study found that compressing mineral wool from an installed 270mm down to 100mm cut its thermal resistance from 6.11 m²K/W to 3.09 m²K/W — roughly halving its performance. NPL is a genuinely independent UK metrology institute and the methodology looks sound, but the study was commissioned by LoftZone, a company that sells the product that solves exactly this problem. Worth citing as NPL-tested rather than as fully independent research — the conflict of interest doesn't make the physics wrong, but it's fair to know it's there.

There's no line in the Building Regs that literally says “don't compress your insulation.” Approved Document L sets a target U-value for a loft, not an instruction about boarding technique. The practical effect is the same either way: compress 270mm of mineral wool down to a 100mm joist depth and it won't deliver the U-value the regs are aiming for, even though that specific sentence doesn't appear anywhere in the guidance itself.

Check the joists can actually take a storage load before boarding the whole loft out. This is the bit I see missed most often. Ceiling joists in a lot of houses, particularly anything built before the 1990s, are sized to carry a plasterboard ceiling below and the insulation on top of it — not a floor doing regular storage duty above that. Boarding the full loft and loading it with boxes can, over time, show up downstairs as a bowed or cracked ceiling. If you're planning anything beyond light, occasional items, get the joist spans checked rather than assuming a boarded loft is automatically load-rated for whatever you put in it.

Don't board over the eaves ventilation gap. Cold pitched roofs need a continuous ventilation path at eaves level — BS 5250:2021 calls for a 10mm continuous gap above a 15° pitch, or 25mm at shallower pitches, plus a ridge gap on steeper or wider-span roofs. Boarding or stacking storage right up to the eaves blocks that airflow, a genuinely common way a previously fine loft starts showing condensation on the underside of the roof felt. NHBC's own guidance is blunt: a loft "isn't intended to be a storage area," and restricting airflow to make more of it one can lead to condensation affecting both stored items and roof timbers. Leave the last metre or so nearest the eaves clear whatever system you use. LoftZone markets the air gap under its raised beams as removing moisture build-up on the underside of the boards too, which fits the same principle — but that specific claim is single-sourced to LoftZone itself, not independently tested.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a raised system, or will battens do? If the loft has anything close to the current 270mm depth of insulation, battens at roughly 50mm won't clear it and you're still compressing most of the layer. Battens are a fair compromise over boarding with no lift at all, but a leg or beam system is the only way to clear full-depth insulation properly.

Is “Loft Leg” just a generic name for any raised loft leg? No — it's a registered UK trademark held by Loft Leg Ltd, though in practice people (and plenty of listings) use "loft legs" loosely to mean any brand of raised support, the same way some people say "Hoover" for any vacuum cleaner. Worth knowing when you're comparing products, since not every "loft leg" on Amazon is the branded product.

Can I board right up to the loft hatch and walls? Leave the eaves gap clear as above, and don't block any extract ducting or a cold water tank's access and lagging while you're at it — a fully-boarded loft with no thought given to what else lives up there causes problems that only show later.

Will boarding the loft affect an EPC or a mortgage valuation? Not directly — an EPC assessment records the insulation depth present, and boarding over it properly (without compressing it) shouldn't change that figure. Boarding done badly, with insulation visibly squashed or moved aside, is exactly the kind of thing that can get flagged as a defect on a survey, which is a more likely route to a valuation query than the EPC itself.

I don’t fit loft boarding kits myself, but a properly done PAS 2035 retrofit assessment checks insulation depth and sub-roof ventilation before any loft measure is specified, which is the point at which a badly compressed loft usually gets caught.
