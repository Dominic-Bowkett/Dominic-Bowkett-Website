The best
home energy monitors
for what the smart meter can’t show you

Most smart meter in-home displays end up in a kitchen drawer within a month, because the one number they show — total power, right now — stops being interesting fast. The monitors below exist to answer the question that display can’t: which circuit, which appliance, or how much of the roof’s solar is actually being used. Since 2020 or so, most UK homes have picked up a smart meter, and with it an in-home display showing a single number that updates every few seconds: how much power the house is using right now. It is genuinely useful for about a fortnight — long enough to notice that the kettle spikes the reading and the fridge doesn’t — and then it goes quiet in a drawer, because a single whole-house total stops being actionable once the novelty wears off. It can’t tell you which appliance is behind a high bill, whether the immersion heater got left on overnight, or how much of the solar panels’ output the house is actually consuming rather than exporting for a few pence a unit.

That gap is what the products below are for. Some clip onto the meter tails and give a smarter whole-house picture with history and alerts; some clip onto individual circuit breakers in the consumer unit and show a genuine appliance-by-appliance breakdown; the cheapest just sit in a socket and tell you what one thing is costing to run. None of them fix anything by themselves — they’re diagnostic, not corrective. If what you actually want to know is why a house is expensive to heat rather than which plug is the culprit, that’s a different job, and an EPC assessment or retrofit assessment answers it properly rather than a gadget guessing at it.

Where a monitor does earn its keep: chasing down phantom loads, working out whether a home battery or extra solar panels would actually pay back, or settling an argument about which appliance is responsible for the bill going up. Here are the six I’d look at, and what each one can and can’t tell you.

How I judge a home energy monitor

Where it actually measures from. A sensor on the main tails sees the whole house as one number; a sensor on a single breaker sees that circuit only; a plug-in unit sees whatever is plugged into it. That placement sets the ceiling on what question the monitor can ever answer, whatever the app’s dashboard looks like.

A real breakdown, not a confident guess. Some whole-house monitors offer “appliance detection” from a single aggregate reading — software pattern-matching against a library of typical appliance signatures. It's a party trick more than a measurement. A meter that reports what a specific circuit is doing, because it's physically clamped to that circuit, is telling you something real.

Installation reality. A plug-in monitor is a five-minute job for anyone. A whole-house or circuit-level monitor means the consumer unit cover off with the supply live while sensors go round the tails or breakers — straightforward for a competent electrician, and not something to improvise if that's not you.

What happens after the free years run out. Several of these bundle cloud dashboard access into the purchase price for a fixed period, then expect a subscription. Worth knowing before assuming the app works forever on the number printed on the box.

Solar and export awareness. If there are panels on the roof, or might be, a monitor that only counts import is answering half the question. The better whole-house units measure both directions.

The picks in detail

01

Emporia Vue Gen 3 (16-Circuit Sensor Kit)

Best overall — the only genuine circuit-by-circuit breakdown here, and my top pick above

Emporia's Vue installs inside the consumer unit: a main sensor pair on the incoming tails, then up to sixteen 50A clamps around individual breakers, each reporting its own real-time use to the app at one-second resolution alongside a running estimate of cost. That is a fundamentally different proposition to a whole-house monitor guessing at appliances from a single aggregate signal — it knows the immersion heater is on because it’s clamped to the immersion heater's own breaker. It supports single-phase, split-phase and three-phase supplies, and handles solar/net metering if the sensors are placed accordingly.

The catch is installation: this lives inside a live consumer unit with the cover off, which is a job for a competent electrician rather than a confident amateur, and panels with only busbar access (no room to clip a sensor round an individual breaker's cable) need Emporia's flexible sensors instead of the standard rigid ones. It is also the most expensive monitor here by some margin. For anyone who has actually asked "which of my appliances is doing this" and wants a real answer rather than a plausible one, it is worth both the cost and the install.

Owl Intuition-lc

Best established whole-house monitor — a name that's been doing this for over a decade

Owl clips a CT sensor round the meter tails rather than individual breakers, giving one live whole-house figure updated roughly every 12 seconds, with a cloud dashboard and phone app on top for historical graphs, peak-usage tracking and configurable tariff rates so it can estimate spend rather than just watts. The “lc” version specifically supports three-phase properties, reporting each phase separately — relevant to larger homes and some rural supplies. Two sensor sizes cover cables up to 200A.

What it doesn't do is tell you which appliance is responsible for a spike; it's a smarter, more permanent version of the free smart meter display rather than a diagnostic tool for individual circuits. The cloud dashboard is included for the first two years, after which Owl asks for a small ongoing subscription to keep using it — worth factoring into the real cost before buying.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The free display already does more than people credit. If the question is just "how much power is the house using right now," the in-home display supplied with the smart meter, or the supplier's own app, already answers it at no extra cost. Buy hardware only to answer a question that display genuinely can't: which circuit, which appliance, or how much solar is being self-consumed.

CT clamp readings are for spotting patterns, not settling a bill dispute. Clamp-based monitors are accurate enough to compare one day against another, or confirm that something switched off when it should have. They are not calibrated, certified metering equipment, and shouldn't be treated as more precise than that.

Opening a live consumer unit is not a five-minute job. The clamps themselves don't touch a live conductor, but reaching the tails or individual breakers to fit them means the board is open with the supply live. Fine for a competent electrician as part of the same visit; not something to improvise for anyone who wouldn't otherwise take the CU cover off.

Check what happens after the free cloud years end. Owl's dashboard, and several similar products, bundle a couple of years of cloud access into the purchase price and then move to a subscription. That's a legitimate business model, but it changes the real lifetime cost of the cheaper-looking option.

A colour touchscreen is decoration, not measurement. The number that matters is what's actually being measured and from where — a nicer display on a whole-house-only reading is still only a whole-house reading.

None of this replaces proper diagnosis of a genuinely expensive house. A monitor will confirm a heating system runs for eleven hours a day; it won't tell you whether that's because the house is losing heat faster than it should, which is a fabric and ventilation question, not a metering one. That's what an EPC or retrofit assessment is for.

Questions I get asked

Will an energy monitor actually save me money? Only indirectly, and only if something changes because of what it shows. Visibility alone nudges most households towards modestly lower use, but the real savings come from acting on a specific finding — an immersion heater left on, a fridge freezer that's failing and drawing more than it should, an appliance nobody remembers is still plugged in. The monitor finds the problem; fixing it is a separate step.

Do I need a smart meter to use one of these? No. Every product here except the free supplier in-home display works independently of the meter type, because the CT clamps and plug-in meters measure the electricity directly rather than reading data from the meter itself. A smart meter is only relevant if you're relying on its own free display or a companion app that reads its data.

Can I fit a whole-house monitor myself? A plug-in monitor, yes, in minutes. A clamp-based whole-house or circuit-level monitor means opening the consumer unit with the supply live to reach the tails or breakers — straightforward for an electrician to add to an existing visit, and typically only a modest addition to the call-out, but not something to improvise without that confidence.

What's the real difference between a plug-in monitor and a clamp monitor? A plug-in monitor only ever sees whatever is plugged into that one socket — useful for testing a specific suspect appliance. A clamp monitor sees everything passing through the cable it's clipped to, which is either the whole house (clamped on the meter tails) or one circuit (clamped on an individual breaker), depending on where it's fitted.

A monitor is a diagnostic tool, not a fix — if what it turns up points at the house itself rather than one appliance, an EPC assessment is the next step, or a full PAS 2035 retrofit assessment if the aim is to actually bring the bills down. If solar is part of the plan, what solar panels cost is worth reading alongside whatever a monitor like the Shelly EM turns up about current usage patterns, and what an EPC costs if it's been a while since the last one.
