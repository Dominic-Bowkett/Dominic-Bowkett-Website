A slow sink is one of the most common things a homeowner mentions when I turn up for a survey, usually in passing, usually with a bottle of something blue under the sink. The question I get asked is which unblocker to buy. The better question is where the blockage is, because a product that works on hair in a shower trap does nothing for a root-filled clay drain in the garden.

Drain blockages fall into three groups. Soft blockages in the waste pipe from a sink, bath or shower: grease, soap scum and hair. Hard or dropped objects in a trap or toilet. And blockages in the underground run, where the cause is often the pipe itself: a displaced joint, roots, a collapsed length. Chemicals and small augers are for the first group only. Mixing them up is how people spend money and still have a gurgling drain.

This guide uses the independent testing Which? has published on the popular gels and enzyme products, plus the mechanical tools I would consider before a chemical. If the blockage is outside the house, start with the drain rods guide, and if it keeps coming back, the drainage survey cost guide explains what a CCTV survey will find that no bottle can.

How I judge a drain unblocker

What it actually shifts. Grease, hair and food residue are different problems. A gel that wins on kitchen grease may be no better than water on hair. Match the product to the stuff in the pipe, not the picture on the bottle.

What it does to the pipe. Modern plastic wastes tolerate a one-off dose of most household unblockers. Old lead, cracked clay, pitch-fibre or poorly jointed runs are another matter, and repeated chemical doses are not kind to any of them. A mechanical tool puts no chemical in the pipe at all.

Speed against safety. Fast usually means stronger. Stronger means gloves, ventilation and a plan for the splash. Slow enzymes cost you time but little else.

Septic tanks and treatment plants. Several of the products Which? tested carry a warning against use with a septic tank. If you are off mains drainage, read the label before you buy rather than after you pour.

Can you take the cause out again. A mechanical tool lets you remove what is blocking the pipe. A bottle leaves you guessing.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Never mix products, and never follow one with another. Bleach-based gels and other drain chemicals can release dangerous gas or spit back when combined. If the first product has not worked, flush with plenty of water and read the label before trying anything else, or move to a mechanical tool.

Do not leave a chemical in a pipe you are about to rod. If you pour a gel and then pass the problem to a plumber or drainage firm, tell them. A pipe full of strong chemical is a splash hazard to whoever opens it next.

Old drains and repeated doses. One dose of a gel in a modern plastic waste is unremarkable. Weekly doses in a Victorian cast-iron stack or a cracked clay run are not, and the fact that you need them is the real finding. Repeated blockages mean something in the system is wrong: a poor fall, a displaced joint, a root, a missing rodding point.

Where the blockage is decides the tool. One basin slow, the blockage is in that trap. Everything gurgling, or the toilet slow and the bath draining badly at the same time, the blockage is further down, probably outside, and no bottle on the shelf will reach it. An overflowing external gully or a full manhole is a rodding job, or a drainage firm.

What salesmen push that does not matter. “Heavy duty”, “professional strength” and a bigger bottle. Which? found prices that did not track results, and found bicarbonate of soda and vinegar did nothing. Fast, cheap and aimed at the right blockage matters more than a loud label.

Prevention beats cure. A cheap strainer in the shower and plug, grease in the bin rather than the sink, and the occasional hot water flush cost almost nothing. If you are inspecting a house before buying, a manhole key to lift the covers and a look at whether the chambers run clear tells you more than any bottle.

Questions I get asked

Are drain unblockers bad for old pipes? A single dose in a sound modern waste generally is not, but strong chemicals are best kept away from old, cracked or corroded pipework, and repeated use is the habit to avoid. If your drains are old, try a mechanical tool first, and keep gel for the occasional grease problem.

Do enzyme unblockers actually work? They work slowly. In Which? testing the enzyme products took many hours to clear a blocked U-bend, so they suit maintenance and slow drains rather than a sink that has stopped altogether.

Is a drain snake better than a chemical? For hair and for solid blockages, usually yes, because it removes the cause rather than dissolving part of it. For a film of grease on a long pipe, a gel reaches further than a short auger can.

When should I stop and call someone? When more than one fitting is affected, when water backs up in a gully or manhole, when the blockage returns within weeks, or when you can smell sewage inside.
