A key safe is usually the first thing I notice on a rental property before I’ve even reached the front door — screwed to the wall at chest height, sometimes with the digits worn shiny from years of the same code. It tells me more about how a property is actually managed than the tenancy paperwork does. A locked box that’s never had its combination changed, sited directly under the porch light where anyone can work on it undisturbed, is a compliance gesture rather than a security measure.

There’s no law that says a rented property needs one. What there is instead is a fast-growing expectation from letting agents, care agencies and out-of-hours contractors that a key is reachable without a landlord driving over — and an insurance question that catches people out more than the safe itself does. Most home insurance doesn’t automatically void cover because a key safe exists, but some policies narrow it specifically because one is fitted, and the gap between a domestic combination box and one independently tested to resist an attack is exactly the distinction an insurer, or a burglar, actually cares about.

This guide covers both ends of that gap: the certified, Secured by Design “police preferred” safes that genuinely earn the label through LPS 1175 testing, and the honest budget options for a single let where the main job is giving a cleaner somewhere to find a key. Six worth buying, what the rating actually tests, and the code-management habits that undo a good safe regardless of price.

How I judge a key safe

The certification, not the claim on the box. “Heavy duty” and “weatherproof” are marketing words; LPS 1175 is a test. The Loss Prevention Certification Board attacks a sample with defined tools for a set time and issues an Issue number plus a letter-and-minutes rating — Issue 8 A1 resisted a Category A attack for one minute, A5 for five. Secured by Design’s “Police Preferred Specification” badge is only awarded once that independent test is passed; it isn’t something a manufacturer can print on packaging by choice.

Key capacity that matches the job. A single-let landlord needs room for one front door key and maybe a communal fob. A managing agent or care provider covering a property with a cleaner, a gardener and an emergency contractor needs a safe that holds four or five without keys tangling on one hook.

A code you can actually change. Every safe below is user-resettable. A factory-set or effectively fixed code left unchanged after a contractor moves on is the single most common way a “secure” key safe stops meaning anything.

Weatherproofing built in, not bolted on. These live outside, facing whatever the weather does to them for a decade. A sliding shutter or hinged cover over the keypad keeps water and grit out of the mechanism — check it’s part of the design, not an optional extra.

Where it goes matters as much as what it is. The best-rated safe on this list is only as good as its position. Directly beside the door, under a porch light, at head height for anyone loitering — all make an attacker’s job easier regardless of the LPS 1175 number stamped inside the lid.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

LPS 1175 is the only rating worth trusting. It’s administered by the Loss Prevention Certification Board, part of the Building Research Establishment, and tests a sample against defined tools for a defined time — not a British Standard in the BS EN numbering sense, but the industry and insurance-recognised measure for security enclosures like these. An Issue 8 A1 rating means one minute’s resistance to a Category A attack; A5 means five. Only the Supra and the Burton in this guide currently carry it.

A key safe rarely voids your insurance outright — but check anyway. The National Residential Landlords Association warns members explicitly that some policies become ineffective once a key safe is fitted, purely because it’s treated as an additional unattended access point. Confirm with your specific insurer before installing one, and lean toward LPS 1175-rated hardware if there’s any doubt — it’s the detail most policies are actually assessing.

The code is usually the weak point, not the box. I’ve seen the combination written on a label stuck to the wall beside the safe more than once, which defeats the point entirely. Rotate the code after every contractor’s visit, and share it through an encrypted note or password manager rather than a text message that sits in someone’s phone indefinitely.

On a listed building, check before you drill. Fixing a key safe into ordinary brick or render doesn’t need planning permission. Original fabric on a listed building can need Listed Building Consent for even a small fixing, particularly on a principal elevation — a call to the local authority’s conservation officer first is cheaper than an enforcement notice later.

What the listings push that doesn’t matter. “Heavy duty,” “anti-tamper” and “commercial grade” on an uncertified box are just words a manufacturer chose to print; without an LPS 1175 number behind them, they carry no weight with an insurer or a determined thief. A smart, Bluetooth-connected key safe sounds like the obvious premium upgrade, but the ones I looked at for this guide are priced and marketed for fleet and property-management use, not a single let — a certified mechanical safe does the actual job for less.

Questions I get asked

Do landlords have to fit a key safe? No. There’s no legal duty, unlike smoke alarms or gas safety checks. Letting agents commonly ask for one so contractors and out-of-hours staff can get in without a landlord attending, and care agencies rely on them for scheduled visits, but it’s an operational expectation rather than a statutory one.

Does a key safe invalidate my home insurance? Not automatically, but check your policy. The NRLA’s own guidance warns that some insurance policies become ineffective once a key safe is fitted, treated as an extra unattended access point. Confirm with your specific insurer before installing one, and favour a certified, LPS 1175-rated safe if there’s any doubt.

What does “police preferred” mean on a key safe listing? It means the product has passed LPS 1175 testing administered by the Loss Prevention Certification Board and carries Secured by Design’s official accreditation — a scheme run with UK police forces, not a manufacturer’s own marketing claim. Only two safes in this guide, the Supra C500 Pro and the Burton Keyguard Digital XL, currently hold it.

Can I fit a key safe on a listed building? Usually, but check first. An unlisted house needs no permission for ordinary brick or render fixings. On a listed building, drilling into original fabric can require Listed Building Consent even for something as small as a key safe, particularly on a principal elevation — the local authority’s conservation officer is the right first call, not a guess.
