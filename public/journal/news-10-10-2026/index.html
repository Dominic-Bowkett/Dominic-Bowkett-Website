Surveyor’s notes
— 10 October 2026

Quiet Saturday, but three stories from the last 48 hours are worth a surveyor’s attention: conveyancing is taking so long that mortgage offers are expiring mid-transaction, the Building Safety Regulator is rationing its attention as the new levy bites, and a Durham landlord has been fined £26,000 after leaving a family without heating or hot water.

Published 10 October 2026 Reading time · 5 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Quiet Saturday, but three stories from the last 48 hours are worth a surveyor’s attention: conveyancing is taking so long that mortgage offers are expiring mid-transaction, the Building Safety Regulator is rationing its attention as the new levy bites, and a Durham landlord has been fined £26,000 after leaving a family without heating or hot water.

Conveyancing is now a rate risk, not just a delay

Mortgage Strategy’s news analysis on 9 October pulls together the numbers: it cites Propertymark’s September Housing Insight Report putting 51.2 per cent of transactions at more than 17 weeks to exchange, and the government’s own homebuying reform roadmap putting average completion at 120 days. Leasehold is worse still, with Connells data quoted at 155 days to exchange against 97 for freehold. One broker describes a first-time buyer whose 4.39 per cent rate “runs out in a couple of weeks” with a replacement closer to 5 per cent.

The practitioner’s read: every one of those weeks is a week in which a mortgage offer, a rate or a seller’s patience can expire, and the survey is the one stage a buyer fully controls. The Conveyancing Association’s Beth Rudolf is quoted blaming “lack of upfront information, complexity of title” and anti-money-laundering caution, and a solicitor blames Building Safety Act certificates that take landlords four weeks to produce. A buyer who instructs the survey the day the offer is accepted, rather than after the mortgage valuation, takes time out of the front of the chain.

Sellers can do their share too: gather the EPC, any FENSA or Building Regulations certificates, guarantees for damp and timber work, and leasehold management information before the estate agent calls. My bet is that the roadmap’s promised saving of around four weeks, which the article mentions, depends on exactly that upfront information being in the pack, and sellers who do not supply it will not benefit.

One more point the coverage glosses over: a long timeline also hands the buyer’s surveyor a second job. Conditions change over four months, so for a purchase that drags, a quick re-check of anything flagged in the report (a roof repair, a damp reading, a service that needed replacing) before exchange is cheap insurance against a surprise on completion day.

The Building Safety Regulator is rationing its time as the levy arrives

Inside Housing reports that the BSR has activated contingency planning after higher-risk building applications nearly doubled, from 34 in August to 64 in September, as developers rush ahead of the Building Safety Levy. The regulator says it will reject applications only where there is “no realistic path to an approval within a reasonable time period”. Gateway 2 approval rates have risen to 87 per cent over the 12 weeks to 5 October, but the median approval time has crept from 22 to 23 weeks.

Separately, GOV.UK announced on 6 October that Professor Sergio Cavalaro of Loughborough University becomes the Chief Construction and Scientific Adviser, on a three-year full-time term, to support delivery of the remaining Grenfell Inquiry recommendations and regulatory reform. The same release says the levy is expected to raise around £3.4 billion over the next decade to fund cladding removal.

None of this touches a typical South East homeowner directly, and I would not pretend otherwise. It matters for small developers and for anyone buying a flat in a block waiting on remediation: the queue is long, the levy raises the cost of new higher-risk schemes, and a 23-week median means a block’s timetable is measured in seasons. If you are buying a flat, ask what stage the building’s remediation and certification have reached before you exchange, not after.

A £26,000 fine shows why the licence is the easy prosecution

LandlordZONE reports that Durham County Council secured £26,000 in total penalties against a landlord whose Annfield Plain property had no heating or hot water through the winter, with damp, mould, excess cold, carbon monoxide and electrical hazards recorded. The sum includes £7,000 for each of three properties without a selective licence and £5,000 for breaching an improvement notice. The council’s Ted Murphy is quoted saying “selective licensing can be an effective tool to improve standards”.

Note what the heaviest penalties were for: missing licences and ignoring a notice, not the hazards themselves. That is how councils prefer to prosecute, because both are simple to prove. It is also a reminder that damp, mould and excess cold are already enforceable against private landlords today, whatever happens to the Awaab’s Law consultation I covered yesterday.

If you let property in a selective licensing area, check the licence is current for every address. If a council notice arrives, answer it in writing and keep dated evidence of the work and of any access attempts: a claim that the tenant refused access only helps if you can prove it.

What I’d actually do

  1. Selling? Assemble the EPC, completion certificates, guarantees and, for leasehold, management information before you list.
  2. Buying? Book the survey on the day your offer is accepted, and ask your broker how long your mortgage offer lasts against a realistic exchange date.
  3. Buying a flat? Ask for the building’s remediation and certification status in writing before exchange.
  4. Landlord in a licensing area? Confirm every property is licensed and respond to any council notice promptly, in writing.
