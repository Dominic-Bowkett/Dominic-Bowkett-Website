Cold hands are the first thing to go wrong in a winter survey. Moisture readings, notes, a camera and a torch all need fingers that work, and a loft in January or a roof edge in a north-easterly will stop them working long before the rest of you complains. Gloves help, but you cannot operate a damp meter or a phone through a thick pair, and thin ones let the cold straight through.

A rechargeable hand warmer is a small lithium battery with a heating element in a pocket-sized case. Switch it on in the van, put it in a pocket, and take a hand out of the cold for a minute between readings. That is the whole idea, and it is why the specification that matters is not the headline temperature but how long it lasts on a setting you would actually use.

Most of these also work as a power bank for a phone, which on a long day with a tablet, a thermal camera app and a phone on photo duty is not a trivial extra. If you want something for the whole body, the heated gilets and jackets guide is the companion to this one.

How I judge a hand warmer

Battery capacity and the setting you will use. A 5,000–5,200mAh cell is the common size, and 10,000mAh is the large end. Published hours are for the lowest setting. Plan on roughly half that on medium, and assume a battery that has seen a few winters will give less.

Heat settings, and how hot is too hot. Three to six levels is plenty. Most top out in the high 40s to high 50s Celsius, which is warm rather than painful. What you want is a low setting that you can leave on in a pocket for a morning, not a maximum that you use for two minutes.

Charging port. USB-C is the sensible choice, because it matches your phone and tablet. Older micro-USB warmers still turn up in listings, and they need a separate lead.

Size and mounting. A slim pebble sits comfortably in a coat pocket. A big power-bank-style unit is better for the van and the bag. A magnet is a genuine convenience on site, though it should be kept away from anything magnetically sensitive.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Run time on medium beats peak temperature. A warmer that reaches a higher maximum but is flat by ten o’clock is no use. Look for the published low and medium figures and ignore the headline maximum.

The power bank function is a bonus, not the point. It is useful on a day with no mains, but a warmer that is also a power bank is doing two things from one battery. Using it to charge a phone shortens the heating time, and the reverse.

Skip the apps and novelty shapes. A button and three or four settings are all you need with cold hands. Colours, game-controller styling and gift boxes add cost and nothing else.

Cold feet need their own answer. A pocket warmer cannot help with the feet. The safety boots guide and wellington boots guide cover the footwear, and the work gloves guide covers the hands once they are out of the pocket.

Treat the battery with respect. Charge it in the open and not on a pile of insulation, stop using one that swells or gets hot while charging, and do not leave a charging unit in the van overnight. Cheap unbranded cells are where the risk sits.

Questions I get asked

Are rechargeable hand warmers worth it? If you spend winter outdoors or in unheated buildings, yes. They cost little, they last for years if looked after, and they make a cold morning more tolerable. For a short walk to the car, a pair of gloves will do.

How long do rechargeable hand warmers last? Manufacturers publish figures from around 6 to 15 hours depending on battery size and setting. Zippo quotes up to nine hours for its 5,200mAh HeatBank 9s. Treat those as best-case numbers on the lowest setting.

Can a hand warmer charge my phone? Many can. Zippo’s HeatBank and Ocoopa’s larger units list a power bank output. Charging a phone will shorten the heating time, so if you need both, the 10,000mAh model is the better choice.

Are hand warmers safe in a pocket? The surface temperatures are designed for it, and most have over-heat protection. Do not leave one on under a cushion, or in a bag against insulation, and do not use one that has been damaged or has a swollen case.
