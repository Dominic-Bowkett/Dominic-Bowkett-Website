I get called to more damp cellars and semi-converted basements than most people would guess for a practice based in East Sussex, where the water table sits close to the surface on a lot of the Weald clay. The conversation is almost always the same: somewhere in the room is a sump pit with a pump in it, the pump is doing its job, and the homeowner wants to know why the room still smells of damp and why the skirting is stained. The honest answer is usually that the pump was never the whole system — it’s the mechanical bit that empties a chamber the water was directed into on purpose, and the rest of the job (the membrane, the perimeter channel, the render, the ventilation) either was never done properly or has failed quietly in the background.

That matters because it changes what you’re buying. A sump pump doesn’t stop water getting into a basement; it moves water that has already been deliberately collected somewhere convenient, as the last stage of a BS 8102 Type C (“drained cavity”) waterproofing system, or as the stopgap in an older house with a wet cellar and no waterproofing design at all. If the room is dry between rain events and the pump only cycles occasionally, it’s doing exactly the job it was designed for. If it runs constantly, or the sump overtops before the float catches up, the pump usually isn’t the problem — the ingress rate or the system it sits in is.

It’s also worth saying plainly: the UK doesn’t really have the dedicated “sump pump” category American hardware stores sell, with pedestal pumps and battery-backup systems built specifically around a plastic basin. What UK basements actually use, almost without exception, is a general-purpose submersible dirty-water pump with a float switch, dropped into a purpose-built sump chamber. That’s not a compromise — it’s the same pump technology, sized correctly and installed properly, and it’s what every product below actually is.

How I judge a sump pump

Flow rate against the actual ingress, not the box. Manufacturers quote maximum flow with no head and nothing in the way; real performance drops the moment water has to travel up and along a discharge pipe. The number that matters is the pump’s flow at the head your installation actually needs, and whether that comfortably beats the rate the sump fills during the worst rain you’ve seen. A domestic groundwater sump rarely needs the biggest pump on the page — it needs one sized to the pit and the inflow, with headroom, not a headline litres-per-hour figure.

Float switch type and the clearance it needs. Most of these pumps use a tethered float that swings on a cable — simple and cheap, but it needs real clearance to travel freely, which a narrow or cluttered sump chamber often doesn’t give it. A jammed float is the single most common reason a “working” pump doesn’t switch on when it should. A vertical or height-adjustable float (the Einhell GC-DP 9035N below has one) swings through a much smaller arc and suits a tight pit better.

Solids handling. A sump pit collects silt, grit and the odd bit of building debris along with the water. A pump rated for only a few millimetres of particle size will clog; the dirty-water pumps here handle 25–30mm particles, which is the difference between a pump that keeps working unattended and one that needs fishing out and clearing every few weeks.

Duty cycle and thermal protection. A sump pump runs unattended, sometimes for hours during a storm, and the worst failure mode is running dry after the water level drops below the intake — which is exactly when a badly positioned float switch lets it happen. Thermal overload protection, which most of the pumps below have, cuts the motor before that becomes a burnt-out pump rather than a warranty claim.

What it’s made of. A sump pit is permanently damp and sometimes acidic groundwater sits in it for years. Stainless steel bodies (the Einhell GC-DP 9035N, the Sealey) shrug that off better than painted or plain polypropylene housings, which is worth the premium on a pump that’s expected to sit there for a decade, not the one used twice a year to empty a water butt.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

One pump is a single point of failure. Every pump above will eventually fail, jam, or lose power at the worst possible moment — usually during the storm that generated the water in the first place. A proper Type C system specifies a duty and a standby pump on alternating or simultaneous operation, or at minimum a high-level alarm that tells you the primary pump has stopped before the room floods rather than after. The UK market doesn’t offer the plug-and-play battery-backup systems sold in the US; the practical equivalent is a second pump on a separate circuit, or an alarm wired to a leisure battery, specified by whoever designs the drainage.

Where the water actually goes. A sump pump moves water from inside the building to outside it — onto a surface water drain, a soakaway, or a ditch, with the landowner’s and water company’s agreement as relevant. It must never discharge to a foul sewer without proper consent, and the discharge point must be far enough from the foundation that the pump isn’t just recirculating the same water back into the ground it came from. I see this got wrong more often than the pump itself is under-specified.

Float clearance is a fitting problem, not a pump problem. A tethered float needs room to swing; cram a 750W pump into an undersized off-the-shelf sump basin and the float will foul the wall or a cable and either run dry or run continuously. Measure the chamber before buying, and choose a height-adjustable or vertical float switch, like the Einhell GC-DP 9035N above, if the pit is tight.

What the listings push that doesn’t matter as much as they suggest. Peak litres-per-hour figures, quoted with zero head and an unobstructed outlet, which no real installation matches. A domestic sump handling groundwater ingress rarely needs the biggest number on the page; it needs a pump that comfortably beats the actual rate of rise at the head your pipework requires, with the solids tolerance and float clearance that fit the chamber it’s going into.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a sump pump for a damp cellar? Not automatically. A sump pump is the mechanical component of a Type C drained-cavity waterproofing system, and it’s only the right answer once the system around it — a membrane, a perimeter drainage channel feeding the sump — has been designed properly, usually by a specialist waterproofing contractor working to BS 8102. A pump dropped into a puddle in the corner of an unlined cellar without that design will keep the worst of the water off the floor, but it won’t fix rising or penetrating damp, and it won’t stop the smell. If you’re not sure which situation you’re in, that’s exactly what a building survey should establish before you spend money on either a pump or a full waterproofing scheme — and it feeds directly into the numbers in the basement conversion cost guide if a full conversion is what you’re actually weighing up.

What size sump pump do I need for a basement? Size to two things: the flow rate at the head your pipework actually needs (not the pump’s zero-head maximum), and the particle size your sump collects, not just the volume. For most domestic groundwater sumps a 400–750W pump moving 150–220 litres a minute comfortably outpaces the inflow; a bigger, 900W pump earns its place where the discharge run is long, climbs significantly, or the sump serves more than one collection point. Oversizing wastes money and cycles the pump harder than it needs to; undersizing means the sump overtops in the one storm that actually tests it.

What happens if a sump pump fails, and do I need a backup? The sump fills, then overtops into the room it was protecting — usually with no warning beyond whatever alarm was or wasn’t fitted. Power cuts during storms are the classic failure mode, since that’s exactly when the pump is needed most. A second pump, a high-level alarm, or both are the standard mitigations; a single pump with no monitoring is a reasonable risk for an occasional-use cellar and a poor one for a habitable basement room.

Can I install a sump pump myself? Dropping a replacement pump into an existing, correctly built sump chamber is straightforward DIY — check the float has clearance, the discharge pipe is secure, and the electrics are on an RCD-protected circuit, ideally with a dedicated fused spur rather than an extension lead into a damp cellar. Building the sump chamber, the perimeter drainage and the membrane system it sits within is specialist waterproofing contractor work, and getting it wrong is expensive to unpick once the room is finished. If you’re starting from a wet, unconverted cellar rather than replacing a pump in an existing system, that’s the point to bring in a survey and a waterproofing designer, not a bigger pump.
