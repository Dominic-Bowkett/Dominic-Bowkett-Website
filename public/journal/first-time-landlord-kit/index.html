landlord kit

Most of the first-time landlords I meet have bought a sofa before they’ve bought a carbon monoxide alarm. Nine things I’d get sorted before the keys go to a tenant — the compliance kit a licence or an insurer will actually ask about, and the two quieter items that end up deciding a deposit dispute.

A first letting almost always starts the same way: furniture, a coat of paint, a new carpet, then a scramble in the last week to work out what’s actually required by law. That’s the wrong order — everything here is cheaper bought calmly a month before a tenancy than found missing during an inspection or a claim.

This pairs the picks from guides already on this site — alarms, key safes and fire blankets each have a dedicated guide going into more depth, linked throughout — with two items that don’t get their own compliance chapter but quietly decide a deposit dispute: something to record condition properly, and something to catch a problem while nobody’s living there.

What this doesn’t cover is paperwork that has to be a certificate from a qualified person — gas safety, EICR, EPC. Those are linked at the end. Here’s what I’d put in a basket, job by job.

How I’d prioritise a first-time landlord kit

Statutory items go in first, no argument. A smoke alarm per storey and a CO alarm wherever there’s a fixed combustion appliance carry a fine attached — up to £5,000 per breach. Everything else here is best practice or evidence, not a legal minimum.

Certification over the word on the packaging. EN 50291 for CO, BS EN 14604 for smoke, BS 5446-2 for heat, BS EN 1869 for a fire blanket, LPS 1175 for a key safe worth calling secure — every pick below carries the relevant one.

Evidence beats memory in a dispute. A deposit scheme adjudicator wants dated proof — timestamped photos, a recorded note of condition, a humidity log — not a landlord’s recollection of how clean the oven was. Two cheap items here exist purely to generate that record.

A void property fails quietly. A frozen pipe, a fridge left running, damp building in an empty flat — none of it announces itself until the next viewing or the next complaint. The cheapest item here exists to catch exactly that.

Kitchen fire response

05

Firechief Premium Kitemarked Fire Blanket, 1.2m

Cheapest genuinely useful item here, and the one every kitchen should have regardless of what else is fitted

Cooking is still the most common cause of accidental house fires, and no single national law forces every let to carry a blanket — the Fire Safety Order 2005 covers shared and converted buildings, and old LACORS guidance most councils still inspect HMOs against expects one in every kitchen. At around £20 the grey area shouldn’t matter: 1.2m x 1.2m, Kitemark certified to BS EN 1869, no training needed — pull the tabs, cover the pan, switch off the heat. What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Know which items are the law and which are best practice. The smoke alarm and CO alarm are the only two with a statutory duty and a fine behind them. Everything else — key safe, blanket, recorder, tape measure, hygrometer, plug — is expectation or evidence, useful but not something a council prosecutes over.

This isn’t the whole compliance picture. A gas safety certificate, an EICR and, usually, an EPC are all certificates a qualified person has to issue — no product fixes that gap. Budget for them alongside this kit, not instead of it.

An HMO licence changes several answers here at once. Councils commonly specify a fire detection standard against BS 5839-6, which can make an interlinked heat alarm compulsory regardless of the national Alarm Regulations, and licence conditions often name a fire blanket explicitly. Check the licence schedule before assuming the single-let minimum above is enough.

Buy once, buy certified. An uncertified alarm, an unrated key safe or a CE-only blanket look identical to the certified versions and cost a little less. None survive scrutiny from an inspecting officer or adjudicator the way the Kitemarked, EN-rated versions above do.

Questions I get asked

What's the minimum I legally have to buy? A smoke alarm on every storey, and a CO alarm in any room with a fixed combustion appliance other than a gas cooker. Those two carry a statutory duty; everything else here is best practice or evidence-gathering.

Do I need all nine before the first tenant moves in? The two alarms, yes. The key safe and fire blanket are worth having from day one too, both cheap and commonly expected by agents and insurers. The recorder, tape measure, hygrometer and smart plug matter most at check-in and during any void, so they can wait until just before.

What about gas safety, an EICR and an EPC — why aren't they here? None are a product. A gas safety certificate needs a Gas Safe engineer, an EICR a qualified electrician, an EPC an accredited assessor — services with their own statutory timescales. See gas safety certificate costs, EICR costs and EPC costs.

Is a fire risk or legionella assessment something I buy separately? Usually a service, and whether you need one depends on the property — fire risk assessment is mostly an HMO duty, while legionella applies to every landlord but many can self-assess a simple system for free. Fire risk assessment costs and legionella assessment costs cover both.
