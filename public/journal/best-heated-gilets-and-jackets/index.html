From November to March a good share of surveying is standing still in the cold. You are on a roof edge for ten minutes, in an unheated loft for twenty, or walking round a damp garden with a clipboard, and the problem is rarely the first hour. It is the fourth, when your hands slow down, your notes get worse and you start cutting the inspection short. Extra layers help up to a point. A heated layer does something a jumper cannot, which is put heat back into the core while you stay still.

Battery-heated clothing has gone from a niche catalogue item to something sold by every major power tool brand. That is useful, because it means you may already own the battery. It also makes the buying decision less obvious than it looks: the cheapest vest and the dearest jacket use the same basic heating technology, and the real differences are the battery, the runtime and whether the outer fabric keeps the weather off.

This guide covers heated gilets and jackets for outdoor and cold-building work. If your problem is rain rather than cold, start with the waterproof jackets guide, because a heated layer goes under a shell, not instead of one.

How I judge heated clothing

Battery first. The garment is the cheap part. Decide whether you want a self-contained pack that charges by USB, or a tool battery you already own. A body-only listing can look half the price of a kit until you remember the battery.

Runtime on the setting you will use. Every maker quotes its longest figure, which is the low setting. High is for the first ten minutes. Judge a garment by its medium runtime, and assume a battery that is a year old will give less.

Where the heat goes. Chest and back zones matter most, because that is where heat is useful when you are standing still. Sleeves and hand pockets are comfort extras. More zones is not automatically better if it shortens the runtime.

What it does in the wet. Heated garments are electrical items. Most are showerproof at best, so plan on a waterproof over the top. Do not wear one in a flooded crawl space, and stop using any with a damaged cable or a swollen battery.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A vest is usually enough. Arms warm up from a warm core. Spending jacket money on sleeves is only worth it if you want one garment that doubles as your coat.

Stated hours are a best case. A maker’s runtime is quoted on low. On medium, with a battery that has seen a winter, expect noticeably less. Carry a spare if the day is long, or run it on low and add a fleece.

Skip the app and the colour options. Bluetooth control and smartphone apps appear on pricier models and add little. Three heat settings on a button is all the interface you need with cold hands.

Cold feet and hands need their own answer. A heated core helps, but a cold roof still gets in through the gloves and boots. The work gloves guide and the safety boots guide cover those.

Be careful with the battery. Lithium packs and cables are not a place to economise. Buy from a known seller, charge in the open and not on a pile of insulation, and do not wear a damaged garment.

Questions I get asked

Are heated gilets worth it? For standing about in the cold, yes. They add warmth in a way extra layers do not, and a vest is the cheapest way to try it. For a mostly indoor job or a short site visit, a good base layer and a coat will do the same job for less.

How long does a heated vest battery last? Manufacturers publish figures of roughly 8–10 hours on low for a 4,800mAh pack, falling to 3–4 on high. Tool batteries vary with capacity: Makita quotes 8, 16 and 28 hours on high, medium and low for a 5.0Ah pack. Treat all of these as best-case numbers.

Can I wear a heated jacket in the rain? Most are showerproof rather than waterproof, and it is a battery-powered electrical garment, so keep it covered. Put a proper waterproof over it and keep the cable connections dry.

Can I use a power bank? Many USB-powered gilets will run from a power bank, but check the output the garment needs. Using the maker’s own pack avoids a mismatch.
