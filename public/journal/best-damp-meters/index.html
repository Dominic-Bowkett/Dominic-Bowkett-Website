Damp diagnosis is the core of what I do. Retrofit assessments under PAS 2035 stand or fall on understanding moisture, and half the building surveys I’m instructed on begin with someone pointing at a stain. So a moisture meter is the hardest-working tool on any damp survey — and it is also, comfortably, the most misunderstood instrument in British housing.

The first thing to understand is what a damp meter actually measures. It does not measure water. A pin meter measures electrical resistance between two points; a pinless meter measures capacitance through the surface. On timber, that correlates well enough with moisture content that the percentage on the screen means something. On brick, plaster and mortar it does not — the reading is qualitative, a relative signal that tells you wetter here, drier there. Good meters are honest about this and express masonry readings as wood moisture equivalent (WME). Bad meters print “27.3%” on a Victorian wall with two decimal places of false confidence.

What separates the good ones from the junk, then, is not accuracy to a decimal point. It is repeatability, an honest scale, and a design that lets you take fifty readings up a wall in a cold cellar without fighting the tool. That’s the lens for everything below.

How I judge a damp meter

Two modes, one scale. Survey work is a two-step: scan a wall non-invasively to map the pattern, then confirm the suspect areas with pins. A meter that does both, and reports both on the same scale, is worth more than two separate instruments — because the comparison between modes is itself diagnostic. If the pinless mode shouts and the pins stay quiet, the moisture is behind the surface, not in it. That single observation has saved clients thousands in unnecessary “damp proofing”.

Repeatability over precision. I don’t care whether a reading of 18 is “really” 17 or 19. I care that the same spot reads the same number twice, so that a moisture profile up a wall — reading every 250mm from skirting to a metre — means something. Cheap meters drift; you press harder and get a different answer.

An honest masonry scale. The meter should make clear that readings on plaster and brick are relative. Protimeter’s green–amber–red WME bands do this well. Meters that report “% moisture” in masonry to one decimal place are describing a number they cannot know.

Field manners. Pin caps that stay on in a toolbag, batteries you can buy in a village shop, a display readable in an unlit under-croft, and a body that survives being dropped from a loft hatch. This sounds trivial until you’re on your fourth survey of the day.

Spares and calibration. Pins bend and wear. A meter you can buy replacement needles for, and check against a calibration standard (Protimeter sell a “check cap” for exactly this), is a working instrument. One you can’t is a gadget.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The meter is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. This is the single most important thing on this page. A damp meter tells you where moisture (or something that conducts like moisture) is. It does not tell you why. Rising damp, penetrating damp, condensation, a plumbing leak and hygroscopic salt contamination can all produce similar numbers, and they have completely different remedies at completely different prices. The diagnosis comes from the pattern of readings, the building’s construction, ventilation, external ground levels and defects — the meter just narrows down where to look.

Profiles beat spot readings. One high number on a wall means almost nothing. A profile — readings at regular heights from skirting level up to about 1.2m, inside and where possible on both faces — is where the story is. True rising damp shows a characteristic band with a defined upper margin; condensation clusters on cold surfaces and corners; a leak radiates from a point. Whatever meter you buy, use it this way.

Know the false positives. Anything conductive reads as “wet”. The classics: foil-backed plasterboard (a pinless meter will paint an entire dry wall red), buried cables and pipes, dense concrete blocks, carbon-containing materials, some wallpaper adhesives — and above all, salts. Old chimney breasts and walls with historic damp carry hygroscopic salts that hold atmospheric moisture and conduct enthusiastically, so a wall can read high years after the original source was fixed. If a reading makes no sense, suspect the wall before the weather.

What the salesmen push that you can skip. Bluetooth apps, colour touchscreens, and “30 material calibration curves” on a £25 meter. Masonry readings are relative whatever the menu says, so extra material modes on a cheap instrument are decoration. Spend the money on the sensing, the build and the scale instead. The only definitive measurement of moisture content in masonry remains a drilled sample — gravimetric testing or a calcium carbide (“speedy”) test — which is lab-and-specialist territory, and rarely needed if the profile work is done properly.

Questions I get asked

What is a normal damp meter reading for walls? On the wood-moisture-equivalent scale the professional meters use, below about 16 WME is the green zone, 16–20 is borderline, and sustained readings above 20 justify investigation. But on masonry these are relative figures, not moisture percentages — so the more useful “normal” is the rest of the building. Take readings on a known-dry wall of the same construction and compare. A whole house reading 14 with one patch at 24 is telling you something; a whole house reading 18 uniformly is probably just that plaster, that meter, that day.

Do damp meters work on plaster? Yes, as screening tools — plaster is actually where they’re most used, since it’s the surface you can reach. The caveats: readings are qualitative (compare, don’t absolutise), gypsum plaster that has once been soaked can remain permanently affected, and salt-contaminated plaster will read damp indefinitely regardless of the wall behind it. Pin meters read only the surface 10mm or so; a pinless meter sees a little deeper. High readings in plaster with low readings in the masonry behind usually point at condensation or salts rather than water coming through the wall.

Pin or pinless — which should I buy? They answer different questions. Pinless scans fast, needs no holes, and finds the extent of a problem; pin measures at a point, confirms whether the surface itself is wet, and works in corners and on timber where a flat sensor can’t sit. For diagnosis you really want both — which is the whole argument for the SurveyMaster. Forced to choose one on a budget: pinless (the Trotec BM31) if your worry is walls, pin (the Brennenstuhl or Bosch) if it’s timber.

Can a damp meter give false readings? Constantly — false positives far more often than false negatives. Foil-backed plasterboard, buried services, hygroscopic salts, conductive additives and even some paints will all read as moisture when there is none. This is precisely why a meter reading is never, on its own, a diagnosis, and why “the man’s meter beeped so we need a new damp-proof course” has parted so many homeowners from so much money. A high reading is a question, not an answer.
