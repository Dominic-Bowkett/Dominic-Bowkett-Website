The best
roof and soffit vents
and what BS 5250 actually asks for

Black mould on the underside of roofing felt, damp sarking boards, a musty loft smell that's worse in winter — a good proportion of what I write up as loft condensation traces back to ventilation that's been quietly reduced over the years, usually by insulation pushed too far into the eaves. The numbers behind "adequate roof ventilation" are more specific than most trade advice lets on, and the fix isn't always the product people reach for first. Here's what the free-area figures actually mean, when a fan genuinely helps and when it doesn't, and eight vents worth buying.

A pitched roof needs air moving through the space above the ceiling and below the tiles, so warm, moist household air — leaking up through loft hatches, downlighters and pipework gaps — doesn't condense the moment it hits a cold roofing felt. On a "cold roof" (insulation flat at ceiling level, the loft void left at outside temperature), that ventilation runs from soffit or eaves level, through the void, and out at the ridge. Block enough of that path and the felt underside starts sweating on cold, still nights — black mould, rotten sarking and rusted fixings, not just a smell.

How much ventilation is needed is set out in BS 5250, the UK code of practice for moisture in buildings, repeated with worked figures in NHBC's technical standards. It's a free area per metre run of eaves, not a fixed number of vents — exactly where trade advice tends to go vague. A roof with an old bituminous felt underlay needs roughly 25,000mm² per metre below a 15° pitch, or 10,000mm² above it, plus a further 5,000mm² at the ridge on steeper or wider-span roofs. A modern breathable membrane needs far less, one reason a recent re-roof behaves very differently to the felt-and-batten original it replaced.

Where I find staining or mould at roof level and it isn't an obvious leak, a look with a borescope through the hatch beats assuming the worst. If loft insulation is under review, loft insulation costs covers that properly, and if a full re-roof is on the table, new roof costs is where ventilation usually gets specified in one go. How I judge a roof or soffit vent

Free area against the actual BS 5250 figure, not a guess at "a few more vents should help." Under 15° pitch needs roughly 25,000mm² per metre of eaves, over it needs 10,000mm², and steeper or wider-span roofs need a further 5,000mm² at the ridge — work from the vent's published figure, not the grille's size.

Where it sits in the ventilation path. Soffit and over-fascia vents feed air in at the eaves; tile and ridge vents let it out higher up. A roof missing either end won't move air properly.

Whether it fits the roof covering. Universal tile vents adapt to most interlocking profiles; pantile and slate vents are shaped for one covering and won't seat or weather properly on the wrong one.

Vermin and weather sealing built in, not bolted on afterwards — an open soffit vent invites birds and insects the same way an unguarded air brick invites mice.

Whether a fan is actually solving the problem. Most of what's sold as a loft or solar fan isn't a BS 5250 ventilation strategy at all — worth knowing before paying a premium for one.

Fans, condensation, and what a powered vent actually solves

Worth being blunt, because the marketing rarely is: neither a mains-powered loft fan nor a solar attic fan is a recognised BS 5250 route to roof ventilation. The standard's compliance routes are passive cross-ventilation sized to the free-area figures above, or a continuous, well-sealed vapour control layer at ceiling level instead. A fan bolted into a soffit or ridge is neither — a comfort add-on, not a fix for a roof short on free area through a cold, still winter night, exactly when a solar fan produces nothing.

Where a fan genuinely belongs is the in-line extractor kit above: a bathroom or kitchen fan re-sited in the loft for noise, still ducted to the room it serves. Legitimate, common — but not loft-void ventilation, and conflating the two is the mix-up worth avoiding before spending on the wrong product.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Loft insulation pushed into the eaves is the single most common cause of blocked ventilation I see. A generous depth of mineral wool feels responsible, but piled against the soffit it closes the airway — an eaves spacer, fitted before topping up insulation, keeps both.

The 15° pitch threshold matters more than it looks. Drop below it and the required free area roughly doubles — a shallow-pitch extension roof is a genuinely different job to the main house.

The underlay changes the calculation. Older bituminous felt needs the full figures above; a breathable membrane needs a fraction, sometimes none with a well-sealed ceiling.

A warm roof (insulation at rafter level, as in most loft conversions) is a different job entirely — a genuine ventilated airspace above the insulation, or a continuous vapour control layer with none at all. No safe halfway house.

A vent that doesn't match the roof covering will leak before it earns its keep — a common cause of the very damp problem it was meant to prevent.

Questions I get asked

How many soffit vents do I actually need? Work from the free-area figure for the roof's pitch and underlay, not a round number: 25,000mm² per metre below 15° pitch (10,000mm² above it) for a felt roof, less for a breathable membrane, divided by each vent's published area.

Is a solar loft fan worth buying for condensation? Not on its own. Little airflow overnight and none through a grey winter week, precisely when risk is highest. Fine for summer heat relief, not a ventilation upgrade.

My loft insulation's been topped up and now there's mould on the felt — is that the cause? Usually not directly, but if it's pushed over the eaves it can block ventilation that kept the void dry. Pulling it back, or fitting a spacer, is usually the fix.

Do I need Building Regulations approval to add roof vents? Like-for-like replacement generally doesn't. Adding ventilation as part of a re-roof or loft conversion falls under Approved Document C and F — worth confirming rather than assuming exemption.
