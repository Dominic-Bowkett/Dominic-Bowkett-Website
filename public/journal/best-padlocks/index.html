The other reason a padlock earns a line in the kit bag is the van itself. How I judge a padlock

A genuine CEN or Sold Secure rating, not just “heavy duty” on the box. Marketing language on a padlock’s packaging is unregulated in a way the rating isn’t — a CEN grade or a Sold Secure mark means the padlock has actually been attacked with bolt croppers, a hacksaw and a hammer under test conditions.

Weatherproofing that’s engineered in, not bolted on. A padlock on a site gate or a van roof rack lives outdoors for months, and a rubber shroud over an ordinary cylinder is a different thing entirely from drainage channels and a sealed keyway designed in from the start. The first sign of the wrong choice is usually a key that won’t turn on the one cold, wet morning it’s needed.

Closed shackle where bolt croppers are the real risk, open or long shackle where a chain needs threading through. A closed-shackle padlock shields almost the entire shackle inside the body, leaving croppers almost nothing to bite on; a chain, a gate hasp or a ground anchor usually needs the extra clearance of an open or long shackle instead, a genuine trade-off rather than one design simply being better.

Keyed-alike availability, so one key does the gate, the tool box and the van. Carrying four padlocks and four separate keys defeats a fair amount of the point — most ranges below are available keyed alike, worth the small premium once more than one lock is involved.

Shackle material, not just shackle thickness. A boron-alloy or properly hardened shackle resists a hacksaw and bolt croppers in a way an untreated but thicker-looking one doesn’t — two padlocks can look the same size on a listing page and behave completely differently under attack.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

“Military grade” and similar marketing copy isn’t a rating. None of the language on a padlock’s retail packaging is independently tested; a CEN grade or a Sold Secure mark is the only claim actually backed by an attack test, worth the ten seconds it takes to check before anything else on the listing.

Weight isn’t the same as security. A heavy padlock feels reassuring in the hand, but a lighter boron-alloy or Titalium-bodied lock can carry the same or a higher rating than something twice its weight built from ordinary steel.

Extra keys supplied is a convenience, not a security feature. Four keys in the box is handy for a shared van, but it says nothing about how the lock performs under a hacksaw or bolt croppers — check the rating first, treat the key count as a bonus.

A shrouded or closed shackle only matters if bolt croppers are the actual threat. A gate hasp or a length of chain often rules a closed-shackle padlock out on fit alone, so the choice is dictated by where the lock is actually going, not by which one looks more secure in a table.

Questions I get asked

What CEN rating do I actually need for a site gate or van tool box?

For most day-to-day site and van use, CEN Grade 4 is a sensible minimum and covers a tool box, a ladder rack or a routine site gate — it’s been tested against an actual bolt-cropper and hacksaw attack rather than just described as heavy duty. CEN Grade 5 or 6, like the Squire SS65CS above, is worth the extra cost for a genuinely high-value compound or a property with a known break-in history, but it isn’t necessary for a tripod case on a roof rack. Where a padlock protects an insured asset, such as a van full of survey equipment, it’s worth checking the policy for a minimum rating first, since some insurers specify Sold Secure Gold or a CEN grade as a condition of cover.

Closed shackle or open/long shackle — which do I need?

It comes down to what the padlock is actually going through, not which one looks tougher. A closed-shackle padlock like the SS65CS or the Warrior Combi shields almost the entire shackle inside the lock body, leaving bolt croppers almost nothing to bite on, but that only works where the fitting is thin enough to pass through the narrow closed-shackle gap. A gate hasp, a chain or a ground anchor usually needs the extra clearance of an open or long shackle instead — the WS75’s fixed shackle and the 83WP’s interchangeable length both solve that without giving up much resistance, since the body does most of the work either way.

Can padlocks be keyed alike across the van, tool boxes and site gates?

Yes, and it’s worth doing deliberately rather than accumulating whatever padlocks happen to be in a hardware store at the time. Most of the ranges above, including the Squire Stronghold and Abus Titalium lines, are available as keyed-alike pairs or sets on request, so one key opens the van locker, a tool box and a site gate rather than carrying a bunch for each. It costs a little more than buying separate padlocks off the shelf, but the time saved not working through a set of keys at a locked gate on a cold morning covers it.

How do I stop a padlock seizing up outdoors over winter?

Most seized padlocks are a moisture and grit problem rather than a mechanical fault — water gets into the cylinder, sits there, and either freezes or lets rust take hold on parts that never dry out between visits. A genuinely weatherproof design like the 83WP’s drainage channels and sealed keyway prevents most of that from the outset, worth paying for on anything living outdoors permanently rather than travelling in a dry tool box between jobs. Where a padlock already shows the first signs of stiffness, a dry lubricant — graphite or PTFE rather than an oil that just attracts dirt — worked into the keyway and shackle pivots a couple of times a year keeps it turning through the winter.
