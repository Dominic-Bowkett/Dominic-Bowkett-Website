Most of a survey is vertical. Loft ladders, hatches no wider than a pizza box, three flights in a Victorian terrace, a stepladder in the kitchen to look at a flat roof upstand. Every one of those wants two free hands, and the guidance on ladder work expects three points of contact. A tote in one fist rules that out before the day has started.

That is the case for the tool backpack, and it is a narrower question than the one in the general tool bag guide, which covers totes and holdalls as well. Here the format is settled and the choices are about rigid shell or soft sides, how many pockets and where, what the base is made of, and whether wheels earn their weight. “Tool rucksack” and “tool backpack” are the same hunt under two names.

A survey backpack carries different things from a joiner’s: meters, a borescope, a laser measure, a camera, a tablet for the RdSAP software and spare batteries, rather than a framing hammer and a handsaw. They have screens and lenses, and they should not be piled under a drill. Heavier hand tools are better in a van box or on a trolley.

How I judge a tool backpack

Structure. A backpack that slumps when half empty buries small kit at the bottom, and does nothing for a screen when a ladder lands on it in the van. A semi-rigid back and sides, or a full shell, keeps the contents where you put them. Soft bags are lighter and a fair choice for light loads, but they protect only as well as their pockets do.

A home for each instrument. The best bags have vertical or dedicated pockets, so the damp meter lives in the same place every day. At the top of a ladder with a torch in your teeth, the hand goes to the pocket without the eyes. A single large compartment suits tools you can find by feel, not instruments.

The base. Wet grass at eight, a damp cellar floor at ten. A moulded or hard rubberised base keeps the contents dry and the bag upright. Fabric wicks water, and a bag stood on a floor with rising damp will show it.

Weight and carry. Armour costs kilograms. Some rigid bags weigh around 4kg empty, and a soft organiser roughly half that. Padded straps, a chest strap and a ventilated back panel matter more at 12kg than any pocket count. If the loaded bag climbs past 12–15kg it has become two bags.

Fit through a hatch. A loft hatch is commonly around 500mm square. A deep, rigid bag can catch on the frame and have to be passed up separately. Check the depth, not just the litres.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The base, the zips and the straps are the bag. Fabric and pockets rarely fail. Bottoms, zips and shoulder-strap stitching do, because a loaded backpack carries 10–15kg every working day. In a shop, run the main zip ten times, press on the base and load the straps; that takes two minutes and says more than the spec sheet.

Buy for the kit you have. Lay out what one typical job uses and buy the smallest bag that gives each item a slot. A litre figure on the label is no help. If the whole kit fits in a heap, it will end up in one.

Skip the extras. Built-in USB charging ports (a battery bank in a pocket does the same job and does not break), laptop sleeves marketed as tech compartments, and tactical styling. Pocket-count arms races too: a 75-pocket bag where you use twelve is just more to unzip.

When a backpack is the wrong answer. Long corridors, lifts and car-park-to-door jobs favour wheels or a trolley, and heavy hand tools belong in the van. The honest pattern is a backpack for the instruments you carry upstairs and something cheaper for everything else. The tool belt guide covers the pouch route for the rest.

Questions I get asked

Is a tool backpack better than a tool bag? For anyone working on stairs, ladders or in lofts, yes, mainly because both hands stay free. A tote or holdall wins on short carries where you are constantly grabbing and returning tools. Most working kits end up with one of each: instruments on the back, consumables in a cheap bag.

Are tool backpacks with wheels worth it? On purpose-built flats, hospitals and anywhere with a lift and a long corridor, they save shoulders. On gravel, stairs and uneven ground the wheels and frame are dead weight, which is why the convertible format is the one to shortlist. If your patch is rural period housing, spend the difference on a better backpack.

How do I stop instruments getting damaged in a tool backpack? Choose structure first: a rigid or semi-rigid shell stops the crush loads that kill screens. Then give each instrument its own slot or a padded zip case inside a general bag. Finally, do not let the bag travel loose in the van where a ladder can land on it. A hard case per instrument is justified for a thermal camera and overkill for a voltage stick.

What size tool backpack do I need? Smaller than you think. The instinct is to buy the biggest and fill it. A bag that holds one job’s kit with a slot each is easier to carry and easier to search. Bulk and rarely used kit can stay in the van.
