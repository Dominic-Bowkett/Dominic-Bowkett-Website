A good share of the defects I write up start above head height: blocked and back-falling gutters, fascias quietly rotting behind the paint, slipped flashings on low chimneys. And a good share of the injuries in British housing start the same way — someone at the top of a ladder, over-reaching with a trowel in one hand. A ladder is a way of getting to a height; it is a poor place to work at one. The moment a job involves two hands, more than a few minutes, or moving along a run — a gutter clear-out, a fascia repair, repointing a gable verge — a mobile scaffold tower is the right answer, and the Work at Height Regulations have said as much since 2005.

The market splits cleanly in two, and the split matters more than any brand name. On one side are towers certified to EN 1004 — the mobile access tower standard, current version EN 1004-1:2020 — with guardrails, toe boards, stated platform loads and a safe method of building the thing without ever standing on an unprotected deck. On the other side is the “DIY scaffold tower”, sold on working height and price: at best a well-made aluminium frame kit from a long-established British firm, at worst an unbranded marketplace special with no paperwork at all. This is the same enforcement world as ladders — the Office for Product Safety and Standards has spent years pulling non-compliant telescopic ladders off the market, and the unrated end of the tower trade deserves exactly the same scepticism. (If a ladder genuinely is the right tool for your job, I’ve written up the telescopic ladders worth having separately.)

The other thing to get straight before spending anything is the height game. Towers are marketed on working height — platform height plus roughly two metres of you. A “5m scaffold tower” puts the platform, and your boots, at about 2.75–3m. That reaches a single-storey gutter and first-floor windows comfortably; it does not reach a two-storey gutter, whatever the headline number suggests. Every height in this guide is stated both ways.

How I judge a scaffold tower

Certification first. EN 1004-1:2020 is not a sticker; it is a design standard covering strength, rigidity, guardrail heights, platform loading and — crucially — a safe assembly method, so you are never standing on a deck with nothing around your knees. A tower that carries it can be used for paid work. A tower that says “load tested to EN 1004” is making a strength claim, not a certification claim — read those words as carefully as I read a damp-proofing quote.

Platform height, honestly stated. Decide the height your feet need to be, then shop on platform height and treat working height as marketing. Gutter on a bungalow: platform around 1.5–2m. First-floor windows, single-storey roofs and porch roofs: 2.5–3m. Two-storey gutters and fascias: about 4m of platform, which means a “6m working height” tower and a step up in money and weight.

Stabilisers as standard, not accessories. Outriggers transform a tower — owner feedback on every DIY kit in this guide says the same thing: wobbly at full height without them, planted with them. EN 1004 towers specify exactly when stabilisers must be fitted for a given height and exposure. If a seller offers them as an optional extra on a tall kit, price them in; you will want them.

One-person reality. A tower you cannot carry through the side gate, or assemble without a second pair of hands, stays in the garage while you fetch the ladder. Component weight, pack size and whether the frames fit in an estate car matter as much as the load rating. This is the entire argument for the MiniMax and the MI TOWER below.

A firm that will still exist. Castors seize, platform hooks crack, wingnuts walk off into the flower bed. Youngman, Toptower, Loyal and BPS have been selling spares for years and answer a phone in England. The unbranded £250 special has no spares, no manual worth the name, and no one to write to when a weld looks wrong.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Hire or buy: do the arithmetic before the shopping. A decent alloy tower hires for roughly £60–£80 a week from the national chains, plus delivery, and what turns up is usually a BoSS Clima or similar — the £1,500-plus trade benchmark few private buyers could justify owning. For a one-off soffit repaint, hiring a proper EN 1004 tower is both cheaper and safer than buying a budget kit. The buying case starts when the weeks add up: a £300 DIY tower pays for itself in four or five hire-weeks, a MiniMax in nine or ten, the MI TOWER only if working at height is part of how you earn. Most people’s honest answer is a modest owned tower for the recurring low jobs and a hired trade tower for the occasional big one.

Ground and gradient beat everything above them. A tower is only as good as what its castors sit on. Firm, level and locked is the whole religion: boards or slabs under soft lawn, adjustable legs on a fall, castors braked before anyone climbs. Tower incidents rarely start with a failed weld; they start with the setup — no outriggers, a slope nobody measured, or someone wheeling the tower along with themselves still on the platform. Climb down, clear the deck, then move it.

If it’s for work, it’s PASMA territory. Use a tower in the course of paid work and the Work at Height Regulations expect demonstrable competence — in practice a PASMA Towers for Users card, a one-day course, and a tower assembled by the 3T (through-the-trap) or advance-guardrail method. Site managers ask for the card and the tower’s EN 1004 plate, in that order. None of this applies in law to your own gutters at home; all of the physics does.

What the listings push that you can skip. Working-height superlatives, mostly. A “massive 8m reach” on a 600mm-wide domestic frame is not a feature, it is a warning — the sensible DIY makers cap their platforms around 3m and say so. Ignore tool trays, ladder clips and “aircraft-grade aluminium” (all of it is, when the copywriter needs it to be). Spend the attention, and the money, on the boring things: certification if you work, outriggers regardless, platform height in metres, and a manufacturer with a phone number.

Questions I get asked

What size scaffold tower do I need for gutter work? Work from your feet, not the marketing. A typical single-storey gutter line sits around 3–3.5m, so a platform at 1.5–2m does it — a “4m working height” kit. A two-storey gutter at about 5–5.5m needs your boots at roughly 3.5–4m: that is the Toptower 5.5m, a MiniMax with the right pack, or the 4m-platform MI TOWER — not a “5m scaffold tower”, whose platform stops nearly a storey short of a two-storey eaves line.

Do I need training to use a scaffold tower at home? Legally, no — the Work at Height Regulations bite on work, not on householders. Practically, borrow the professional habits anyway: build it exactly per the manual, fit every brace and guardrail, come up through the trapdoor rather than climbing the outside, and never stand on an unguarded platform “just for a second”. If you are a tradesperson, assume any organised site will want a PASMA card and an EN 1004 tower before you leave the ground.

Are cheap DIY scaffold towers legal — and are they safe? Legal to sell and to use at home, yes; certified for work, no. The gap between a £280 kit and an EN 1004 tower is real: no guardrail-first assembly method, lighter frames, a rating that assumes one careful adult rather than a site. Used within those limits — level ground, outriggers on, one person, no improvised extensions — the established-brand kits here have years of owner feedback behind them. The ones to avoid are the unbranded marketplace towers with no stated standard, no manufacturer and no spares; that is exactly where product-safety enforcement keeps finding trouble with ladders, and towers fall further.

Can I use a scaffold tower on a slope or on grass? Only with the tools designed for it, and only modestly. Adjustable legs take out small falls — a driveway crossfall, a patio step-down — they do not make a hillside level. On grass or soft ground, spread the load with boards or slabs under the castors and recheck after rain: a leg that sinks 20mm moves the top of a 4m tower a long way. If the setup starts to feel like an engineering problem, that is the signal to hire proper access, or someone who owns it.
