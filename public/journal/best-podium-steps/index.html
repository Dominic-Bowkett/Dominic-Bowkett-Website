The best
podium steps
for site access

A stepladder gets you up; it doesn’t keep you there safely once a job takes longer than thirty seconds. A podium step trades a bit of extra bulk in the van for a proper platform to stand on — useful for anything that needs both hands free and more than a moment’s balance, from a communal stairwell smoke detector to a meter cupboard mounted just out of reach. Here’s what BS8620 and PAS250 actually mean, and seven podium steps worth carrying, from a £180 fibreglass platform to a £750 fully caged unit.

Half the times I’d reach for a podium step aren’t in a loft — they’re on a half-landing of a converted Victorian house, checking an emergency light fitting or a smoke detector bolted to the ceiling three metres up. A telescopic ladder gets a foot near it for long enough to glance and climb back down. A podium step gives a flat, guarded platform to actually stand on while you look properly, take a photograph, or write up a defect without one hand permanently locked to a rail. For EPC and retrofit work in particular — communal stairwells, hallway extract fans, meter cupboards fixed above head height — that difference adds up over a working week.

The products in this guide split into two genuinely different categories, and it matters which one a job actually calls for. A caged podium built to BS8620 (also referred to as PAS250) surrounds the platform on all four sides with a guardrail and usually a self-closing saloon gate, so you step in, the gate shuts behind you, and you’re enclosed before you start work. A platform stepladder built to EN131 Professional is closer to a wide-topped swingback ladder — no cage, often no gate, but lighter, cheaper, and frequently made from non-conductive fibreglass for work near electrics. Neither is universally “better”; they suit different jobs, and the comparison below covers both.

None of the units below have had a survey van’s worth of genuine use behind them — what follows is built from manufacturers’ specifications and the patterns in published owner feedback, filtered through what a working surveyor actually needs rather than what a warehouse fleet buyer needs. How I judge a podium step

Certification, and which type it actually is. A caged unit built to BS8620/PAS250 and a platform stepladder built to EN131 Professional are both legitimate, but they’re not interchangeable — check which standard is quoted and what it actually covers before comparing price against price.

Guardrail and gate design. A self-closing gate that shuts automatically once you step onto the platform is a genuine safety feature, not a marketing line — it means the enclosure is complete without you having to remember to latch anything. A platform stepladder without a cage relies on a rear rail and your own balance instead.

Platform height matched to the actual job, not the maximum on the box. Most survey access points — stairwell fittings, meter cupboards, loft hatch surrounds — sit in the 1–1.75m platform range. A unit that goes taller than that is a specialist tool, not a better everyday one.

Weight and one-person handling. It has to come out of the van, up a stairwell without a lift, and back down again, alone, more often than not. A folded weight in the 20–40kg range is manageable; anything heavier needs a trolley or a second pair of hands.

Footprint and stability on the actual surface. Outriggers and non-slip platform surfaces matter on an uneven driveway or a worn stone hallway floor. A handful of podiums are built with adjustable rear legs specifically for use on a staircase — a standard flat-ground unit should never be improvised onto steps instead.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Certification matters; the branding around it mostly doesn’t. BS8620/PAS250 for caged podiums and EN131 Professional for platform stepladders are the two standards worth checking for on any listing. Beyond that, marketing language like “industrial strength” or “heavy duty” on a product that already meets one of those standards is decoration, not a differentiator.

A self-closing gate is a genuine safety feature. It removes the single most common failure mode of an open-guardrail platform — someone forgetting, or not bothering, to close a manual barrier before starting work. It’s worth the extra cost and weight over a guardrail-only design if the job means standing on the platform for more than a minute or two at a time.

Castors are convenience, not a safety feature. A podium should never be moved with anyone standing on it, wheels or not — step down, wheel it, step back up. Locking castors matter for getting a heavy unit through a building without lifting it, not for working safely once it’s in position.

The 150kg load rating is standard, not a selling point. Nearly every unit in this guide, caged or platform-stepladder style, carries the same 150kg figure. It’s a baseline to confirm is present, not a spec worth comparing between products that all meet it.

Maximum height sells better than it performs on a typical job. A 2m-plus platform looks like more capability on a spec sheet, but most survey access points — stairwell fittings, meter cupboards, loft hatches — sit well under 1.75m. Buying more height than a job needs mostly buys more weight to carry and more storage space in the van.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a podium step, or is a stepladder enough?

It depends on how long you’re actually going to be at height. For a five-second glance — checking a loft hatch is shut properly, say — a step ladder or telescopic ladder is quicker to deploy and put away. Once a job needs both hands free for more than a moment — photographing a defect, checking a smoke detector properly, working on a fitting — a platform to stand on rather than a rung to balance on is the safer and, over a few minutes, less tiring option.

What’s the difference between BS8620 and EN131 Professional?

BS8620 (often marketed as PAS250, its predecessor standard) covers caged podium steps specifically — a platform enclosed by a guardrail on all sides, usually with a gate. EN131 Professional is a broader ladder standard that also covers platform stepladders — a wide-topped stepladder with a rear rail but no full cage. Both are legitimate professional standards; they simply apply to different product types, so check which one a specific listing is certified to rather than assuming either implies the other.

Can a podium step be used safely on a staircase?

Only if it’s specifically designed for it, like the BPS Stair Podium above with its adjustable rear legs. A standard flat-ground podium set up unevenly across steps is unstable in a way its rating doesn’t account for — if a job regularly means working on a staircase or half-landing, it’s worth buying the purpose-built version rather than improvising with a standard unit.

Do I need training or a certificate to use a podium step?

There’s no separate qualification required to use a podium step the way PASMA covers mobile scaffold towers — but the Work at Height Regulations 2005 still apply, meaning anyone using one, employed or self-employed, needs to be competent to assemble and use it correctly. In practice that means reading the manufacturer’s instructions properly, checking the platform is level and locked before stepping on, and never exceeding the rated load. It’s worth doing once, properly, rather than assuming a podium is self-explanatory because it looks simple.

A podium step earns its space in the van on the jobs a stepladder makes uncomfortable rather than impossible — communal stairwells, meter cupboards, anything needing more than a glance.
