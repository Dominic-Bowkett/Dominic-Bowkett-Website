A survey produces a steady trickle of jobs too small to justify unpacking the proper tool for. Freeing a stiff meter cupboard catch, snipping the cable tie holding a loose alarm sensor, trimming a damp-meter probe cover, gripping a rounded brass stopcock nut that will not turn by hand, opening a sample bag sealed a little too well. None of these is a job for the SDS drill or the cordless screwdriver covered elsewhere on this site — it is a job for whatever is already in a pocket or clipped to a belt, and that is the gap a multitool fills.

The category splits more sharply than most kit on this site. At one end sit full-size, pliers-based tools built to trade standards — genuinely useful for anything involving grip, cutting or leverage. At the other sit keychain minis that are really a knife, a bottle opener and a pair of tweezers with delusions of grandeur. Both have a place in a surveyor’s pocket; the trick is knowing which jobs actually need which tool, rather than buying the one with the biggest number printed on the packet.

One thing sets this category apart from every other guide here: several of these tools carry a locking blade, and that is not just a spec — it is a legal question in England and Wales. More on that below, but it is worth reading before buying the biggest one on this list. First, the one I’d buy.

How I judge a pocket multitool

Pliers quality first, because that is the tool actually reached for. The knife blade gets the marketing, but on a survey the pliers do the real work — gripping a rounded nut, bending a bent bracket back, pulling a splinter of lath out of a wall. Jaws that meet cleanly at the tip and a spring that opens one-handed matter more than anything else on the spec sheet.

One-hand access to the tool actually needed. A hatch cover, a torch, a stopcock — the other hand is rarely free. A tool with the blade or pliers accessible from the outside, without unfolding the whole handle first, is worth more in practice than a longer tool list buried behind a stiff frame.

Locking blade versus UK carrying law. Full-size multitools nearly always include a locking knife blade, and a locking blade is treated in law the same as a fixed blade — carrying one in public needs a good reason, work being the obvious one. It is a genuine factor in choosing between a full-size tool and a compact, non-locking one, not a technicality to skim past.

Replaceable wear parts over a headline warranty. A lifetime warranty sounds generous until the wire cutters dull and the whole tool has to go back. Leatherman and Victorinox both sell replacement cutter inserts for their full-size tools — a genuinely more useful guarantee of long service than the warranty term itself.

Carry method that matches how the rest of the kit is worn. A nylon belt sheath competes for space with a tool belt, knee pads and a phone holster already in use; a pocket clip does not. Neither is wrong, but it is worth matching the tool to how the rest of the day is dressed.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Tool count is marketing, not usefulness. An “18-in-1” or “24-in-1” badge counts every blade, file face and screwdriver bit separately, and several of those functions never get used on a survey. What is worth paying for is pliers that grip cleanly, cutters that stay sharp, and a driver set that covers Pozidriv and Phillips — not a bigger number on the box.

A locking blade is a legal question, not just a safety feature. Under Section 139 of the Criminal Justice Act 1988, a locking folding knife is treated in law the same as a fixed blade, and carrying one in public requires a good reason — work being the standard one, provided it can actually be explained if asked. Every full-size tool in this comparison carries a locking blade; only the Gerber Dime’s short, non-locking blade falls outside that rule entirely.

Replaceable wear parts beat a headline warranty. Leatherman and Victorinox both sell replacement wire-cutter inserts for their full-size tools, which matters more day to day than a 25-year or lifetime warranty term — a dull cutter is a cheap part swap rather than a reason to send the whole tool back.

Blade steel grades matter less than whether the tool gets carried at all. 154CM versus 420HC is a real difference in edge retention, but it is a marginal one next to the basic question of whether a tool small and light enough to actually be in a pocket on the day it is needed, rather than left in a drawer because the trade-grade option felt like too much to carry.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a multitool if I already carry a screwdriver and voltage tester?

Yes, for a different set of jobs. A cordless screwdriver and a voltage tester cover driving fixings and checking for live conductors; neither grips a rounded nut, snips a cable tie or trims a probe cover. A pocket multitool fills the gap between those dedicated tools rather than replacing either of them — it is the thing reached for when the job is too small or too odd to justify the proper tool.

Is it legal to carry a locking-blade multitool for survey work in the UK?

Generally yes, provided it is genuinely for work and that can be explained if asked. English and Welsh law treats a locking folding blade the same as a fixed blade under the Criminal Justice Act 1988, meaning it needs a good reason to be carried in public — a working tool used for a specific job is the standard example, but the burden is on the carrier to justify it, and “it might be useful” is not enough. Carrying it only when it is actually needed for work, rather than as a general everyday item, is the safer practice.

What tool count do I actually need for survey work?

Far fewer than the marketing numbers suggest. Pliers, wire cutters, one flathead and one Pozidriv/Phillips driver, and a blade cover the great majority of jobs that come up — everything from the Gerber Dime’s 12 tools up to the Victorinox’s 24 delivers that core set; the extra functions above it are rarely the deciding factor.

Can the wire cutters be replaced when they dull?

On the full-size Leatherman and Victorinox tools, yes — both sell replacement cutter inserts rather than requiring the whole tool to be sent back or replaced. It is worth checking before buying a budget tool, since several of the cheaper options in this comparison do not offer the same replaceable-parts arrangement.

A pocket multitool handles the small, odd job that turns up between the proper checks; working out what is actually wrong with a property, and what it will cost to put right, is what a building survey is for.
