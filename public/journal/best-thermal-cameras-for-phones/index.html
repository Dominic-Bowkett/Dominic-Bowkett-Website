A phone thermal camera is the version of this tool most people actually buy. The main thermal imaging camera guide covers the full range — phone plug-ins through to professional handhelds — but skates past this category in a paragraph, and it’s the one most homeowners and budget-conscious site staff actually want covered properly. Seven cameras that clip, plug or pair to a phone you already own, none pretending to be a £1,200 handheld.

The pitch is straightforward — a genuine 256×192 thermal sensor, the same class fitted to cameras costing four times as much, for around £250 in a module smaller than a memory stick. What the pitch leaves out is that “works with your phone” is doing a lot of quiet work: Android and iPhone use different connectors, some units tie up your phone’s only port and drain its battery, and a case that’s fine for everyday use can block the socket a dongle needs. None of that is a reason to avoid this category — it’s a reason to buy the right one first time.

Keep the physics in mind. These units measure surface temperature, not moisture, and need a real temperature difference between inside and out to show anything useful. A dongle on a mild afternoon with the heating off shows a flat grey nothing whatever the resolution on the box. Cold morning, heating on since before dawn, curtains open — that’s when any of these earn their keep.

How I judge a phone thermal camera

Native IR resolution, not the “enhanced” number. The figure that matters is the sensor’s physical pixel count. 256×192 is achievable at this price point now, and the floor I’d treat as fit for a report; 160×120 and below is curiosity, not a conclusion. Software upscaling makes the on-screen image bigger, not more accurate.

Which phone it actually fits. This is the question the resolution spec doesn’t answer. USB-C and Lightning are not interchangeable, several models sell separate SKUs for each, and a wrong purchase is the single most common mistake here. Check the connector in the listing, not just the brand.

What it does to your phone while it works. Every wired unit here draws power through the port it’s plugged into, draining your phone’s battery alongside the camera’s own. A thick case can also block the socket a dongle needs — worth checking before you’re stood in a loft with a case you can’t get off.

Thermal sensitivity (NETD). The smallest temperature difference the sensor can distinguish, quoted in millikelvin. Under 50mK a cold bridge renders as a defined shape; well above it, the same defect is a vague smudge — where the cheapest options fall away.

The app. A phone thermal camera lives or dies by its software — export quality, whether it saves radiometric files you can re-analyse later, and whether it fights you every time you open it. None here are brilliant; some are noticeably less annoying than others.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Check the connector before the resolution. Every spec sheet leads with pixel count. What actually stops a purchase working is the connector: USB-C, Lightning, or a wireless pairing, and several models here sell entirely separate listings per connector. Read the listing title in full before you buy.

A thick phone case can beat you before the camera does. Wired dongles need to seat fully into the port, and a rugged case with a raised bezel or port cover — exactly the kind sensible on a building site — can stop that. Either buy a case with an open port, or accept you’ll be removing it before every use.

Battery drain is real, not a footnote. Every wired camera here pulls power from the phone it’s plugged into on top of running its own sensor and screen. On a cold morning survey with the phone already working hard for photos, that adds up — a power bank in the van is cheap insurance.

“Super resolution” is a screen size, not a sensor spec. TOPDON’s TISR, and equivalents from other brands, upscale the native image with software interpolation to look sharper on screen. It changes nothing about what the camera can actually resolve — find the native pixel count before comparing two cameras.

What the salesmen push that you can ignore: temperature ranges up to 550°C (a house lives between about −10 and 60°C), the number of colour palettes, and frame rate above about 25Hz. One thing that genuinely matters and rarely gets mentioned: whether the app saves radiometric files you can re-analyse later, rather than a flattened image showing only whatever the screen displayed on site.

Questions I get asked

Are phone thermal cameras any good, or are they toys? The current 256×192 units — the Mini2, InfiRay P2 Pro, the two TOPDON models — have sensors in the same class as dedicated cameras costing three or four times as much, and the images they produce are genuinely usable for cold bridging, missing insulation and draughts. What separates them from a standalone camera is convenience, not optics: they occupy your phone and have no dedicated screen for one-handed use on a ladder. The true toy tier is 80×60 sensors like the FLIR ONE Gen 3 — fine for curiosity, not a conclusion.

Should I buy Android or iPhone compatible? Buy for the phone you actually own — but if the camera might need to work with someone else’s phone too, the dual-compatible units (HIKMICRO Mini2 V2, TOPDON TC002C Duo) remove the question for a modest premium. iPhone owners have fewer options generally; Seek Thermal’s Lightning models and the FLIR ONE Edge Pro’s wireless pairing are the two realistic routes.

Can a phone thermal camera find damp? Not directly — no thermal camera measures moisture. What it sees is the temperature signature damp often creates: evaporative cooling on a wet wall, or saturated masonry lagging behind the room’s temperature. That makes even a modest sensor a useful first screen for where to look closer, but a cold patch on a phone screen is a prompt to check with a damp meter, not a diagnosis on its own.

Will it work through my phone case? Wireless units like the FLIR ONE Edge Pro don’t care. Wired dongles need the port clear, and a case with a raised edge or port flap can stop the connector seating — check this before you’re stood in a loft unable to fit the camera on.
