A multimeter earns different real estate in a tool bag than most of what’s in this series — it isn’t there to prove one named fault the way a damp meter or a socket tester is, it’s there for the moment something looks dead, faulty or simply unaccounted for and a client wants an answer before the report goes out. Here is what is worth carrying for that, from a trade-grade auto-ranging meter to a genuinely capable budget option, and what actually earns the extra spend.

An immersion heater that won’t heat, an extractor fan that doesn’t turn, a storage heater that’s stone cold on a January visit — once the obvious explanations are ruled out (switched off, tripped, simply unplugged), the next question is whether the fault is electrical at all. A multimeter is the tool that answers it: is this cable continuous end to end, does this element or connection have the resistance it should, is a fuse actually blown rather than just suspected. It is the general-purpose instrument in this series — not built to prove one specific fault the way a damp meter or a socket tester is, but to reach anywhere a probe can go and measure what’s actually happening once something is already off or already suspect.

That makes it a different animal to the socket tester covered elsewhere in this series. A socket tester is a fixed-format plug-in check on one 13A outlet. A multimeter goes wherever the job goes — immersion heater terminals, an extractor fan’s flex, a suspect junction box behind an access panel, the element on a storage heater — and reads voltage, continuity and resistance rather than just flagging a wiring pattern. The two do different jobs and a lot of survey kit bags end up carrying both.

It is worth being clear about what a multimeter is not, too. HSE guidance on safe isolation calls for a dedicated two-pole voltage tester that meets GS38, proved before and after use — not a multimeter’s rotary dial and shared probe sockets, which are built for diagnosis rather than the specific job of proving a circuit dead before hands go near it. Treat a multimeter as the tool for working out what’s wrong once something is already switched off or already suspect, not as the thing that makes it safe to touch. First, the one I’d buy.

How I judge a multimeter

Auto-ranging over manual, and a CAT rating stated plainly. There is no time on site to guess which range a manual-ranging dial should sit on before a reading appears, and a rating of CAT III 300V or better should be printed on the casing itself — not implied by the price or buried in a spec sheet nobody reads before buying.

Non-contact voltage detection as the first check, not the only one. A built-in NCV sensor is a fast, useful first pass — hover it near a cable or terminal before anything else happens — but it is a screening tool, not proof of anything, and the meter’s proper voltage function still has to confirm what NCV suggests.

A continuity buzzer loud enough to hear from a loft hatch. Testing a cable run or an appliance element usually means one hand on the probes and eyes somewhere else entirely — the buzzer needs to do the work the display can’t.

True RMS if it will ever see anything other than a clean sine wave. Modern LED drivers, variable-speed fan controllers and some heating controls distort the waveform enough that an averaging meter reads the wrong number. A true-RMS meter reads correctly regardless.

Robust enough for a tool bag, not a bench instrument. A rubberised overmould, a sensible bail stand or magnetic hang point, and probes that won’t need replacing after a few months matter more day to day than an extra decimal place of resolution.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Count number is marketing, CAT rating and auto-range are not. A “6000 counts” display resolution sounds like a spec that matters, but every meter in this guide reads clearly enough for site use. The genuine difference between a £20 meter and an £90 one is CAT rating, auto-ranging and whether it is true RMS — not how many digits the screen can show.

Non-contact voltage detection is a first check, not a diagnosis. Hovering an NCV sensor near a cable is a fast, useful screen for “is something live here,” but it can be fooled by nearby live cables or induced voltage on a dead one. Always confirm with the meter’s proper voltage function, and never treat NCV alone as proof a circuit is dead.

True RMS matters for anything that isn’t a clean sine wave. A modern LED driver, dimmer or variable-speed fan controller can make an averaging meter read a voltage that is simply wrong. For plain mains checks the difference is smaller, but true RMS is cheap enough now that there is little reason to buy without it.

A stated CAT rating is not the same as a certified one. A genuine CAT III or CAT IV marking is printed on the casing and stated plainly in the listing — if it is missing entirely, assume the meter is not rated for what it is being sold to do. On premium brands that rating is independently certified; on budget meters it is often closer to a printed claim than a tested one, which matters more for anything worked near a consumer unit than for a straightforward continuity check.

Temperature probes and capacitance testing are rarely worth paying extra for. They turn up on some mid-range meters as an added extra, and they are pleasant to have, but they are not the reason to choose one multimeter over another for the checks a survey actually calls for.

Questions I get asked

Is a multimeter safe to use to prove a circuit is dead before work starts?

Not on its own, by HSE guidance. Safe isolation calls for a dedicated two-pole voltage tester that meets GS38, proved on a known live source, used to test dead, then proved again afterwards — a process built around a tool with fixed, non-interchangeable probe sockets so it can’t accidentally be left on the wrong function. A multimeter’s shared sockets and rotary dial make it a better diagnostic tool than a safe-isolation one; use it to work out what’s wrong once a circuit is already off, not to decide that it is.

What’s the difference between a multimeter and a socket tester?

A socket tester is a fixed-format plug-in device that checks one 13A outlet’s wiring, and on the fuller models its earth loop and RCD, in a single go. A multimeter is a general-purpose instrument that reaches wherever a probe can go — an immersion heater’s terminals, a fan’s flex, a suspect junction box — and measures voltage, continuity and resistance rather than reading a fixed wiring pattern. Most survey kit bags carry both, for different moments.

Do I need true RMS for general survey work?

For a plain mains voltage or continuity check, an averaging meter and a true-RMS one usually agree closely enough that it makes little practical difference. The gap opens up around anything with a distorted waveform — a dimmer, an LED driver, a variable-speed controller — where an averaging meter can read a figure that is simply incorrect. True RMS costs little extra on most meters now, so there is not much reason to go without it.

What does CAT III mean, and is CAT IV worth paying for?

CAT ratings describe the transient voltage spikes a meter is built to withstand at different points in an electrical installation. CAT III covers fixed wiring and distribution circuits within a building, which is the baseline for anything a surveyor would be testing domestically. CAT IV extends that to the origin of the installation — the meter tails and supply side of the consumer unit — and is a genuine extra margin of protection rather than just a bigger number on the box, though CAT III 600V is adequate for the great majority of checks covered in this guide.

A multimeter earns its place for the same reason as most of the kit in this series — it turns “doesn’t seem to be working” into something specific enough to write down. On a retrofit assessment or an EPC, that is often the difference between a vague note about a heating control and a clear finding an installer can act on.
