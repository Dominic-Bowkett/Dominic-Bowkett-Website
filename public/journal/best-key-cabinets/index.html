A key safe holds one set of keys by a front door. A key cabinet is the other end of the problem: the wall-mounted box in an office, a cupboard or a hallway that holds the keys to every property you look after. Landlords with a handful of lets keep these in kitchen drawers until the day a contractor turns up and nobody can say which ring is which house. A cabinet with numbered hooks and an index fixes that for the price of a tank of diesel.

The purchase is mostly three decisions: how many hooks, how deep, and how it locks. Depth is the one people get wrong, because a shallow box built for bare keys will not close on a fob or a key ring. The lock is the next, and the honest position is that none of these is a safe. Most are pressed steel with a cylinder or cam lock, and they stop casual access, tidy a key register and give you a controlled place to hand keys out from. They do not stop a determined thief with a crowbar. That is why the key safe guide deals with certified boxes separately, and why a cabinet belongs in a locked room rather than on a hallway wall.

There is a compliance angle for landlords and agents. Keys to occupied homes are access to people’s belongings, so who can take them, and when, should be traceable. A cabinet with an index, and ideally different codes for different people, is the simple control that goes with the wider paperwork: the HMO licence cost, fire risk assessment cost, EICR cost and gas safety certificate cost guides cover the jobs that bring contractors to your door, and every one of those contractors needs a key. How I judge a key cabinet

How many hooks, and how deep? Count the keys you hold, add half again, then check the depth. Under about 100mm is bare keys on tags only. Fobs, alarm remotes and key rings want 140mm.

How does it lock? A cylinder key lock is simple and battery-free, but the cabinet key becomes a key to protect. A keypad lets you give codes to different people and change them. A mechanical combination does the same with no battery. Cam locks on the cheap boxes are tidiness, not security.

What is the steel like? Thin pressed steel opens with a screwdriver. Heavier commercial cabinets have thicker doors and better hinges. Published weights are a rough guide: a 20-key box under 1kg is not the same product as a 17kg 200-hook cabinet.

How does it fix to the wall? A cabinet is only as secure as its fixings and the wall behind them. Masonry with proper plugs, not plasterboard, and no screws visible from the front.

Can you keep a record? Numbered hooks, colour-coded tags and an index card are the point. A cabinet you cannot read is a drawer with a door.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Where it lives matters more than which one it is. A cabinet in a locked office or a locked cupboard is a key register. The same cabinet in a hallway is an invitation. Fix it low, out of sight from the street, behind a door.

Do not label keys with addresses. Use numbers and keep the index separate. A lost key with a full address on it is a burglary with directions.

A cabinet is not a fire-escape answer. Locks on the way out of a shared house are their own issue for the fire risk assessment, and nothing in this guide solves it. For site and van, see the padlock guide, and for the wider landlord checklist the first-time landlord kit.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. Large hook counts you will never fill, keypads with no stated battery arrangement, and “fireproof” claims on a pressed steel box with no test standard behind them.

Questions I get asked

What is the difference between a key cabinet and a key safe? A key safe is a small, often certified box for one set of keys, usually outside a property. A key cabinet is a larger box for many keys, normally kept indoors as a register. Certified security ratings are mostly found on key safes.

How many hooks do I need? Count every key you hold, including spares and meter-cupboard keys, then add half again. A landlord with ten lets can easily be holding forty keys.

Are electronic key cabinets better? They are better where several people need different access, because codes can be changed and tiered. For one person a key-locked cabinet is simpler and does not need batteries.

Can a key cabinet be fixed to a leasehold wall? In your own office or cupboard, yes. In a shared lobby or a leasehold common area, ask the freeholder first, since you are drilling the structure.

Key control is one of the quiet items in a rented portfolio, and it tends to surface when something goes wrong. A buy to let survey looks at the property itself, and getting in touch is the way to arrange one. For the rest of the kit, there are more field-kit guides.
