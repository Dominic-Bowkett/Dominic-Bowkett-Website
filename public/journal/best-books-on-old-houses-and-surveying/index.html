The best books
on old houses
and surveying

Most of what I diagnose on site has already been written down somewhere, usually more than once, by someone who has spent longer looking at old buildings than any one surveyor gets to in a career. Seven books below, from a first practical read for someone buying a period house through to the texts surveying students actually sit exams on.

None of what follows replaces a survey. A book can’t put a damp meter against your own skirting board, climb into your own loft, or tell you what’s specific to the actual house you’re buying or the one you’re training to report on. What a good one does instead is explain why a defect happens rather than just that it does, and hand you the vocabulary to ask a surveyor, a builder or a conservation officer a sharper question than “is this bad”.

The list below splits roughly in two. The first four are written for the person living in, or about to buy, an old house — plain English, care-and-repair focused, no exam in mind. The last three are the professional texts building surveyors are actually trained from: denser, pricier, and the honest next step for anyone who wants the diagnosis behind the advice rather than the advice on its own.

Seven books, current editions only, what each one is actually for and who it suits.

The one I’d start with

Old House Handbook — Roger Hunt & Marianne Suhr

Produced in association with the Society for the Protection of Ancient Buildings, this is the closest thing the market has to a single reference for anyone who owns or is buying a pre-1919 house and wants to understand it rather than just decorate it. It works material by material — solid walls, lime render, timber windows, roofs — and the argument running through every chapter holds up: repair with breathable, like-for-like materials rather than a modern sealed fix bolted onto a wall built to let moisture out, not trap it in. Now in its second edition.

Typically £22–£30 (hardback)

How I judge a book on old houses or surveying

Written for the reader it claims to be for. A book pitched at a first-time period-house buyer that’s actually a surveying-exam text in disguise, or the reverse, wastes money on both sides — check the level before the cover.

A current edition, not a bargain-bin old one. Building Regulations, damp-proofing standards and even the vocabulary a surveyor uses date fast; a secondhand third edition from a decade ago can quietly repeat guidance that’s since been superseded.

Diagnosis over decoration. The useful books explain why a defect happens — why a solid wall needs to breathe, why a Victorian bay window fails at the cill first — rather than reading as a style guide with a repair chapter bolted on.

A repair philosophy that fits how the house was actually built. Older housing stock is solid-walled, lime-based and vapour-permeable; a book written with modern cavity-wall assumptions in mind gives advice that can trap damp in, not out.

Real authorship. SPAB involvement, RICS-adjacent authors, or a title that’s run to a fourth or ninth edition and is still the set text on a surveying course — all signals the content has been tested against real practice, not just written once and left alone.

The picks in detail

01

Old House Handbook — Roger Hunt & Marianne Suhr

Best overall — the one I’d start with; my top pick above

SPAB’s conservation-first repair philosophy runs through every chapter of this one: fix a solid wall, a timber window or a lime-rendered elevation with a breathable, like-for-like material, not the sealed modern equivalent that traps in moisture a Victorian or Georgian wall was built to release. It’s organised by building element rather than by room, which makes it genuinely useful to dip into once a specific defect is found rather than only read cover to cover before moving in. The second edition brings the materials and suppliers referenced up to date.

For

  • SPAB involvement gives the repair advice real conservation credibility
  • Organised by building element, easy to use as a reference once you’re in

Against

  • Hardback only — no cheaper paperback edition currently listed
  • Assumes a genuinely old house; less relevant to 1930s-onward stock

Typically £22–£30 (hardback)

Check price on Amazon
02

Period Property Manual — Ian Rock

Cheapest general entry point — a punchier read than the Handbook above

Haynes built its name on car manuals, and the same step-by-step, heavily illustrated format is applied here to housing stock running from the 1300s through to the early 1900s — a broader date range than the Victorian-specific title below. It reads faster than the SPAB-backed Handbook, trading some conservation depth for a punchier, more DIY-adjacent tone that suits someone wanting a practical overview before a purchase rather than a reference to keep returning to. The cheapest of the four homeowner-tier books here.

For

  • Covers the widest date range of any book in this guide
  • Cheapest homeowner-tier title, genuinely useful content for the price

Against

  • Less conservation depth than the SPAB-endorsed Hunt & Suhr titles
  • Broad range means less depth on any one house type

Typically £15–£20 (hardback)

Check price on Amazon
03

The Victorian House Manual — Ian Rock & Ian R MacMillan

Best for Victorian and Edwardian terraces specifically

The huge majority of Britain’s older housing stock is Victorian or Edwardian terrace, and this manual narrows in on exactly that: solid brick party walls, suspended timber ground floors, slate roofs, sash windows, and the specific ways each one fails. It sits alongside the broader Period Property Manual above rather than replacing it — buy this one instead if the house in question is a terrace or semi from roughly 1837 to 1901 and the wider date range of the other title isn’t needed.

For

  • Sharp focus on the single most common older house type in the UK
  • Technical data tables for Victorian and Edwardian construction specifically

Against

  • Narrower scope than the Period Property Manual if your house predates 1837
  • Overlaps significantly with the Period Property Manual — buy one, not both, unless the house is genuinely Victorian

Typically £19–£25 (hardback)

Check price on Amazon
04

Old House Eco Handbook — Marianne Suhr & Roger Hunt

Best for retrofit and energy upgrades without wrecking old fabric

The companion volume to the Old House Handbook above, aimed squarely at the question I get asked most often on a retrofit assessment: how do you make an old house warmer without doing something to it that a modern cavity-wall assumption would suggest, and a solid wall can’t tolerate. Internal wall insulation, breathable membranes, secondary glazing versus double glazing in a listed or conservation-area context, and where solar and heat pumps genuinely fit an old building — all covered with the same repair-first philosophy as its sister title. Foreword by Kevin McCloud, same as the Handbook.

For

  • The only book here written specifically around retrofit and energy upgrades
  • Consistent breathable-materials philosophy with the main Old House Handbook

Against

  • Best read as a second book alongside the Handbook, not instead of it
  • Some overlap with a PAS 2035 retrofit assessment’s own scope

Typically £18–£24 (hardback)

Check price on Amazon
05

Building Surveys (9th edition) — Peter Glover

Best for survey methodology and report writing — the professional-tier starting point

First published in 1983 and now in its ninth edition, this is the standard reference for how a building survey is actually planned, carried out and reported — not what a defect looks like, but the process around inspecting a building systematically and writing it up in a way that stands up if it’s ever challenged. It’s the natural first professional-tier book for anyone training toward chartered surveying status, or a homeowner who wants to understand what a HomeBuyer or Building Survey actually involves before commissioning one.

For

  • Long print run through nine editions — genuinely tested against real practice
  • Covers the survey process itself, not just individual defects

Against

  • Assumes some surveying vocabulary — not a first read for a general homeowner
  • Costlier than any of the four homeowner-tier titles above

Typically £45–£55 (paperback)

06

Understanding Housing Defects (4th edition) — Marshall, Worthing, Heath & Dann

Best element-by-element defects reference

Where Glover’s book above covers the survey process, this one is the defects themselves: foundations, walls, roofs, floors and services, each broken down by cause, how it presents, and what the remedy actually involves, in the systematic style RICS-adjacent training expects. It’s the reference I’d point a trainee surveyor to for the “why does this happen” question behind almost anything flagged on a report, and it pairs naturally with Glover’s survey-methodology text rather than duplicating it.

For

  • Systematic, element-by-element structure makes it genuinely useful as a reference
  • Now in its fourth edition — content kept current across multiple updates

Against

  • Priciest homeowner-adjacent title in this guide
  • Dense technical writing, not aimed at a general reader

Building Pathology: Principles and Practice (3rd edition) — David S. Watt

Best for the building science behind the diagnosis — the deepest of the seven

The most academic book on this list, and deliberately so: rather than listing defects and remedies, it works through the underlying building science — moisture movement, decay mechanisms, structural behaviour, prognosis — that the other two professional-tier books above assume the reader already has some grip on. It’s the one I’d recommend last, once Glover and Marshall’s books have covered the practical ground, for anyone who wants to understand the physics behind why a defect develops the way it does rather than just how to spot and report it.

For

  • The most technical read of the seven — not a first book for anyone

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Edition number matters more than cover design. Regulations, materials and even accepted repair philosophy move on between editions — a cheap secondhand copy of an old edition can quietly repeat guidance that’s since been superseded. Check the edition before the price.

Hardback versus paperback is a durability choice, not a content one. The Hunt & Suhr and Haynes titles are hardback reference books meant to survive being flicked through repeatedly during an actual renovation; the professional textbooks are paperback because students carry and annotate them. Neither format implies better content.

A surveying textbook still assumes some technical vocabulary. Anyone wanting a plain-English read about their own house should start with the homeowner-tier four, not jump straight to Glover or Marshall expecting an easy read — the professional texts are written for people already partway through training.

These are diagnosis books, not weekend-DIY manuals. The professional tier explains why a defect happens and how a surveyor assesses it, not step-by-step instructions for fixing your own damp problem this weekend — that practical repair detail lives in the homeowner-tier titles instead.

None of this replaces an actual survey of your actual house. A book can tell you what a bowing solid wall generally means; only a surveyor standing in front of your specific wall can tell you what it means for that one.

Questions I get asked

Do I need to buy all seven, or just one? Just one, to start. The Old House Handbook covers most of what a period-house buyer needs; only add the Eco Handbook if a retrofit or energy upgrade is actually planned, and only reach for the professional tier if you want the diagnosis behind the advice, not just the advice itself.

I’m buying a 1930s semi, not a genuinely old house — is any of this relevant? The Period Property Manual is the most useful of the four homeowner-tier books for anything built up to the early 1900s; a 1930s semi sits just past that range but still shares enough construction methods (solid or early cavity walls, suspended timber floors) that Marshall’s defects reference below is more relevant to it than the Victorian-specific title.

Are these books a substitute for getting a survey done? No. A book gives you vocabulary and a general understanding of how old buildings behave; a survey tells you what’s actually happening in the specific house you’re buying. Read one of these before a survey if anything, not instead of one.

I’m training toward chartered surveyor status — where do I actually start? Glover’s Building Surveys for the survey process itself, then Marshall, Worthing, Heath and Dann’s Understanding Housing Defects for the element-by-element diagnosis detail. Watt’s Building Pathology is worth adding once those two are familiar, not before.

A book explains how old houses generally behave; a survey tells you what’s happening in the one you’re actually buying.
