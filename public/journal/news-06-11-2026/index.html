Surveyor’s notes
6 November 2026:
EPC fines, Budget tax

A £30,000 EPC penalty is on the table, the Budget has raised tax on rental profit, arrears are climbing and house prices have gone flat. Here is what each means on the ground.

Published 6 November 2026 Reading time · 6 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

This week’s theme is cost. Government has put a number on what an EPC breach will cost a landlord, the Budget has raised what rental profit costs to keep, and the house price and arrears data say the people paying are already stretched.

The EPC C deadline now has a £30,000 stick behind it

GOV.UK’s Energy Independence Bill factsheet on rental minimum standards, published 4 November, says the measure “will introduce a new maximum civil penalty of £30,000” for breaches of the 2015 Regulations and gives local authorities enhanced powers. It repeats that the standard rises to EPC C from 1 October 2030, and that a rented home must meet it or have a valid exemption registered. The £10,000 maximum required investment per property comes from the January 2026 consultation response. Letting Agent Today, relying on Propertymark’s analysis, says the Bill was introduced to Parliament this week.

What nobody has answered is whether £30,000 is per property, per breach or per landlord. Property118 says the same: it is not yet clear. The factsheet does not settle it, and I will not guess. It is also a Bill, so the detail can still change.

My read is that the real change is behavioural. Today a landlord with an F-rated flat can sit on it because enforcement is patchy. A councils-with-teeth regime changes that, and councils will start with the easy targets, the properties whose EPC is already on the public register. Four years sounds generous, but a heat pump or fabric upgrade in a solid-wall house takes months to scope, fund and install.

Who should do what: if you let property, pull your current EPC and read the recommendations list. If you are at D or below, get a retrofit-minded assessment this winter rather than in 2029, when every assessor and installer will be booked.

The Budget took another two points off rental profit

Landlord Today reports that tax on property income rises to 24%, 44% and 49% from April 2027, on top of the rise announced last year. Modelling by The Lettings Partnership, for a £250,000 property let at £1,406 a month, puts the extra basic-rate tax at £484 a year. The article rounds that to “almost £500”, and it is a modelled example, not a typical bill.

Read it beside the EPC story. A landlord facing a spending cap of up to £10,000 per property is also being told that each pound of rent keeps less after tax. I think that pushes small, marginal landlords with one or two old terraced houses toward selling rather than upgrading. That is a bet, not a certainty.

One detail worth checking: Meacher-Jones’s Budget summary says the 0% VAT rate on energy-saving materials ends as planned on 31 March 2027, with 5% applying from 1 April 2027. I have not checked that against the Budget documents, so confirm it with your accountant or installer before relying on it. If it holds, there is a small incentive to get insulation and heat pump work invoiced before that date.

Rent arrears are the part of the squeeze tenants feel first

Reposit’s data, reported by Property118, shows the share of tenants in arrears rising from 12% to 18% year on year. It puts the average across the first three quarters of 2026 at 18%, against 15% in the same period of 2025 and 2024, with average arrears of £1,837. The same piece cites Ministry of Justice figures of 23,635 landlord possession claims in Q2 2026, up 6%, and ONS average private rent of £1,406 a month, up 3.8%. The headline says “record highs”, but the article text says the highest level in three years, and it does not link Reposit’s report. Treat it as one provider’s dataset.

I see this as the practical end of everything above. Tenants are paying more, landlords are keeping less, and the Renters’ Rights Act has taken away the quick no-fault exit. A landlord who has let a property fall into disrepair is the one most exposed when a dispute reaches a council or court. A dated condition record, a current EPC and gas and electrical certificates are what protect you.

Nationwide says prices are flat, and buyers are still cautious

Nationwide’s October index shows annual growth slowing to 0.5%, the slowest since January 2024, with prices unchanged month on month and an average price of £273,566. Chief Economist Robert Gardner says “Surveyors report that new buyer enquiries remain weak”, and links the slowdown to higher energy prices and expectations of higher Bank Rate. He also expects the market to regain momentum once the energy shock fades.

This is a national figure and says little about any one South East street. What it does tell me is that sellers have less room. In a flat market a buyer with a survey in hand has more leverage on any defect.

Sellers: get ahead of the survey. Fix visible defects, find out what an assessor will find, and have the EPC, gas and electrical papers ready. Buyers: do not use a flat market as a reason to skip the survey. It is the reason to commission one, because the findings are now genuine negotiating leverage.

What I’d actually do

  1. Landlords: pull your EPC now and, if you are D or below, book a retrofit-minded assessment this winter rather than in 2029.
  2. Landlords with one or two old houses: work out your post-April 2027 after-tax yield before committing to a large upgrade, then decide whether to improve or sell.
  3. Anyone planning insulation or heat pump work: ask your installer about the 31 March 2027 VAT date, and confirm it with your accountant.
  4. Landlords: keep a dated condition record, EPC, gas and electrical certificates for every property.
  5. Buyers and sellers: commission the survey early and use it, whichever side you are on.
