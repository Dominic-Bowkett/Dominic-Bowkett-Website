Checking the smoke alarms is a five-minute job on almost every visit I make to a let property, and it tells me more about a landlord than the boiler does. A single battery alarm on the landing, bought once in 2016 and never touched since, says one thing. A matched set that all sound together when I press the test button on the one furthest from the door says another. Most of the landlords I meet assume the first is already against the law and the second is a nice-to-have. It is closer to the opposite.

Here is the part that catches people out: the Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Alarm (Amendment) Regulations 2022 did not introduce a duty to fit a smoke alarm on every storey — that has applied to private landlords since the original 2015 regulations. What 2022 actually changed was the CO duty (extended from solid-fuel appliances only to any fixed combustion appliance) and who it applies to (social landlords too, not just private ones). Neither instrument requires alarms to be interlinked, or a heat alarm in the kitchen. That is Scotland’s requirement, under the tolerable standard that has applied to every home there, regardless of tenure, since February 2022.

None of which means an English landlord should stop at the bare minimum. BS 5839-6:2019, the code of practice the industry designs to, recommends an interlinked system as standard for a rented home — and if the property is a licensed HMO, your council’s licence conditions have very likely already made that compulsory, whatever the national Alarm Regulations say. This guide covers both routes in: wireless retrofit kits needing no electrician, a hard-wired option for a rewire or new build, and the heat alarms that belong in the kitchen alone.

How I judge a smoke or heat alarm

The Kitemark, not the marketing. Smoke alarms carry BS EN 14604:2005; heat alarms carry BS 5446-2:2003 — standards specifying real test conditions rather than self-declared claims. A BSI Kitemark means the certification was independently audited. Every alarm below carries one or the other; a lot of what surrounds them in a search results page does not.

Which interlink ecosystem, because you can’t mix them. Wireless interlink is proprietary per manufacturer — RadioLINK+, Wi-Safe 2 and X-Sense’s own protocol don’t talk to each other. Pick one system for a property and stay in it. Hard-wired interlink sidesteps the brand question but wants an electrician and, ideally, a property being rewired anyway.

A sealed 10-year battery. The most common failure I see is not a dead sensor, it’s a missing battery — borrowed for a remote control, or pulled when the low-battery chirp got annoying at 2am. A sealed-for-life lithium cell closes that off completely, which for a tenancy that outlives the landlord’s last visit is the difference between compliance on paper and compliance in reality.

The right sensor in the right room. A standard smoke alarm in a kitchen is a nuisance-alarm generator — steam and cooking fumes will set it off until someone disables it. A heat alarm, triggered by a genuine temperature rise rather than particles in the air, belongs there instead. Optical (photoelectric) smoke sensors, used throughout below, are also the sensible default over old ionisation types — faster on the smouldering fires that actually start in furniture and bedding, nothing radioactive inside.

Data, for the landlord who isn’t on site. AudioLINK+ and Wi-Safe 2’s optional gateway both turn an alarm into a record of when it last sounded and whether it’s still within its 10-year life. None of that is a legal requirement; it’s useful if the property is twenty minutes away and the tenant isn’t who you’ll hear it from first.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

England’s law and Scotland’s law are not the same law. England requires at least one smoke alarm per storey (a duty dating to 2015) and a CO alarm in any room with a fixed combustion appliance other than a gas cooker (the genuine 2022 change, alongside extending the duty to social housing) — not interlink, not a heat alarm. Scotland’s tolerable standard has required interlinked smoke alarms in the main living room and every circulation space, an interlinked heat alarm in the kitchen, and an interlinked CO alarm by any fuel-burning appliance, in every home regardless of tenure, since 1 February 2022. Let in both countries, and building to the Scottish spec everywhere means you never fall short of the English one.

Your HMO licence has probably already decided this for you. Councils issuing HMO licences under the Housing Act 2004 typically specify a fire detection standard against BS 5839-6 — commonly Grade D1 category LD2 for a smaller shared house (interlinked, sealed-battery or mains, covering escape routes and the kitchen), stepping up to a Grade A panel system for larger HMOs of three-plus storeys or five-plus occupants. Check your licence schedule rather than the national Alarm Regulations if the property is an HMO; the schedule is very likely the stricter document.

The test button proves less than it feels like it proves. Pressing it confirms the sounder, the electronics and, on an interlinked set, that the radio signal reaches the other units — not that the sensing chamber is still as sharp as the day it was fitted. Optical chambers slowly accumulate dust over a decade, which is why every alarm here carries a stamped end-of-life date rather than an indefinite one. Test monthly, and specifically after any period a property has stood empty.

What the listings push that doesn’t matter here. Multi-sensor combination alarms are convenient but check what they replace — a smoke-plus-heat unit in a hallway still isn’t a kitchen-specific heat alarm if the kitchen is a separate room. App connectivity is genuinely useful for a landlord who isn’t on site and pure theatre for one who is. An unbranded ten-pack with no Kitemark and a suspiciously low price isn’t a bargain; it’s ten units you’ll replace the day an assessor or insurer asks for the certification.

Questions I get asked

Do landlords in England have to fit interlinked smoke alarms? Not under the national Alarm Regulations — those require one smoke alarm per storey and CO alarms by combustion appliances, full stop. Interlink is a BS 5839-6 best-practice recommendation that most letting agents, lenders and insurers now expect, and it’s very often a mandatory HMO licence condition. Scotland is the one place in the UK where interlink is a straightforward legal requirement for every home.

Where does a heat alarm go instead of a smoke alarm? In the kitchen, and generally nowhere else. Cooking produces enough steam and airborne particulate to trip a photoelectric sensor regularly enough that people start disabling alarms — a heat alarm, responding only to a genuine temperature spike, doesn’t have that problem. Smoke alarms then cover hallways, landings and the main living room, the split every pairing above is built around.

Can I mix smoke alarm brands on one interlinked system? Not the wireless kind. RadioLINK+, Wi-Safe 2 and X-Sense’s own protocol are proprietary and don’t recognise each other’s signal. Pick one brand’s wireless ecosystem per property and buy every alarm in it from that range; the only reliable way to mix brands is a fully hard-wired system, where the cable carries the interlink signal instead of a radio protocol.

How long do smoke and heat alarms actually last? Ten years for a sealed-battery unit, matched to the battery’s own life; a replaceable-battery alarm is usually rated five to seven years on the sensing chamber regardless of how often the battery gets changed. Every alarm here prints a replace-by date on the casing — write the fitting date on it in marker too, because nobody remembers a decade later.
