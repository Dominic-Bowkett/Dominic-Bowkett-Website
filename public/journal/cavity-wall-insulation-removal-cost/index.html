Cavity wall
insulation
removal cost
in 2026

Extracting failed cavity wall insulation typically costs £1,500–£4,000 for an ordinary 3-bed semi in 2026, at roughly £20–£45 per square metre of wall. But before you pay any of that, it's worth checking whether a 25-year guarantee means someone else pays for it instead.

Published 30 Sep 2026 Reading time · 9 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Cavity wall insulation removal — extraction, in trade terms — is what happens when the fill that was meant to cut a heating bill has instead started causing damp, cold bridging, or corrosion in the wall ties holding the two leaves of brickwork together. In 2026, having it professionally extracted typically costs £1,500–£4,000 for a 3-bed semi-detached house, worked out at roughly £20–£45 per square metre of wall. A terrace or small semi usually comes in lower, from around £1,200; a large or hard-to-access detached property can run to £7,000 or more, particularly where the original fill was urea formaldehyde foam rather than mineral wool or bonded beads.

What that headline figure doesn't tell you is whether you should be the one paying it. Cavity wall insulation fitted by a registered installer since the mid-1990s very often carries a 25-year guarantee from the Cavity Insulation Guarantee Agency (CIGA), and where CIGA confirms the insulation itself has genuinely failed, extraction and reinstatement can be covered under that guarantee rather than coming out of your pocket. Whether that route is open to you is usually the first question worth answering — not the last, after a contractor's already been paid.

I diagnose buildings for a living; I don't extract or install cavity fill, so I've no stake in whether a job goes ahead or which route you take. This is a straight read on 2026 removal pricing, the CIGA claim route a surprising number of homeowners don't know exists, and how to tell whether what you're looking at is failed insulation at all, or something else being blamed on it.

Urea formaldehyde (UF) foam, common in cavity fills from the 1970s and 80s, is by some distance the hardest material to get out — it has to be broken down and vacuumed out in sections rather than simply blown clear, and extraction rates of only 60–80% of the material aren't unusual. That typically adds £500–£1,500 to a job compared with mineral wool or bonded bead fill, which come out more easily, and it's the single biggest swing factor between two quotes that otherwise look like they're pricing the same wall.

What drives the price

Wall area, not floor area. You're paying to empty a void behind the brickwork, so the number of external elevations and their height matters more than the size of the rooms inside — the same logic that applies when the insulation goes in, which I've covered in full in cavity wall insulation cost.

Material. Mineral wool is the cheapest and most straightforward to extract; EPS bonded beads come out with relative ease too. Urea formaldehyde foam is the outlier — slower, messier, and priced accordingly, as above.

Access and condition. Scaffolding, render or cladding that has to come off first, and a cavity partly blocked with old mortar droppings or debris, all add time and cost, exactly as they do for an installation.

What's bundled in. Replacement insulation (if you're refitting with a more suitable material), remedial repairs, and redecoration are usually priced as separate line items rather than folded into the extraction fee. Where corroded wall ties turn out to be part of the problem — common on houses built roughly 1920 to 1980 — that's a different job again, and one I've written about separately in wall tie replacement cost.

Region. Installers and extraction specialists in the South East, Surrey and London carry higher overheads, which shows up in the quote the same way it does for any trade in this series.

Do you actually need it removed?

This is the question worth sitting with before anyone drills a hole in the wall to start emptying it. Genuine cavity insulation failure tends to show a recognisable pattern: damp patches or staining on internal walls that appeared some time after the fill went in, usually worse on the more exposed elevations after a spell of wind-driven rain; patchy, irregular cold spots on a thermal image rather than an even chill across the whole wall; and, on older houses, wall ties that have corroded from sitting against consistently wet fill — something that only shows up if someone actually opens one up and looks, rather than assumes.

But a good share of what gets blamed on cavity insulation isn't the insulation at all. I've covered the mechanics in cavity wall insulation cost: a bridged damp-proof course, blocked guttering, failed pointing, or ground levels raised too close to the damp course can all produce damp that looks identical to a failed cavity fill, and none of them are fixed by taking the insulation out. Pulling it out of a wall that was never the actual cause solves nothing and leaves the house both damp and less insulated than before.

The only reliable way to tell the difference is to look inside the cavity, not just at the symptoms on the plaster. A borescope through a small drilled hole shows whether the fill is wet, slumped, or in reasonable order, and an independent damp survey — priced separately in damp survey cost — rules the other causes in or out before anyone commits to a £1,500-plus job. If the firm quoting the removal is the same one that then quotes the replastering and a fresh round of insulation, get a second opinion first.

The CIGA guarantee route

This is the section most cost guides for this job skip, and the one most likely to change what you actually pay. The Cavity Insulation Guarantee Agency was set up in 1995 and provides an independent 25-year guarantee on cavity wall insulation fitted by CIGA-registered installers across the UK and the Channel Islands. It's transferable to a new owner at no charge, which matters if you've bought a house with cavity fill already in place — the original installer's paperwork, or a CIGA search, tells you whether cover still exists.

Where CIGA's independent inspection confirms a genuine defect — failure of the insulation material itself, or a fault in how it was installed — remedial work, which can include extraction and reinstatement, is carried out at no cost to you, up to a cap of £20,000. On an ordinary semi that's several times the typical extraction cost quoted above, so a confirmed claim can genuinely mean a £0 bill rather than the £1,500–£4,000 you'd otherwise be quoted.

The order of operations matters more than almost anything else here. The guarantee is void if the insulation has already been altered or disturbed, so having a firm remove it privately before CIGA has assessed the claim can void the cover you were trying to use. The right sequence: notice the problem, check whether the property is CIGA-registered (your paperwork, the original installer, or CIGA on 01525 853300), submit a claim and let CIGA arrange an independent inspection, then act on the outcome — not the other way round.

It doesn't cover everything. Pre-existing structural defects, damp from a source unrelated to the cavity fill — rising damp, a roof leak, a plumbing fault — and installations that were never CIGA-registered fall outside the guarantee, and you'd be back to the market rates above. Some manufacturers run their own guarantee schemes instead of CIGA, so check whose name is actually on the certificate.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • The CIGA question asked before the quote. A reputable extraction firm should ask whether your installation is CIGA-registered before pricing the job, not skip straight to a number — there's no commercial reason for them to raise it unprompted, which is exactly why it's worth asking yourself.
  • A borescope check, not a doorstep guess. The cavity should be looked into before a full-extraction price is given, so you know what's actually in there.
  • Per-square-metre pricing broken down, so a localised or partial extraction on one affected run isn't quoted as if it were a whole-house job.
  • Replacement insulation and remedial work itemised separately — wall tie replacement, repointing and redecoration are different jobs with different price tags, not one bundled figure.
  • Waste disposal covered explicitly, particularly for urea formaldehyde foam, which is handled as controlled waste.
  • No pressure to sign on the day, especially from a firm that also did the original installation.

Questions I get asked

How much does cavity wall insulation removal cost? In 2026, budget roughly £1,200–£3,000 for a terrace or small semi, £1,500–£4,000 for a typical 3-bed semi, and £3,500–£7,000 or more for a large detached or hard-to-access property. Most quotes work out at £20–£45 per square metre, and urea formaldehyde foam typically adds £500–£1,500 over mineral wool or bead fill.

Is cavity wall insulation removal covered by a guarantee? It can be. Insulation fitted by a CIGA-registered installer since 1995 carries a 25-year, transferable guarantee, and a confirmed defect means rectification — including extraction and reinstatement — is covered up to £20,000, at no cost to you. The guarantee is void if the insulation's already been disturbed, so the claim has to come before any private removal work, not after.

How do I know if my cavity wall insulation has failed? Typical signs are damp patches on internal walls, worse after wind-driven rain, patchy cold spots on a thermal image, and, on older houses, corroding wall ties sitting against wet fill. A lot of what gets blamed on cavity insulation is actually a separate defect — a bridged damp course, blocked gutters or failed pointing — so get it properly diagnosed, ideally with a borescope look into the cavity itself, before accepting that the fill is the cause.

Can I have just part of the cavity emptied rather than the whole wall? Yes, localised extraction of the affected run is common and usually priced per square metre of the area cleared, so it costs less than a full-house job. The caveat is that a failure in one spot can point to a wider problem across the whole installation, so it's worth asking whoever surveys the job whether the rest of the cavity is worth checking before it's closed back up.
