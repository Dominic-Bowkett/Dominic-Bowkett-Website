I wrote the head torch guide first, and for a lot of the job it’s the right tool — hands free in a loft, both palms on a ladder in a sub-floor void. A handheld torch does something a head torch can’t: throw a tight, deliberate beam at one specific thing from a distance, without your whole head pointing at it. That’s a chimney stack at the far end of a garden in December dusk, a drain run lit down rather than into, or handing someone a torch and saying “look at that stain, there” instead of asking them to stand where you are.

Rechargeable earns its keep over AA or AAA disposables the way it does anywhere else — no dead batteries in the door pocket of the van, no garage stop before a 7am start. The trade-off is the one thing disposables never ask of you: knowing the charge state before you leave the house. Every torch below shows a battery indicator or signals clearly through its output when it’s getting low, which is the minimum I’d accept for something working on a stranger’s property.

The torch market is also where lumens claims get genuinely silly — a handful of budget listings on Amazon quote outputs in the hundreds of thousands, not physically plausible from a battery the size of a AA cell. Olight, Fenix, Nitecore and Ledlenser sit at the credible end, output tested to the ANSI/PLATO FL1 standard and checked by the flashlight-review community rather than just printed on the box. That’s where most of this guide sits, with two cheaper picks included and flagged as exactly that.

How I judge a rechargeable torch

Real lumens, not marketing lumens. A genuine 1,000–1,700 lumens from a known brand is properly bright; a no-name listing claiming tens of thousands from the same size battery is printing a number, not measuring one. Beam distance in metres is the more honest figure, since it can’t be inflated without the physics showing up in a review within weeks.

Throw against flood. A tight reflector throws light a long way in a narrow beam — useful for a chimney stack or a roofline you’re not climbing to. A wide, floody beam lights a whole meter cupboard evenly but doesn’t reach far. An adjustable-focus head gives you both, at the cost of one more moving part to fail.

Battery and charging, together. A removable 21700 or 18650 cell means carrying a charged spare; a sealed battery means a smaller, simpler torch that eventually needs replacing whole. USB-C direct into the torch is the standard worth having — the same cable as your phone. A magnetic charging cable works well but is another proprietary lead to lose from a bag.

Waterproof rating that matches the job. IPX8 or IP68 shrugs off a wet crawl space or a puddle outside a downpipe; IPX6 handles rain and splashing but not submersion.

Runtime on the mode you’ll actually use. Every torch here quotes an eco-mode runtime in tens or hundreds of hours, which is true and almost irrelevant. The number that matters is runtime on a genuinely useful mid brightness, and that’s the figure I’ve led with below.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Lumens on the box, sanity-checked. Above about 2,000–3,000 lumens from a pocket-sized torch, you’re into genuinely bright territory that a known brand’s reputation is staked on being accurate. Below a recognisable brand, treat any implausible number as exactly that, and look instead at beam-distance figures, which are harder to fake convincingly.

Removable batteries matter more than peak brightness. A sealed cell means a smaller, simpler torch, but one battery, full stop. A torch that takes a 21700 or 18650 cell lets you carry a charged spare for the day a socket isn’t nearby — the biggest practical difference between the premium picks here and the pocket-EDC ones.

Zoom and adjustable focus are genuinely useful, not a gimmick. A twist- or slide-adjustable head that goes from spot to flood earns its keep, where one minute you’re lighting a meter cupboard and the next picking out a crack forty feet up a gable end. The trade-off is one more moving part and seal to fail over years of use.

Strobe and SOS modes are for someone else’s job. Every torch here has them; none matter for survey work. Don’t pay a premium for a longer list of tactical modes — what matters is a genuinely bright turbo, a sensible mid brightness for most of the day, and a low mode that won’t wreck your night vision reading a meter dial.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a torch as well as a head torch? For most survey work, yes, and they do different jobs. A head torch lights wherever you look, hands free, for a loft or a suspended floor. A handheld torch throws a directed beam a long way, for checking a roofline from the garden or showing a client exactly what you’re pointing at without them standing where you are. See the head torch guide for the hands-free half of the kit.

Does a higher lumens figure always mean a brighter, more useful torch? No. Beam angle matters as much as raw output — a tightly focused beam throws further at the same lumens than a wide flood does. The honest comparison point is beam distance in metres for throw, and lumens only within torches of a similar beam type.

Does USB-C versus a proprietary charging cable actually matter? In practice, yes, once you own more than one rechargeable device. A torch with USB-C built in charges from the same cable as your phone, one less specific lead to go missing from a bag. A magnetic charging cable works well but is genuinely proprietary — lose it and that torch is dead weight until a replacement arrives.

How long does a rechargeable torch hold its charge sitting unused in the van? Lithium-ion cells self-discharge slowly — most will hold a usable charge for several weeks to a couple of months of storage, though output may have dropped noticeably by the far end of that. For a torch kept purely as an emergency spare, a top-up charge every month or so beats trusting it’s still full from six months ago.

A torch tells you what’s there; it doesn’t tell you what to do about it.
