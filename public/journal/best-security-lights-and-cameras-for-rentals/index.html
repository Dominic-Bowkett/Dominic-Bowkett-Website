and cameras for rentals

Outdoor lighting and cameras come up on most buy-to-let surveys, usually because someone has bolted a floodlight to a gable end at an angle that lights the neighbour’s bedroom. The brief is rarely written down. The landlord wants the back door lit, the bins and bikes left alone, and fewer calls about a dark path. The tenant wants to feel safe and not be watched. Those two aims overlap more than you would think, and they part company the moment a camera goes up.

So this guide splits the job in two. Lighting first: a plain PIR floodlight is among the cheapest security upgrades on a rented house, it has no data-protection side, and it needs no app. Cameras and video doorbells second: useful in the right place, but with duties attached that a light does not have. A landlord who treats a camera as just another gadget is the one who reads the ICO’s guidance for the first time after a complaint.

Below are seven products across both jobs: three lights, two doorbells and two cameras. They come from manufacturers’ specifications and published user feedback, not from my own testing. How I judge security kit for a rented house

Does it record people? This comes before brightness or battery life. A light does not. A camera or doorbell does, and the moment it films an identifiable person it creates data you are responsible for. If the answer is yes, ask whether a light would do the same job.

Who owns the account? A smart doorbell or camera belongs to the app account behind it. If the landlord owns the account, the landlord sees the footage, and so can anyone they share the login with. If the tenant owns it, it leaves with them. Decide that before you buy and write it into the tenancy paperwork, not after.

Where does the field of view end? A camera that takes in a public pavement, a neighbour’s garden or a shared hallway is a different question from one that only sees your own doorstep. The ICO’s guidance on domestic CCTV is the place to start, and for a landlord running a business the rules are stricter, not looser.

What does it cost to keep? Cloud plans, batteries and replacement units add up across several properties. A long-life LED lamp with a sensor can run for years on nothing, while a battery camera needs attention every few months. Price the upkeep, not just the box.

Is it safe to fit? Mains lights are electrical work. Outdoor circuits and new circuits fall under Part P of the Building Regulations, and a landlord has a duty to keep the electrical installation safe, so it is a job for a registered electrician, not a Saturday afternoon.

Solar PIR security light

Cheapest

Self-contained solar lights with a PIR sensor are sold under many names, so this is a category search rather than a single model, and there is no brand here I would recommend with confidence. They suit a side passage, bike store or shed where running a cable is not worth the money. Treat them as a deterrent and a courtesy light, not a lighting scheme: output is modest, performance drops in a dull British winter, and the cells age. Look for a panel that can sit apart from the lamp so it can face the sky.

For

  • Cheap enough to replace when it fails
  • No data-protection issues

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Light the door, not the garden. Most trips on a dark path and most opportunist break-ins happen at the back and side of a house. One sensor-controlled light on each external door does more than a camera on the front, and it does it without a legal footprint. Set a short timer and a low sensitivity, and check the beam at night.

Cameras belong in the landlord’s areas. A communal entrance, a shared bin store or a car park is a reasonable place, with a sign and a clear reason. A camera inside a tenant’s room, or pointed at their window or private garden, is not. Whatever you fit, tell tenants in writing before it goes live, say who can view the footage and how long it is kept, and be ready for a subject access request.

Check the tenancy and the lease. In a let property the tenant has exclusive possession of their part of it. Fitting a doorbell to the front door of a single let, or a camera on a flat’s own balcony, may need agreement first. In a block, the freeholder or management company may have rules too. Do not assume the front of the building is yours to change.

Escape routes need proper lighting. A PIR security light outside is not emergency lighting and does not replace it inside. If you have an HMO or a block with shared escape routes, see the emergency lighting guide.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. Eye-watering lumen figures, 2K and 4K video that nobody will watch, and AI person detection that adds little to a fixed light. The things to compare are the field of view, what the sensor does at night, where the footage goes and what it costs after year one. A key safe or a decent lock is often a better rental upgrade than a camera: see the key safe guide and the code locks guide, and the wider landlord list in the first-time landlord kit.

Questions I get asked

Can a landlord install a camera at a rented property? In the landlord’s own communal areas, usually yes, with a clear purpose, signage and a data-protection approach. Inside the let part of the home, or in a way that watches the tenant, generally no. If the tenancy gives exclusive possession, get the tenant’s written agreement before fitting anything at their door.

Do I need to register with the ICO for a video doorbell? Domestic use is mostly outside the main rules, but a landlord filming people at a rental is probably acting as a business, which brings UK GDPR duties and may mean paying the ICO’s data protection fee. I am a surveyor, not a data-protection adviser, so check the ICO’s current guidance before relying on this.

Are PIR security lights worth it? Yes. At the price of an LED floodlight, they are among the cheapest ways to make a back door feel safer. Evidence on crime reduction is mixed, and a sensor light is a deterrent and a convenience, not a lock. Combine it with a good door and a lock that meets the insurer’s standard.

What if the tenant wants to fit their own doorbell? That is their choice, but the tenancy should say whether drilling the frame or door needs consent. A stick-on battery doorbell is the easy case; one that needs holes in a fire door is not, because that can undo the door’s fire resistance. A listed building or managed block can add further rules.

Outdoor lighting, doors and access are all things I look at on a rental, because they sit between the security question and the safety one. A buy to let survey covers the property itself, and getting in touch is the way to arrange one. For the rest of the kit, there are more field-kit guides.
