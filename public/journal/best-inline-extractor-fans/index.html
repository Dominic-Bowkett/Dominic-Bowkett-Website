Most bathroom fan problems I see on survey aren't a dead fan — they're the right fan in the wrong job. A 100mm wall-mounted fan, the kind covered in my bathroom extractor fan guide, is built to push air straight through a wall or a short length of ducting to the outside. Ask it to clear a windowless ensuite three rooms deep, or serve a bathroom and a separate WC off one shared run, and it simply can't hold enough pressure over the distance and bends involved. That's the job an inline fan is built for: mounted mid-duct, usually in a loft or ceiling void, pulling air from one or more rooms through a longer run to a single outlet.

The distinction that actually matters when buying one is mixed-flow versus axial. An axial fan — a simple propeller in a tube — loses static pressure fast once it's fighting distance, bends or a grille at the far end; it's fine for a short, straight hop and struggles beyond that. A mixed-flow (sometimes called centrifugal) fan uses an impeller design that holds pressure over a genuinely long run, which is the whole reason this category of fan exists. Get that wrong and you've bought an inline fan that behaves like an underpowered wall fan.

An inline fan isn't the answer to every damp bathroom, though. If condensation and mould are turning up well beyond the room the fan serves — landing ceilings, bedroom corners, wardrobes on external walls — that's a whole-house ventilation question, and a PIV unit or a proper look at MVHR installation costs is the more honest fix than swapping one room's fan. And where the duct run to an external wall is short and straight, a standard wall-mounted fan is simpler, cheaper and easier to service — don't buy an inline fan for a job a wall fan already does well. With that sorted, here are the six I'd look at.

How I judge an inline extractor fan

Mixed-flow versus axial — the fork that decides everything else. A mixed-flow (centrifugal) fan holds static pressure over a long run of ducting; an axial fan is a propeller in a tube that loses pressure fast beyond a short, straight hop. Check which type you're actually buying before comparing prices — some budget "inline" fans are axial units in inline housings.

Duct diameter and total run against real static pressure, not the headline rate. Every extract-rate figure on a spec sheet is measured free-air, with nothing attached. Wire the same fan into 5m of ducting with a couple of 90° bends and the number it actually delivers drops — a rate with real headroom above the Part F minimum matters more than one that only just clears it on paper.

Where it physically sits. An inline fan lives mid-duct, in a loft or ceiling void, which means it needs to be reachable for cleaning and eventual replacement. A fan boxed in behind a fixed ceiling with no hatch nearby is a problem for whoever has to service it in five years' time.

Control that matches the room. A timer suits predictable use; a humidistat responds to actual moisture whenever it rises, which matters more in a room used outside a routine. Several fans here are timer-only, which is where a standalone humidistat controller earns its place.

Noise quoted honestly. A dB(A) figure at a stated distance and speed is useful; a "quiet" claim with no figure or measurement point behind it isn't worth much when the fan is sitting above a bedroom ceiling.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Mixed-flow versus axial is the real fork in the road, not price or brand. A mixed-flow fan is built to hold static pressure over a long duct run; an axial fan loses it quickly beyond a short, straight hop. Check which type is actually inside the housing before comparing anything else — the word "inline" on the box doesn't tell you which one you're getting.

The spec-sheet extract rate is measured free-air, not through your actual ducting. Every figure in the comparison table above is a best-case number with nothing attached. Wire the same fan into a real run with bends, a grille and a length of flexible ducting, and what it actually delivers is lower — buy headroom over the Part F minimum, not a number that only just scrapes it.

Where it sits has to be reachable. An inline fan lives mid-duct, almost always in a loft or ceiling void. If there's no hatch or access panel nearby, whoever eventually has to clean or replace it is in for a difficult afternoon — plan the mounting position around future access, not just the shortest run.

Humidistat, timer and PIR measure different things. A timer runs for a fixed period after being triggered by a switch — simple, but blind to actual humidity. A humidistat reacts directly to rising moisture, which is why Part F guidance favours it for windowless rooms and spaces with unpredictable use. A PIR sensor reacts to movement, not moisture, which suits general air freshness better than condensation control — don't assume the two are interchangeable.

One fan can legally serve more than one room — if the sizing adds up. Part F permits a single extract system to serve several wet rooms (an ensuite and a separate WC, say) provided the combined extract rates for every room served are met and the ducting is sized for the total load. That's a proper calculation, not just physically connecting two grilles to one duct.

What gets pushed that matters less than it sounds. A maximum free-air airflow figure isn't what the fan delivers day to day through real ducting, and a bigger duct diameter isn't automatically better if the room's actual extract requirement doesn't need it. Real headroom on a realistic, ducted extract rate beats any single headline number.

Questions I get asked

What's the actual difference between an inline fan and a normal bathroom fan? A standard bathroom fan mounts through the wall or ceiling and pushes air a short, direct distance outside. An inline fan sits mid-duct, usually in a loft or ceiling void, and is built to hold pressure over a longer run with bends — the job a wall fan can't do.

Do I need Building Control sign-off to fit an inline fan? Replacing an existing fan like-for-like on its existing ducting and cabling generally doesn't need notifying. Adding a new duct run, cutting a new external outlet, or converting a passive vent into a powered system is more likely to raise a question — ask whoever fits it to confirm.

Can one inline fan serve two rooms, like an ensuite and a separate WC? Yes, provided the combined extract rates for both rooms are met and the ducting is sized for the total airflow — that calculation is the installer's job, not something to assume from the fan's spec sheet alone.

Will fitting a humidistat make the fan run constantly? No — it only switches on once relative humidity crosses the threshold you set, then runs an overrun period once it drops back down. A naturally steamy bathroom running near-constantly usually means the threshold needs adjusting, not that the humidistat itself is faulty.

I don’t install ventilation systems myself, but a ventilation testing visit shows what an existing inline fan actually delivers against what Part F expects, and a PAS 2035 retrofit assessment is the right next step if condensation is spreading well beyond one room — that's usually a PIV unit or a proper look at MVHR installation costs, not a fan swap.
