A tablet doesn’t like condensation and a phone doesn’t like being handled with wet or muddy fingers, so plenty of what gets recorded on a wet site day still gets written down by hand — a crack pattern, a damp-meter reading grid, a rough floor layout before it goes into a proper plan. The trouble is what an ordinary notepad does on exactly the kind of day that makes taking notes by hand necessary in the first place: driving rain on a roof inspection, a dripping loft hatch, condensation running down the inside of a single-glazed window while it’s being measured. Standard paper turns to pulp within a page or two, and whatever was written on it goes with it.

A genuinely waterproof notebook solves that one problem — treated or synthetic paper that can still be written on while it’s wet, not just a cover that shrugs off a splash while the pages underneath stay ordinary. It doesn’t replace a dictaphone for a running verbal commentary through a loft; it’s the thing that’s still legible after it’s been rained on or handled with a wet glove.

What follows is built from manufacturers’ own paper-technology claims and cross-checked published feedback, including one notebook worth buying for its format but which doesn’t actually belong in the waterproof category at all — and is sold alongside these seven anyway.

How I judge a waterproof notebook

Genuinely waterproof paper, not just a wipeable cover. Treated or synthetic paper that can be written on while wet is a different product from a tough cover wrapped around ordinary paper — check the paper technology, not just the word “waterproof” on the listing.

What actually writes on it. Coated and synthetic papers can resist a standard ballpoint as well as they resist rain; most manufacturers here recommend a pencil or a pressurised all-weather pen instead.

Format matched to the job. A pocket pad for a couple of figures, a full spiral or A5 page for a sketch or a reading grid — the wrong size either gets left in the van or runs out of room.

Binding that survives a pocket. Spiral, stapled and casebound all behave differently in a jacket pocket or a tool bag over a full day; a rigid hardcover that digs in on a ladder gets left behind.

Enough pages without becoming another thing to carry. A 48-page pad runs out mid-survey; a 160-page casebound is bulk that only earns its place if the notes need to last months, not one visit.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Genuinely waterproof paper, not a wipeable cover. Coated, synthetic or stone paper can be written on while it’s actually wet. A tough or wipeable cover round ordinary paper only protects the block while it’s closed — the Exacompta Chartwell here is the honest example of that distinction, worth checking before assuming the word “waterproof” on a listing means what it says.

Pencil or a pressurised pen beats an ordinary biro. On coated or synthetic paper a standard ballpoint can skate across the surface or fail to lay ink down at all, especially once the page is properly wet — every manufacturer here using treated paper says as much on its own listing.

Format matched to the job, not the widest page available. A pocket pad that never gets carried because it’s inconvenient protects nothing. Buy the size that actually fits the pocket or bag it’ll live in day to day.

Casebound versus spiral is about longevity, not toughness. A sewn hardcover earns its price when it’s the one place a job’s notes live for months; a stapled or spiral pad is disposable, and that’s fine if that’s what it’s bought for.

What the listings push that doesn’t matter: tactical branding and cover colour. Olive drab, a “tactical” name and black-over-green options sit on the same paper technology as the plain version and change nothing about what the notebook can survive.

Questions I get asked

Can I write on a waterproof notebook with a normal pen?

Sometimes, but don’t rely on it. The coated and synthetic papers used across this guide are designed to repel water, which means an ordinary ballpoint can skate across the surface instead of soaking in, especially once the page is actually wet. Every Rite in the Rain listing recommends a pencil or one of the brand’s own pressurised all-weather pens; the same caution applies to Field Notes’ Yupo paper and Modestone’s stone paper. A regular biro works fine on the Exacompta Chartwell, because that one uses traditional paper rather than a waterproof coating.

What’s the actual difference between waterproof and water-resistant?

Waterproof paper — coated, synthetic or stone paper — can be written on while it’s actually wet and dries without the ink running or the page disintegrating. Water-resistant covers just protect a normal paper block from splashes while it’s closed; open the cover in the rain and the pages inside behave like any other notepad. The Exacompta Chartwell in this guide is the water-resistant case in point: a tough cover round paper that’s stronger when damp than standard stock, but not paper that’s meant to be written on while wet.

Which format should I buy — pocket pad or full page?

A pocket stapled pad like the Rite in the Rain 135 is for short notes and quick figures, and it’s small enough to actually be carried every day, which matters more than page size if a bigger notebook ends up left in the van instead. A full A5 or 4×6 spiral notebook — the 946 or the Modestone — earns its size back the first time a sketch, a damp-meter reading grid or a room layout needs a proper page rather than a margin.

Do I need a specific brand, or will any “waterproof” notebook do?

Check what the paper technology actually is before buying, not just the word “waterproof” on the listing. Rite in the Rain’s coated paper, Field Notes’ Yupo synthetic sheet and Modestone’s stone paper are all genuinely writable while wet; a notebook that only claims a “weatherproof” or “water-resistant” cover, like the Exacompta Chartwell in this guide, is a different and more limited product even when it turns up in the same search results.

A notebook that survives the weather is a small thing next to the finding it records — a damp reading, a crack width, a missing vent.
