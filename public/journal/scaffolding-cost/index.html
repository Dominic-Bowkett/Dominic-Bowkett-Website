Scaffolding cost
in 2026

Scaffolding for an average two-storey semi typically costs £600–£1,500 for a standard 6–8 week hire, covering erection, dismantling and the hire period — from as little as £150–£350 for a small access tower up to £1,400–£2,800 or more to fully wrap a larger detached house.

Published 4 Sep 2026 Reading time · 8 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Scaffolding is one of those costs that turns up as a line item on almost every roof, render, repointing or extension job I survey, and it rarely gets shopped around the way the headline work does. For a standard domestic job — erection, dismantling and a fixed minimum hire period bundled into one price — that typically runs to £600–£1,500 for a two-storey semi, from £150–£350 for a small access tower up to a chimney or gutter, and £1,400–£2,800 or more to fully wrap a larger detached or three-storey property.

But it depends on more than the size of the house. How many elevations need covering, how many lifts (storeys) it has to reach, how long it needs to stand, whether it can sit entirely on your own land or has to encroach on the pavement, and what extras — loading bays, debris netting, a temporary roof over a reroof — get added all move the number well before the scaffolder turns up with the tubes.

I should say plainly where I sit on this one: I don't erect or hire scaffolding, that's a specialist contractor's job. What I do is see it go up and come down on the majority of jobs I'm involved with, as a survey finding, a party wall matter, or simply the access that lets a roofer or render contractor do their work safely. That vantage point is useful for judging whether a quote is reasonable and whether a job genuinely needs the full rig — it isn't a reason to take my word over a scaffolder's on their own pricing.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 4 September 2026 against current UK scaffold hire pricing, cross-referenced across several sources, and are a starting point rather than a quote. As with the rest of this series, the South East — my own patch included — tends to price above the England-wide average, and London runs higher again, by as much as 20% or more compared with other regions.

Some scaffolders price larger or more complex jobs by area rather than by job type: typically £15–£25 per m² of scaffolded elevation for a standard domestic job, rising for awkward access or extra-high lifts. If any part of the scaffold needs to stand on a public pavement or road — the usual case on a terraced or semi-detached house with a front garden shorter than the scaffold's footprint — a highway licence from the local council is a legal requirement on top of the scaffold price itself, typically £90–£260 for a 28-day period depending on the council. Confirm in the quote whether that's included or something you or the scaffolder need to arrange separately.

What drives the price

Height and number of lifts. Each additional storey means another working platform, more bracing and ties, and more time to erect and dismantle safely — the jump from a two-storey to a three-storey scaffold is rarely a straight-line increase in cost.

How much of the building it wraps. Scaffolding one elevation for a chimney repair is a different job to a full wrap for repointing or rendering all four sides. Quotes are usually priced per elevation or by total scaffolded area for exactly this reason.

Ground conditions and how it ties in. Sloping or soft ground needs base plates, sole boards and sometimes a bespoke design rather than a standard configuration, all of which adds time. How well the rig ties into the building fabric matters too — and is worth watching, since poorly placed ties through render or old brick pointing can cause damage that costs more to put right than the scaffold saved.

Duration and the minimum hire term. Most firms quote a fixed minimum hire period of 6–8 weeks as standard, covering erection, dismantling and that initial term as one price. Extend beyond it and weekly charges apply, typically 5–10% of the original quote per additional week — worth factoring in if the build programme behind it is at all likely to slip.

Extras. A loading bay for materials, debris netting or sheeting (roughly £3–£6 per m²), a full temporary roof over a reroof (£1,500–£4,000), or a bridge over a conservatory or extension all add to the base price, and are worth pricing as separate line items rather than accepting as an unexplained lump sum.

The highway licence. Covered above, but it's easy to miss in a quote comparison — one scaffolder's price might include it, another's might not, which can make a cheaper-looking quote the more expensive one once it's added.

Do you actually need full scaffolding?

Sometimes not. If the job is genuinely inspection only — checking a chimney stack, a valley gutter or flashings that are visible but hard to reach — a drone roof survey can often answer the question for a fraction of the cost and without the 6–8 week commitment a fixed scaffold implies, since there's nothing to erect or dismantle at all. It isn't a substitute where actual work at height is needed, only where the point is to see and photograph the roof clearly.

For small, short jobs — clearing a gutter, replacing a handful of slipped tiles, a quick repair to a soffit — a scaffold tower hired by the day, or a mobile elevating work platform for a single visit, is usually far cheaper than committing to a fixed rig with a minimum hire term built around weeks rather than hours. Where I'd push back is on using that logic for anything involving sustained work at height — a reroof, repointing, rendering, or extended painting — where scaffolding isn't a cost to shop down but the thing that keeps the people doing the work safe, and a contractor cutting corners on it is a red flag about the rest of the job too.

One thing that catches people out: if the scaffold needs to stand on next door's land, or even just oversail their airspace to reach your own wall, that's usually a Party Wall etc. Act 1996 matter requiring notice and, often, a formal party wall agreement before it goes up — not something to leave until the scaffolder is on site. It's a different process to the highway licence, and getting it sorted late is one of the more common causes of a delayed start I come across.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Check whether erection, dismantling, hire period and highway licence are itemised or bundled — and what happens, in writing, if the job overruns the minimum term.
  • Ask if the crew are CISRS-trained and the firm is a member of NASC (the National Access & Scaffolding Confederation) — a genuine quality signal in this trade, not just a badge.
  • Confirm the weekly inspection regime. A scaffold anyone could fall two metres or more from must be formally inspected at intervals of no more than 7 days by a competent person, with a written, dated record — a visible "scaff tag" on site is a reasonable proxy that it's actually happening.
  • Ask who's applying for the highway licence and when, if any part of the rig will sit on the pavement or road.
  • Get a note of how it ties into the building and who's making good any damage to render, pointing or paintwork once it comes down.
  • Sort access over neighbouring land before the erection date, not after, if the scaffold or its footprint crosses the boundary.

Questions I get asked

How much does scaffolding cost per week? Weekly rates vary a lot by size: a small access tower runs to roughly £100–£200 a week, a chimney or single-elevation platform £350–£700 a week, and a full wrap around a two-storey semi around £650–£1,200 a week. In practice, though, most domestic scaffolders quote a single price for a fixed minimum hire period rather than a bare weekly rate, so the per-week figure matters most once you're extending beyond that initial term.

Do you need a licence to put scaffolding on the pavement? Yes. If any part of the scaffold stands on a public pavement or road, a highway licence is a legal requirement under the Highways Act 1980, typically costing £90–£260 for a 28-day period depending on the council. It's usually the scaffolding contractor's job to apply for it, but confirm that in the quote — I've seen jobs stall because nobody applied for it in time.

How long can scaffolding stay up before it costs more? Most domestic quotes are built around a fixed minimum hire period of 6–8 weeks, covering erection, dismantling and that initial term as one price. Keep it beyond that and weekly extension charges apply, usually around 5–10% of the original quote per additional week — worth factoring in before you commit to a build programme that's likely to slip.

Do you always need full scaffolding, or is there a cheaper alternative? Not always. If the job is genuinely inspection only — checking a chimney, valley or gutter run rather than working on it — a drone roof survey can answer the question without any scaffold at all. For small, short jobs like clearing a gutter or replacing a few tiles, a scaffold tower hired by the day is usually far cheaper than a fixed rig with a 6–8 week minimum term. But for anything involving sustained work at height — a reroof, repointing, rendering — full scaffolding isn't a cost to shop down; it's what keeps the people doing the work safe.
