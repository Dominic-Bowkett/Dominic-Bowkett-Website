The best SDS drills
for inspection holes
and site work

Most of what I ask of a cordless SDS drill is one clean 10–12mm hole through a mortar joint so a borescope can go into the cavity — then making good so you’d never know I was there. These are the drills I’d trust to do it without drama.

A surveyor’s SDS drill has a narrower job description than a builder’s. I’m not chasing walls, breaking out screeds or fixing down a hundred frame anchors a day. I’m drilling one, occasionally two, 10–12mm inspection holes through the outer leaf of a cavity wall — through the mortar joint, not the brick — so a borescope can tell me whether the cavity is clear, bridged, or full of slumped insulation. Then I fill the hole, point it to match, and put the kettle on.

That use-case changes what matters. A 5kg breaker-adjacent machine is the wrong tool when you’re at the top of a ladder holding the drill in one hand and your balance in the other. What you want is light, brushless, controllable at low speed so the bit doesn’t skate off the joint, and on a battery platform you already own. What separates the good ones from the junk isn’t headline joules — almost anything with an SDS chuck will make a 10mm hole in lime or cement mortar. It’s weight, balance, trigger control and whether the thing starts cleanly where you put it.

Everything below is SDS-plus, which is the only shank size this job needs. I’ve included one corded machine for people who want maximum value and don’t mind a cable, and two budget cordless options that are honestly fine for occasional use.

How I judge an SDS drill

Weight and balance first. The classic inspection hole is at first-floor level, drilled from a ladder, one-handed. Every hundred grams matters more than every extra joule. A compact SDS at 2.5–3kg with battery is comfortable; a 4kg machine is a liability up there, whatever the spec sheet says.

Low-speed control. Mortar joints are 10mm wide. You want a trigger that will spin the bit slowly and truly while you establish the hole, without the hammer action punching the tip sideways into the arris of the brick. Cheap drills tend to go from nothing to everything; good ones feather.

Brushless, on a platform you own. All the serious contenders are body-only. A bare tool at £150 is good value if you have batteries; add £80–£120 for a battery and charger if you don’t, and suddenly the kit deals or the budget brands make more sense. Brushless matters less for power than for battery life and motor longevity at our duty cycle.

Enough impact energy, not the most. For 10–12mm holes in mortar, 1.5–2 joules is ample. Even hard Victorian cement pointing gives up in seconds. The joules arms race is for people drilling 20mm holes in C40 concrete all day, which is not us.

Dust behaviour. Drilling above your head or at eye level puts silica-laden dust exactly where you don’t want it. I don’t carry an M-class extractor for two holes; I carry a £5 rubber dust cup that sits on the bit and catches most of it, and I drill from a slight downward angle where I can. More on this below.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Drill the joint, not the brick. This is technique rather than tooling, but it decides whether the make-good is invisible or an apology. A 10mm hole through a perp or bed joint disappears under matched mortar; a hole through a brick face never quite does, and on some stock you can’t buy a match at all. Start the drill slowly, rotation only if the mortar is soft, and let the hammer in once the hole is established. On lime mortar you barely need hammer at all.

The make-good kit costs less than a drill bit. Mine is a small tub of ready-mixed repair mortar, a jointing bar, a strip of plastic to keep mortar off the brick arrises, and a couple of mortar dyes for the estates where everything is pale or everything is soot. Fill in two lifts so it doesn’t slump into the cavity, and finish the joint profile to match the wall — bucket handle, weatherstruck, whatever the original bricklayer did. Photograph before and after for the file.

Take dust seriously, cheaply. Drilling masonry makes respirable crystalline silica, and the HSE limits exist for good reasons. For two holes I use a rubber dust cup on the bit (a few pounds, fits any SDS bit), an FFP3 mask, and common sense about wind direction and open windows. If you’re drilling indoors — say through an internal leaf — a helper holding a vacuum nozzle under the hole catches nearly everything. What you don’t need for survey work is a £500 M-class extractor bolted to the drill; that’s for people cutting chases all day.

What the salesmen push that you can ignore. Bluetooth connectivity and tool-tracking apps do nothing for a one-minute task. Chisel mode is genuinely optional — nice for raking out a joint, never essential. Anti-vibration ratings matter enormously at trade duty cycles and barely at ours; you will not approach the HSE exposure action value drilling inspection holes. And nobody drilling a 10mm hole needs SDS-max, which is a different, heavier shank system for holes from about 20mm up.

Ask before you drill. An inspection hole is minor, but it is still making a hole in someone’s house. I get explicit consent (in writing, in the booking terms and again on the doorstep), check what’s behind and around the drilling position — cables and gas barrels have no respect for mortar joints — and on anything listed or in a conservation area, think twice and drill somewhere discreet, or not at all.

Questions I get asked

What size hole do I need for a cavity wall borescope?

Ten millimetres suits most borescope probes; I carry a 12mm bit as well for chunkier dual-lens camera heads and for cavities where I want room to angle the probe. Drill through a mortar joint at a slight upward angle so the probe naturally looks across and up the cavity, and stop as you feel the bit break through the inner face of the outer leaf — you want a hole through one leaf, not a core through both. My guide to the borescopes worth buying covers what goes through the hole once you’ve made it.

SDS or combi drill for masonry?

A decent combi on hammer setting will get through soft mortar and common brick, and for years plenty of surveyors managed that way. The difference is mechanism: a combi’s hammer is two ratcheting discs pushing the whole chuck back and forth, while an SDS has a piston striking the bit itself — several times the blow energy, with the bit free to move in the chuck. In practice that means an SDS makes a cleaner hole in a fraction of the time, doesn’t glaze or wander in hard cement mortar, and doesn’t cook its gearbox doing it. If you drill masonry more than occasionally, the SDS pays for itself in tidiness alone.

Do I need SDS-plus or SDS-max?

SDS-plus, without hesitation. The two are different shank sizes for different classes of tool: SDS-plus covers bits up to roughly 20–26mm and light chiselling; SDS-max is the big-machine standard for breaking, coring and holes beyond that. Everything in this guide is SDS-plus, every 10–12mm masonry bit you’ll ever buy is SDS-plus, and an SDS-max machine is unusable for fine work — apart from anything else, small-diameter SDS-max bits barely exist.

Can you drill an inspection hole in a cavity wall and make good?

Yes — done properly it’s a minor, reversible intervention, which is exactly why borescope inspection is so useful before specifying cavity wall insulation, diagnosing penetrating damp or checking wall-tie condition. The hole goes through the mortar joint of the outer leaf only, with the owner’s consent, away from services, and is filled the same visit with a matched mortar in two lifts and the joint profile reinstated. On a well-executed repair you’ll struggle to find the hole a season later. What I don’t recommend is drilling brick faces, drilling without checking for buried cables, or leaving holes open “for ventilation” — an open 12mm hole is a wick for driving rain and an invitation for masonry bees.

Inspection holes usually happen because something bigger is being decided — whether a cavity can take insulation, why a wall is damp, whether a retrofit plan matches the building in front of it.
