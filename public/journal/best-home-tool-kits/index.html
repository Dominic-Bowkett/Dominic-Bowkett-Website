Most of what I flag in a HomeBuyer Survey as “monitor” or “minor, attend to in due course” turns into a first-weekend job once the keys are actually in a new owner’s hand — a door that catches on the frame, a loose banister spindle, a curtain pole that’s never been put up, a radiator valve that needs a firm quarter-turn rather than a call to a plumber. None of it needs a tradesperson. All of it needs a screwdriver that fits, a hammer that isn’t borrowed, and a pair of pliers that don’t round off the nut before the job’s done.

A surprising number of first-time buyers move in owning no tools at all — a flat-share rarely generates the need, and the one drill in the house goes back to a parent or a housemate the day the old tenancy ends. I get the “what should I actually buy” question often enough in the fortnight after completion that it’s worth answering properly: not a trip round a hardware shop item by item, but one boxed kit that covers the list without forty separate decisions.

Seven kits below, from a genuinely cheap starter case to one built to trade standard, what’s actually inside each one, and the handful of things every boxed kit leaves out that are worth adding separately.

How I judge a tool kit

The five tools that do most of the work. A claw hammer, an adjustable spanner, combination pliers, a decent multi-bit screwdriver and a tape measure handle the large majority of first-house jobs — everything else in a boxed kit is useful padding around that core five. Judge a kit on how those five feel in the hand before counting the rest.

Real pieces, not marketing pieces. A “150-piece” kit that counts every individual screw, wall plug and drill bit separately can hold fewer actual tools than an honestly-labelled 40-piece case. Look at what’s itemised on the listing, not just the headline number.

What the case is missing. None of the kits below include a stud finder, a proper socket tester or a long tape measure for a room — genuinely useful early additions covered in their own guides, linked below, rather than padded into a box that’s already full.

Material over count. Pliers and spanners in these price brackets are chrome-plated carbon steel rather than trade-grade chrome vanadium throughout; fine for occasional DIY, but the reason a £250 professional set doesn’t belong on this list — the jump in material quality above the £60 mark is real but isn’t what a first house needs yet.

A kit is a starting point, not a toolbox for life. Every case here earns its keep in the first year and then gets supplemented tool by tool as actual jobs come up — a cordless drill, a stud finder, a longer level. Buying the professional set on day one solves a problem that doesn’t exist yet.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Piece count is a marketing number, not a tool count. A “150-piece” kit that counts every wall plug, screw and drill bit individually can contain fewer actual tools than an honestly labelled 40-piece case. Read the listing’s itemised breakdown before comparing two kits on the headline figure alone.

A socket set matters more than it sounds for flat-pack furniture. Most flat-pack fixings use a hex key rather than a socket, but anything metal — a garden gate, a bike, a bed frame — increasingly turns up with a hex-head bolt that a socket and ratchet handles far faster than an Allen key alone.

None of these kits include a stud finder, a proper socket tester or a long tape measure — genuinely useful early purchases for a first house that none of the boxed kits below bother with, because they’re specialist rather than general-purpose. See the linked guides below for those.

A blow-moulded case beats a canvas bag for anything that lives in a shed or a car boot, but a bag packs flatter into a kitchen drawer or an understairs cupboard. Worth thinking about where the kit is actually going to live before choosing on tool contents alone.

“Chrome vanadium steel” printed on a budget kit’s pliers doesn’t mean what it does on a single trade tool. At this price bracket it’s usually a thin plating over softer metal underneath rather than genuine hardened chrome vanadium throughout — fine for the occasional job these kits are built for, not something to lean on for daily trade use.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a trade-brand kit like the Makita, or is a cheaper set enough for a first home? For most first-year jobs — hanging pictures, tightening hinges, assembling furniture — a mid-range kit like the VonHaus or Draper covers everything. The Makita earns its price if the tools are going to see regular use beyond the house itself, a car or a bike included.

What’s missing from every kit in this guide that I’ll probably need anyway? A cordless drill, a stud finder before putting up a shelf on a stud wall rather than blindly into plasterboard, and a proper socket tester before trusting an unfamiliar socket. All three are covered in their own guides, linked below.

Is a bigger piece count always better value? No — check what’s actually itemised. Two kits at a similar price can differ hugely in genuine tool count once wall plugs, screws and driver bits are stripped out of the headline number.

Should I just buy the individual tools separately instead of a kit? For five or six specific tools, possibly — but a boxed kit removes the decision fatigue of choosing a hammer, pliers and a screwdriver set separately, usually for less than buying each individually, and keeps everything in one place from day one.

For the jobs a boxed kit doesn’t cover: a stud finder before the first shelf goes up, a socket tester before trusting an unfamiliar socket, and a proper tape measure for anything longer than a picture frame.
