The best
pipe insulation
for lofts and garages

Most winters I get asked to look at a split pipe somewhere near a cold water tank or an outside wall — a straight length of fifteen or twenty-two mil copper that's sat completely bare in an unheated loft or garage for years, doing fine until the one night it drops hard below freezing. Lagging it wouldn't have stopped that pipe carrying water when it's genuinely cold enough for long enough, but it buys real time, and on the runs closest to the tank it's usually the difference between a damp patch and a ceiling down. Here's what actually matters when you're choosing pipe insulation — foam against rubber against mineral wool — and six products worth buying.

Pipe insulation does two separate jobs, and it's worth knowing which one you're solving for before you buy anything. Around cold-water pipework in an unheated loft or garage, it slows heat loss from the pipe, buying time before standing water freezes and expands enough to split it — it doesn't stop that happening in a hard enough or long enough freeze, whatever the packaging implies. Around hot water and heating pipework, the same lagging keeps paid-for heat inside the pipe instead of warming an unheated loft nobody uses, which shows up, in a small way, on an EPC. And on a cold pipe running through a warm, humid space — an airing cupboard, a bathroom stud wall — it stops the pipe sweating and dripping condensation onto whatever's stored nearby, a different job again wearing the same product.

It's cheap by the standards of anything else in a survey report, and one of the few defects a homeowner can genuinely fix in an afternoon rather than booking a tradesperson. The catch: most generic foam lagging is thinner than Building Regulations actually specify for compliant pipework — it matters less on a short exposed run, more on a long primary heating run through a cold loft. Below is how I'd judge what's on the shelf, and six products — foam, elastomeric rubber and genuine mineral wool — worth considering.

Worth saying up front: if pipes keep freezing despite proper lagging, or a garage conversion needs pipework kept genuinely warm through winter, insulation alone isn't the fix — that's a job for trace heating tape or, better, rerouting the pipe out of the cold space altogether. And if the real issue behind the call is heat loss across the whole house rather than one exposed pipe run, lagging a metre of copper won't move the needle much on its own.

How I judge pipe insulation

Wall thickness against the diameter you're actually lagging. A 9mm wall on a small-bore pipe does more, proportionally, than the same 9mm on a wide one — Building Regs' own compliance table scales the required thickness by pipe diameter, not a flat figure across every size, so check the wall thickness against the bore you're buying for rather than assuming any foam tube does the same job.

Material and fire rating. Plain polyethylene foam is cheapest and most common, but combustible with no meaningful fire rating. Elastomeric nitrile rubber (Armaflex, K-Flex and similar) carries a genuine fire classification and wider temperature range, the spec called for on plant rooms and anywhere fire rating matters. Mineral wool sits above both — non-combustible, the usual choice near a boiler or hot flue.

Self-seal versus needing separate tape. Most of what's sold now has a factory-applied adhesive strip along the slit, so it closes around the pipe without extra fixings. It's worth checking before you buy a bulk roll — taping every joint by hand on a long loft run is a lot slower and rarely as neat.

Temperature range. A cold-water-only product is fine on the tank feed but isn't necessarily rated for the primary flow and return running alongside it. Check the stated range against what's actually in the pipe, not just what's in the loft.

Price against how much pipe you're actually covering. There's no need to spec a premium elastomeric rubber for a short cold-feed run to an outside tap when a length of basic foam does the same practical job for a fraction of the cost — save the better product for primary heating pipework and anywhere a fire rating genuinely matters.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Most off-the-shelf foam and rubber is thinner than Building Regs actually specify. Approved Document L (Volume 1: Dwellings) requires hot water storage pipework to be insulated for at least 1m from the cylinder connections, and sets minimum thicknesses in its Table 4.4 by pipe diameter — but those figures assume a thermal conductivity of 0.025 W/(m·K) or better. None of the mainstream products here reach that: Climaflex sits around 0.034–0.038 W/(m·K), K-Flex and Armaflex around 0.035. A 9–13mm wall on ordinary foam or rubber doesn't automatically satisfy the simplified table at every diameter — either the wall thickness needs increasing pro-rata, or compliance is demonstrated through the fuller BS 5422:2023 calculation instead. Worth knowing before assuming any lagging is “Part L compliant” simply because it's insulation.

BS 5422 was updated in 2023 — check which version a source is quoting. This is the standard that governs how thickness is calculated by pipe diameter and temperature. The 2009 edition (amended 2014) was withdrawn on 30 June 2023 and replaced by BS 5422:2023 — plenty of trade content online still cites the old edition as current.

Fire ratings aren't all measured the same way. Armaflex's “Class O” is the older UK surface spread of flame classification to BS 476 Parts 6 and 7; K-Flex's Euroclass B(L)-s2,d0 is a separate, newer test to EN 13501-1. Both are genuine, but not interchangeable — if a specification names one system, match it rather than assuming any “fire-rated” product satisfies it.

Lagging slows heat loss, it doesn't stop freezing outright. In a prolonged hard freeze with no heating running, an insulated pipe still eventually reaches freezing — insulation buys hours, not immunity. Where pipes have frozen or split repeatedly despite being lagged, the fix is usually trace heating tape under the insulation, or moving the run out of the cold space, not a thicker wrap.

It won't fix a genuinely cold house, and it's not a leak repair. A metre of lagging on one exposed pipe does nothing for a whole-house heat loss problem, and nothing for a joint that's actually failed — that's a plumber's job, not an insulation one.

Questions I get asked

Do I need to insulate every pipe in the loft, or just the exposed ones? Focus on anything running through an unheated space — loft, garage, exterior wall void — especially the last metre or so nearest the cold water tank or cylinder connections, which Approved Document L specifically calls out. Pipework already buried inside a warm, insulated floor or wall doesn't need it.

Does pipe insulation actually show up on an EPC? Yes, in a small way — the RdSAP assessment behind a domestic EPC records whether primary hot water pipework is insulated as one input to the heating system's assumed efficiency. It won't move a rating by itself, but it's one more box genuinely ticked rather than flagged.

Can I use pipe insulation instead of proper loft insulation? No — different problems. Pipe lagging cuts heat loss from that specific pipework; it does nothing for the fabric heat loss through the roof that loft insulation addresses. Both jobs are usually worth doing, but one doesn't substitute for the other.

What thickness do I actually need? For an ordinary cold feed, 9–13mm foam or rubber matched to the pipe bore is the normal DIY spec. For primary heating pipework, or where a specification names a standard directly, check the wall thickness against BS 5422:2023's tables for the actual diameter and water temperature rather than assuming any off-the-shelf product covers it.

Pipe insulation isn't its own line item on a domestic EPC visit, but primary pipework insulation is one of the smaller inputs behind the rating, and it's exactly the cheap, easy fix I'll flag on the day rather than leave for someone to discover the hard way.
