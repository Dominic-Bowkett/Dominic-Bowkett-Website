Most roof coverings get assessed from the ground — binoculars, a long lens, a drone pass on anything two-storey, steep or awkward. But every so often a job genuinely calls for a closer look: a suspected slipped ridge tile that photographs ambiguously from the ground, a flashing detail at a chimney stack, a valley gutter that needs checking for debris and splits rather than guessed at. On a single-storey extension roof, in decent weather, with someone available to foot the ladder, getting up there properly beats squinting through a lens. That is a roof ladder’s job, and it is a genuinely different tool to the telescopic and step ladders that handle the rest of a survey — different failure modes, different certification questions, and a two-person job rather than a one-person one.

The category splits cleanly into two. Dedicated roof ladders are a single purpose-built unit with the ridge hook and running wheels integrated into the ladder itself — no guesswork about whether it fits what you own, but a large, heavy, expensive thing that needs somewhere to live. Roof hook & wheel kits are the pragmatic alternative: a hook-and-wheel assembly that clamps onto the top rungs of an extension ladder you already carry, turning it into a roof ladder in a couple of minutes and packing away small when the job is done. For occasional use, the hook kit is usually the right answer; the dedicated ladder earns its considerably higher price when the roof itself is the regular job rather than the occasional one.

Whichever route you take, this is not solo kit. Getting a roof ladder into position and keeping it there needs someone footing the base while the other person climbs, in line with what the Work at Height Regulations 2005 actually expect of access equipment used properly. If there is nobody free to do that, the honest answer is not a better roof ladder — it is binoculars from the ground or a drone pass instead. First, the one I’d buy.

How I judge a roof ladder

The hook actually fits the rungs you’ve got. “Universal” on a listing means it is designed to fit the round or box-section rungs used by the mainstream UK ladder brands — it is not a guarantee for every ladder in every van. Check the rung profile against the kit’s stated compatibility before assuming it will clamp on.

Wheels, not just a bare hook. The wheels are what let you roll the ladder up the slope under control and lift it clear of the tiles rather than dragging a hook across them. On the older clay tiles and slate common on the housing stock I survey, that difference is the whole point of buying a proper kit rather than improvising.

The ladder underneath is rated for the job. A hook kit is only as good as the extension ladder it is clamped to. That means EN131 Professional class — the same standard covered in the telescopic ladder guide — not a domestic ladder pressed into roof duty because it happened to be in the shed.

Roof pitch within the manufacturer’s stated limit. Most of the kit in this guide is rated to somewhere around 45–55 degrees, which covers the great majority of ordinary UK pitched roofs. That figure is set per model, not a general rule of thumb, and steeper pitches are a different job entirely — scaffolding or a tower, not a heavier hook.

Genuinely a two-person tool. Footing the base while someone else climbs is not optional extra caution, it is how this equipment is designed to be used. If that is not available, the roof ladder is the wrong tool for that day, whatever is in the van.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The real decision is dedicated ladder versus hook kit — not which hook kit. Every hook-and-wheel kit in this guide sits in roughly the same £28–£48 band regardless of brand, doing essentially the same job in essentially the same way. The decision that actually changes what you are buying is whether roof access is regular enough in your work to justify several hundred pounds on a dedicated unit, not which trade name is stamped on a £35 hook.

Wheels exist to protect the roof, not just to help you climb. Rolling a wheeled hook up the slope under control, rather than dragging a bare hook across the surface, matters most on the older clay tiles and slate that turn up constantly on the housing stock I survey. A cracked tile caused by the access equipment is not a good look on a survey visit.

The extension ladder underneath is doing half the work. A hook kit does not upgrade a domestic ladder into trade-rated equipment; it is only as good as what it is clamped to. EN131 Professional class on the ladder itself is the baseline, covered in more detail in the telescopic ladder guide.

Roof pitch is a genuine limit, not marketing small print. Most kit here is rated to somewhere around 45–55 degrees. That is a manufacturer figure for that specific model, and steeper pitches change the job to scaffolding or a tower rather than a beefier hook.

Things the listings push that you can ignore: “universal fit” presented as a guarantee rather than a starting point — check it against your own rungs regardless. “Heavy-duty” without a stated weight rating attached to it. And vague “suitable for all roofs” claims that conveniently avoid naming a maximum pitch, which is the one figure that actually determines whether a kit is right for a given job.

Know when the answer is not to get on the roof at all. Steep pitches, wet or frosted tiles, fragile or unknown coverings, or simply nobody available to foot the ladder — any of those is the point at which binoculars from the ground or a drone pass is the better call than pushing a roof ladder into a job it was never suited to.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a dedicated roof ladder or will a hook kit do?

For occasional use on a domestic pitched roof with a decent extension ladder already in the van, a hook-and-wheel kit is the pragmatic answer — it clamps on in a couple of minutes and packs away small when the job is done. A dedicated roof ladder earns its far higher price when the roof itself is the regular job rather than the exception, or when the extension ladder underneath genuinely is not up to also carrying a hook kit. For the odd close-up look at a ridge tile or a flashing detail, that is more ladder, and more spend, than the job usually needs.

What roof pitch can a roof ladder be used on?

Most of the hook-and-wheel kits and dedicated roof ladders in this guide are rated to somewhere around 45–55 degrees, which covers the great majority of ordinary UK pitched roofs, but the exact figure is set by the manufacturer for that specific model and is worth checking rather than assuming. Steeper pitches, and anything with loose, fragile or wet coverings, move the job into scaffolding or tower-hire territory rather than a heavier roof ladder.

Can I use a roof ladder on my own?

Not safely, and not in line with what the Work at Height Regulations 2005 actually expect of access equipment used properly. Getting a roof ladder into position and footing the base while someone else climbs is a two-person task by design. If nobody is available to foot the ladder, that is the point at which binoculars from the ground, or a drone pass, becomes the sensible way to look at a roof rather than a compromise.

Will a roof hook kit fit any extension ladder I already own?

Most kits are marketed as universal and will clamp onto the standard round or box-section rungs used by the mainstream UK ladder brands, but “universal” on a listing is a claim worth checking against your actual ladder before relying on it, not a guarantee. Cross-reference the rung profile and width against the kit’s stated compatibility, and if in doubt, a kit from the same manufacturer as the ladder underneath removes the guesswork entirely.

A roof ladder covers the occasional close-up look; most roof-covering assessment on a domestic building survey still happens from the ground, with binoculars or a drone pass doing the routine work. Between the three, most pitched roofs get covered without anyone needing scaffolding.
