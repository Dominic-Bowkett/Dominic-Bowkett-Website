Most surveys never need hi-vis. A terraced house with a front door on the pavement does not call for a fluorescent jacket, and wearing one to a HomeBuyer inspection mostly makes the vendor nervous. But a fair share of the work is not like that: a snagging inspection on a development still being built, a part-finished extension with a skip on the drive and a delivery lorry reversing across the plot, a roof inspection past a scaffold, or a house at the end of a lane with nowhere to park but the verge. In all of those, being seen is cheaper than the alternative.

I have already covered the lighter option in the hi-vis vests guide, which is the right answer for most of the year. A jacket earns its place when the weather turns, because a vest over a thin shirt in January on a bare site is not a plan. A hi-vis jacket is a coat first and a safety garment second, so the sensible question is what sort of coat you actually need.

The market splits by weather. Bombers and parkas are for cold. Softshells are for the dry-but-breezy middle. Waterproof shells are for rain and double as the van spare. Six picks follow, drawn from manufacturers’ descriptions and published owner feedback, with the caveat that workwear ranges are reshuffled constantly and the live listing always wins.

How I judge a hi vis jacket

The class marking, not the colour. EN ISO 20471 is the standard that matters, and the class number on the label (1, 2 or 3) is set by how much fluorescent and retroreflective material is fitted. Class 3 is the highest and is what most jackets with sleeves achieve, because the sleeve bands count towards the total. A jacket sold as “hi-vis style” with no standard or class on the label is a fashion item, however bright the fabric looks.

Waterproof or showerproof. On workwear, EN 343 is the standard for foul-weather protection, rated for water penetration and breathability. A jacket that carries both EN 343 and EN ISO 20471 is the one to buy for a wet site. A softshell is usually wind- and water-resistant only, which is fine for the dry day and wrong for the downpour.

Warmth that suits the job. A surveyor spends the day alternating between standing outside and climbing into lofts, and a very warm parka turns a crawl space into a sauna. A shell you can layer under suits the mixed day; a parka suits the day you will spend outdoors.

A cut that works on a ladder. Check the length and the arm room. A short bomber clears a tool belt and a harness; a long parka does not. Stretch panels help on a step ladder, and a hem that rides up exposes your back to the rain.

Reflective tape that lasts. The tape is what keeps the jacket compliant. Follow the wash label, because hot washes and tumble dryers crack the bands, and a jacket with peeling tape no longer does its job.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Start with how often you will need it. If the answer is a few snagging visits and the odd building site a year, buy a cheap waterproof jacket and keep it in the van. If you are on live sites every week from October to March, the case for a softshell or a decent parka is stronger, and the extra money buys comfort over a hundred cold mornings rather than ten.

Second, check whether the site or client sets the requirement. Many main contractors and housebuilders specify Class 2 or 3 hi-vis as a condition of entry, and will turn you away at the gate without it, along with a hard hat and safety boots. Rail and highways work carry their own requirements, which are well beyond a general survey kit and which the principal contractor will tell you about in advance.

What gets pushed at you, and does not matter much: brand logos, a dozen pockets you will never use, and a high hydrostatic head figure on a coated fabric. Colour is mostly irrelevant too. Yellow, orange and the two-tone versions are all compliant if the class marking is there, and the dark panels on a two-tone jacket hide the dirt better.

The sensible stack for a cold wet day is a base layer, a softshell or fleece, and a hi-vis waterproof on the outside, with overtrousers below. A hi-vis jacket over a bulky fleece is warmer and more flexible than one heavy parka. If you want a jacket that is also a good rain shell without the hi-vis, see the waterproof jackets guide.

Finally, replace them when they wear out. A jacket coated in mud, grease and loft dust loses its fluorescence, and one with cracked or peeling tape is no longer doing its job. Wash on a cool cycle, dry away from direct heat and bin it when the colour has faded to dull.

Questions I get asked

Do I need to wear hi-vis on a house survey? Not for an ordinary occupied house, and it tends to be unhelpful. It is a sensible precaution on building sites, on part-finished properties where plant is moving, and for any inspection that involves working near a road. If the site manager or the client asks for it, wear it. The site rules apply to a surveyor as they do to anyone else.

What is the difference between Class 2 and Class 3 hi-vis? Class 3 has the most fluorescent and reflective material, and in practice means a garment with sleeves. Class 2 has less and is usually a vest or a short-sleeved top. Class 1 is the lowest and is not suitable where there is traffic. For a jacket, expect Class 3.

Can I wear a hi-vis jacket over a hard hat and a harness? A jacket that fits over a thick layer will usually allow it, but check the cut on the listing. Harnesses are normally worn over the outer layer on a roof job so the connection points stay clear, and a jacket with a long hem can interfere. Try the combination on before the day.

Is a hi-vis softshell as visible as a plain hi-vis jacket? Yes, if it is marked to the same class. The standard measures the area of fluorescent and reflective material, not the fabric type. Check the label.
