Most of the holes a surveyor drills are not dramatic. A 10mm hole through brick to get a borescope into a cavity, a pilot hole for a moisture probe, a fixing for a data logger, a screw out of an access panel that someone painted shut in 1998. None of that needs a hammer drill built for a building site, but it does need a drill that is compact enough for a loft, strong enough to cope with hard masonry and light enough to hold on a ladder. That is the case for an 18V cordless combi.

A combi drill is a drill driver with a hammer mode. It drives screws, drills timber and metal, and hammers through brick and block with a masonry bit. It will not do everything an SDS does: for repeated holes in dense masonry or concrete, an SDS drill is the proper tool and is quicker with less effort. But a combi is the better all-rounder, and for a few inspection holes a week it is often the only drill you need.

One caution before you buy anything. Drilling into a wall is an intrusive act: you can hit a cable, a pipe or a lintel, and in an occupied house you are leaving a hole. Check for services first with a cable detector or a stud finder that detects live wires, wear safety glasses and a respirator for masonry dust, and get the owner’s permission. In a listed building, ask the conservation officer first.

How I judge a cordless combi drill

Compactness. Length and weight come first for survey work. A drill that does not fit between joists or inside a boiler cupboard stays in the van. Compact brushless 18V combis solve this, and that matters more than extra torque.

Torque against the job. Stated torque runs from about 50Nm to well over 150Nm. A borescope hole in brick needs far less than a large holesaw does. Once a drill is past 55 to 70Nm, the quality of the masonry bit and your patience matter more than the number.

Brushless motor. Brushless drills run cooler, last longer on a battery and keep going where a brushed motor bogs down. The price premium is small now and I would not buy a brushed drill for regular site use.

Chuck and hammer mode. A 13mm metal keyless chuck holds a 10mm masonry bit securely and is what to look for. The hammer mode should be selectable with a clear switch, since you do not want it on when driving screws into plasterboard.

Battery platform. This is the decision that matters for years. Choose the platform you will also buy an SDS, an impact driver and a torch on. Swapping later means buying chargers and batteries twice.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The bit matters more than the drill. A blunt or cheap masonry bit makes any drill feel weak. A decent carbide-tipped bit in the right size does more for hard brick than another 30Nm. Buy bits separately and replace them when they stop cutting.

Battery capacity, not just voltage. Every drill here is 18V, but a 2.0Ah battery is a very different day to a 5.0Ah. For a handful of holes a 2.0Ah is light and fine; for a full day of site work take two larger ones.

Skip the big kits. Combi drills sold with 100-piece bit sets and a bag look good value. You will use three bits from them. Buy the body, a battery and a good set of masonry bits.

Be wary of unbranded imports. Cheap drills quote impressive voltages and torque. Established brands publish tested figures and sell spare batteries and chargers for years. Buy the brand that will still support the battery in five years.

When a combi is the wrong answer. If you are regularly drilling dense concrete or large holes, buy an SDS. If your work is access panels and consumer units, a cordless screwdriver is lighter and does the job.

Questions I get asked

What size hole do I need for a cavity borescope? Most inspection borescopes need 8 to 12mm, so a 10mm masonry bit is the sensible default. Drill from a mortar joint where you can, since it is easier to make good than a hole through the brick face. The borescope guide has the camera diameters.

Can a combi drill replace an SDS? For occasional holes in brick and block, yes. For repeated drilling in hard masonry or concrete, no: an SDS hammers with far less effort and a combi will overheat and tire your wrist. If the drilling is regular work, buy both.

Is a brushless drill worth it? Yes, for regular use. It runs cooler, lasts longer between charges and keeps going under load. A brushed drill is acceptable if you drill a few holes a year.

Do I need an impact driver as well? Not for survey work. A combi handles the screws a surveyor drives. An impact driver becomes useful if you fix heavy timber or long screws, which is builder’s work rather than inspection.
