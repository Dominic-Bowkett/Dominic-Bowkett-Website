Heat pump
servicing cost
in 2026

An air source heat pump service typically costs £120–£200 a visit, or £144–£345 a year on a service plan. Most warranties expect it; the law, as far as I can find, does not.

Published 11 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Servicing an air source heat pump typically costs £120–£200 for a one-off annual visit in 2026, with a few firms quoting up to £300. A service plan that bundles the visit with warranty cover and call-outs runs from about £10–£20 a month, and some manufacturer contracts cost more than that.

The number matters less than the reason you are paying it. For most owners the real driver is the warranty, not safety or the law, and that changes what a fair price looks like and whether a plan earns its keep.

I am a building surveyor and retrofit assessor. I do not install or service heat pumps, and I am not F-gas certified, so I have no stake in who you hire. What follows is what the published figures say, where they disagree, and what I would check before signing anything.

The short answer

Prices were checked on 11 October 2026. The headline range comes from independent 2026 guides that broadly agree: Reonic (updated 8 September 2026) puts an air source service at £120–£180, Heatable and Book a Builder both say £100–£200, and Switch Together is higher at £150–£300. Taken together I would budget £120–£200 and treat anything above £250 as a quote to question.

Plan prices vary more than visit prices. Octopus lists its Cosy Care plan at £144 a year (£12 a month), and says that without a plan its annual service costs £275. At the other end, Renewable Heating Hub reported a homeowner paying £345 a year for a Daikin Gold service contract; that is one person’s experience, not a published price list, so treat it as a ceiling rather than a norm.

What drives the price

Air or ground source. A ground source unit has a ground loop, a bigger indoor unit and more to check. Expect £200–£350.

Who does it. A manufacturer-approved engineer or the original installer usually costs more than a local independent, but some warranties name who is allowed to do the work. Check before you shop around.

Cylinder and extras. If you have an unvented hot water cylinder, Mitsubishi Electric’s guidance includes servicing it to the G3 regulations, which may be a separate line on the bill. A buffer tank, a second heating zone or a ground loop all add time.

Region. Kent, Sussex, Surrey and London call-out rates tend to sit toward the top of the national ranges, so budget at the upper end if you are in my patch.

What a service should actually include

The better-documented lists agree on the core. Outdoor unit: cleaning the heat exchanger, checking the fan and the condensate drain. Water side: system pressure, expansion vessel charge, strainer and magnetic filter, and antifreeze concentration where the system uses it. Electrics and controls: connections, fault log, and a look at the weather compensation curve. NIBE lists up to 37 individual checks and promises a written report; Mitsubishi Electric adds capturing system efficiency data.

Two of those matter more than the rest. The first is the written report, because that is the evidence a warranty claim will ask for. The second is the controls review. In my heat pump running cost guide the biggest lever on your bill is flow temperature and weather compensation, and a service is the one scheduled moment someone competent looks at both. If the engineer leaves without reading the settings, you have bought a clean fan, not a service.

That is not an academic worry. Renewable Heating Hub’s review of manufacturer service guidance noted a gap between the procedures manufacturers publish and what sometimes happens on the day, with short visits and incomplete inspections. Ask how long the engineer expects to be on site and what they will record.

Do you have to service a heat pump?

I could not find any law that requires a domestic heat pump to be serviced. The obligation is contractual. Daikin says on its own website that annual servicing is a requirement to maintain the warranty, and trade guides report the same for Vaillant and Mitsubishi Electric. Reonic adds that extended 10-year warranties generally need the unit registered and serviced every year without a gap, and that missing a year can void a claim, compressor repairs included.

So the honest answer is: if you are inside the warranty period and the warranty says so, pay for the service and keep the report. Once the warranty has ended, the service is good practice rather than a duty, and what it buys you is catching a blocked coil or a drifting pressure before winter.

The F-gas point most people get wrong

Many articles imply that an annual refrigerant check is a legal requirement. For most homes it is not. Under the UK F-gas rules, mandatory leak checks begin at 5 tonnes of CO2 equivalent, once every 12 months for 5 to under 50 tonnes. Divide 5 tonnes by R32’s global warming potential of 675 and you get about 7.4 kg of refrigerant. The NetRegs guidance lists checks as not applicable below 5 tonnes, and Reonic notes most domestic units sit under the threshold.

That does not make refrigerant work a free-for-all. Anyone recovering refrigerant, brazing into the circuit or fault-finding on it needs F-gas certification. But a routine service that looks at the refrigerant side is a performance and condition check, not a statutory leak test. Check your own unit’s nameplate charge and refrigerant type if you want to be sure; R290 (propane) units are not F-gas at all, though they bring their own safety rules.

Service plans: worth it?

It depends on what is bundled. Compare like for like:

  • Plan versus one-off. At £144 a year, Octopus’s plan is cheaper than its own £275 one-off visit, and it adds monitoring and a priority call-back. Against an independent’s £120–£200, the saving disappears unless the extras matter to you.
  • Locked in. The Octopus plan is only open to units it installed, is limited to units under two years old, and uses Octopus engineers only. Many installer plans work the same way, so you may lose the freedom to choose.
  • What the warranty needs. Octopus’s page, as I read it, says the service in its plan is not a condition of its warranty. That is unusual; check yours rather than assuming.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • No written service report. It is your warranty evidence.
  • No mention of controls or flow temperature. A service that skips the settings misses the biggest efficiency lever.
  • A price far below £100. Check the visit includes the water side and a refrigerant condition check, not just a quick look at the outdoor unit.
  • A plan you cannot read. Ask for the terms, the cancellation rules and what is excluded.
  • Claims that a refrigerant check is legally required. For most domestic units that is wrong.
  • Written quotes. Get at least three, with the length of visit and the scope written in.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to service an air source heat pump? Typically £120–£200 for a one-off annual service in 2026, with some firms quoting up to £300. Ground source units cost more, around £200–£350. Service plans bundling the visit with cover run from about £10–£20 a month.

Do heat pumps need to be serviced every year? Not by law, as far as I can find, but most manufacturers make an annual service a condition of the warranty, and Daikin says so on its own website. Missing a year can leave you paying for repairs the warranty would otherwise cover.

Do I need an F-gas certified engineer to service a heat pump? Anyone recovering refrigerant, brazing into the refrigerant circuit or fault-finding on it needs F-gas certification. Mandatory leak checks only start at 5 tonnes of CO2 equivalent, which is about 7.4 kg of R32, more than most domestic units hold.

Is a heat pump service plan worth it? Sometimes. A plan at about £144 a year costs less than a one-off visit at Octopus’s £275 non-plan rate, but plans mostly pay off when they bundle warranty cover and call-outs. Check what you actually get before comparing it to a £120–£200 one-off service.
