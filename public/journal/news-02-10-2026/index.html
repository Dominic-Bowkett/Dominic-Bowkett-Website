Surveyor’s notes
2 October 2026:
licensing row

Landlord trade bodies are lobbying on licensing and the 2030 EPC deadline, a council has issued a Renters’ Rights Act fine, and the Greens want a rent cap. Here is what it changes on the ground in the South East.

Published 2 October 2026 Reading time · 6 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A day of lobbying and a first sign of enforcement. Two landlord bodies are asking government for things, a council has fined a letting agent under the Renters’ Rights Act, and a smaller party has pitched a rent cap. None of it is law, which is exactly why it is worth reading carefully.

Licensing is being lined up for the chop, and the paper trail has to replace it

As Landlord Today reports, Propertymark wants local discretionary licensing schemes removed once the national Private Rented Sector Database is fully operational. The report says that database launches on 15 December 2026 and rolls out nationwide over 12 months. Propertymark points to selective licensing fees above £1,000 per property in several areas, says roughly two-thirds of councils have not prosecuted a landlord in three years, and argues that enforcement should be “concentrated on landlords who are most likely to be operating outside the law”. It also asks for a 20% cap on the share of fees spent on initial administration.

Propertymark is a trade body for agents, so read this as a lobbying position, not a finding. It has a point on cost and on patchy enforcement. But a national database tells a council who the landlord is. It does not tell anyone what condition the house is in. Licensing, for all its faults, is often the thing that sends a competent person through the door.

My bet is that licensing survives in some form in the worst-affected areas, and that condition evidence matters more either way. If you let property, be ready to register when the database opens, and in the meantime get a dated condition report on older stock. Damp, cold and electrics are what a complaint will turn on, whoever ends up inspecting.

The 2030 EPC C deadline needs a plan, and landlords will wait to be given one

Also from yesterday, Property Investor Today covers a parliamentary roundtable, chaired by Clive Betts MP, where the NRLA and Energy Saving Trust called for a clear roadmap on the private rented sector energy standard. NRLA chief executive Ben Beadle is quoted saying that “2030 may sound some way off, but upgrading homes on the scale now needed requires landlords, installers, lenders, local authorities and tenants to start preparing now.” The list of asks runs from installer capacity and accessible finance to advice from the planned Warm Homes Agency and stronger enforcement.

The risk they describe is the one I would worry about too. If everyone waits until 2029, every landlord needs an assessor, an installer and a finance product in the same twelve months. Prices go up and quality goes down. The sensible landlord is the one who finds out now what their EPC says and what it would take to reach C.

The cheap wins come first: loft insulation, draught-proofing, heating controls. They are often the bulk of the gain. If your rating is a low D or an E, the gap to C can be large and the route to it may involve walls or heating, which takes planning. Older solid-walled homes across the Weald need the most care, because insulating them badly can create the damp problem you were trying to avoid. That is what a PAS 2035 assessment is for.

A Renters’ Rights Act fine: a prohibited tenancy and a threat

Property Industry Eye reports that Chesterfield Borough Council fined a letting agent £8,652 for two breaches: issuing a prohibited six-month fixed-term tenancy, and telling tenants to leave after they said they would complain. The fine was reduced to £7,354.20 for prompt payment, and the council also served an improvement notice. The agent was not named. Cllr Jean Innes said “we will take action where landlords and agents fail to comply with their duties”.

Two things stand out. First, the tenants raised concerns about the property and the response was to ask them to leave. That is exactly the retaliation the Act is meant to stop, and councils are clearly willing to say so publicly. Second, the improvement notice shows the usual chain: a complaint leads to an inspection, and the inspection leads to a condition finding. The fine got the headline, but the notice is what costs money.

If you let in the South East, treat every tenant repair request as a record you may one day have to produce. Answer it in writing, fix it, date it. And if your agent is still offering fixed terms, ask them why.

A Green rent cap: noise now, a bargaining chip later

Landlord Today reports that Green Party leader Zack Polanski, speaking at the party conference in Brighton, wants annual rent rises capped at the lowest of CPI, wage growth or 2%, with a three-year “emergency brake” first. It is a conference pitch tied to a by-election, not a government plan, and I would not price it into any decision today.

The reason to notice it anyway is the pressure it puts on the debate. Rent caps elsewhere tend to discourage maintenance and push landlords to sell, and a cap does nothing for the condition of the stock. If a policy like this ever gets traction, the owners with documented, well-maintained and energy-efficient homes are the ones best placed to argue their rents are justified.

What I’d actually do

  1. All landlords: diarise the 15 December 2026 database launch and check whether you will need to register, then keep your property details and compliance certificates in one place.
  2. Landlords with older stock: commission a condition survey now. If a council ever inspects, a dated report and a repair log are your best defence.
  3. Anyone below EPC C: get a current EPC and a costed route to C. Do the cheap measures first, and get an assessment before touching solid walls.
  4. Landlords using agents: ask in writing how they handle tenancy terms and repair requests under the Act. The fine lands on whoever breached it, and you will still be dealing with the complaint.
  5. Everyone: ignore rent-cap headlines until there is a bill. Spend the time on the building instead.
