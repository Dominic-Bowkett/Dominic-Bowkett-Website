A surprising amount of a building surveyor’s working day happens in dust that’s worth taking seriously. Topping up loft insulation for an EPC recommendation means kneeling in loose mineral wool with fibres hanging in the torch beam; lifting a suspended timber floor to check for rot disturbs decades of settled dust and old insecticide treatments; a strip-out inspection on a Victorian terrace throws plaster, old paint and masonry dust into a room with no ventilation to speak of. None of that is exotic industrial exposure — it’s a normal Tuesday — but a cheap paper mask off a hardware shop shelf is very often only rated FFP1 or FFP2, well below what most insulation manufacturers specify as their own minimum.

This sits alongside PPE already covered elsewhere rather than replacing it. Safety glasses fog up fast against an unvalved mask on a warm loft job, which is worth knowing before picking one; work gloves handle the same insulation and demolition materials a respirator is protecting against from the other direction. Anyone already carrying a mould test kit for a damp inspection will recognise the same logic here — a specific tool for a specific airborne risk, not a generic one bought on price alone.

How I judge a respirator mask

Filter class that actually matches the job. FFP3 is the sensible minimum for loose-fill loft insulation, fine demolition dust and old plaster — most insulation manufacturers say as much in their own safety data. FFP2, or the paper mask from the bottom of a toolbox, is built for coarser nuisance dust, not the finer fibres a loft top-up or a strip-out throws into the air.

A seal that holds around glasses and stubble. A mask that gaps at the cheekbone or nose bridge is doing very little, whatever the box claims about filtration efficiency. A mouldable nose clip and a face seal that copes with safety glasses arms matters more day to day than any other spec on the packet.

Valved or not, depending on how hot and how long. A valve vents warm, damp exhaled air straight out rather than through the filter, which cuts fogging and heat build-up on a long loft or crawl space job. It adds nothing to the protection on the way in — valved and unvalved versions of the same mask filter incoming air identically.

Running cost over a working week, not just the shelf price. A box of disposables adds up fast across back-to-back dusty jobs; a reusable half mask with replaceable filters usually costs more upfront and less per job after the first handful of uses.

Comfort over several hours, not five minutes in a shop. Strap adjustment, weight and breathing resistance decide whether a mask stays properly seated through a whole loft and floor-void inspection, or ends up pushed under the chin the moment it gets uncomfortable.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

FFP3 is the real minimum for insulation and demolition dust, not FFP2. A “dust mask” bought from a hardware shop shelf is very often FFP1 or FFP2, which is built for coarser nuisance dust. Most insulation manufacturers specify FFP3 as the minimum for their own loose-fill and blown products, which is a reasonable baseline for demolition and old plaster dust too.

A seal beats a filtration percentage printed on the box. Manufacturer claims about filtration “beyond the EN149 standard” mean very little if stubble or a loose strap leaves a gap at the cheekbone. A snug fit matters more than any marketing number.

Facial hair defeats any half mask, whatever the brand. No amount of premium seal design compensates for a beard breaking contact with skin along the jawline. The only real fix at that point is a loose-fitting or powered respirator instead of a tight-fitting half mask, not a different tight-fitting model.

Valves are about comfort, not protection level. Valved and unvalved versions of the same mask filter incoming air identically — the valve only vents outgoing breath, which cuts fogging and heat, and does nothing to change what’s being filtered on the way in.

This is not asbestos-rated equipment. Every mask here protects against nuisance, insulation and demolition dust. Suspected asbestos-containing materials need work stopped immediately and a specialist assessment under the Control of Asbestos Regulations, not a tighter mask from this list.

Questions I get asked

Is FFP3 overkill for loft insulation, or is FFP2 enough?

Most insulation manufacturers, including the major mineral wool brands, specify FFP3 as the minimum for loose-fill or blown installation, which is a sensible baseline to follow for a top-up inspection too. FFP2 copes fine with coarser nuisance dust, but filters the finer respirable fibres in loft insulation less effectively, which is exactly the size range that matters for lung health.

Can I use one of these masks if I think I’ve found asbestos?

No. Every mask on this list is intended for general nuisance, insulation and demolition dust, not licensed or notifiable asbestos work. If a material’s suspected of containing asbestos, the right response is to stop disturbing it, leave the area, and follow HSE guidance on getting it properly assessed — not to reach for a tighter-fitting mask from this guide.

Do I need a fit test for these to actually work?

A proper seal check before every single use — breathing in sharply and feeling for air leaking in around the edges — matters more for most people than a formal test. Where RPE is provided as a legal requirement rather than a personal choice, though, a qualitative or quantitative fit test is what actually confirms a specific model seals properly on a specific face, since fit varies a surprising amount between otherwise similar masks.

How often should filters or disposable masks be replaced?

A disposable mask gets binned at the end of the job it was opened for, or sooner if it’s become damp, damaged, or noticeably harder to breathe through. Filters on a reusable half mask last across several jobs, but should be swapped the moment breathing resistance climbs, the filter looks visibly clogged, or the mask has been exposed to something it wasn’t rated to filter.

A respirator that actually seals properly matters more on a dusty job than any spec sheet detail — the kind of unglamorous reliability that counts across a full retrofit assessment or a loft inspection ahead of an EPC.
