The best
water leak detectors
and when it’s worth automating the shut-off

Escape of water — a burst pipe, a failed washing machine hose, a dishwasher that quietly weeps under the worktop for a fortnight — is the claim UK insurers pay out on more than almost anything else in the house. Most of the products below just tell you it's happening, which only helps if someone's home to act on the alert. A smaller number stop it happening at all. Six worth knowing about, from a £12 battery alarm to a whole-house system that shuts the water off itself.

The Association of British Insurers puts the figure at around £1.8 million a day paid out on escape-of-water claims, and reckons nearly one in five claims on buildings and contents insurance is for leak damage. A weather-related burst pipe alone averaged over £10,000 to put right. None of that is exotic risk — it's a washing machine hose, a compression joint under a sink, or a stopcock nobody in the house has ever turned. Water finds the cheapest way down through a ceiling and keeps going until something stops it, usually long after it started.

That's the gap these products fill, and it's worth being clear about which job each one does. A leak sensor is a smoke alarm for water: it tells you something is wrong, loudly, and if a phone's involved, from wherever you are. It does nothing to stop the leak. Only an automatic shut-off valve does that, cutting the supply the moment it sees a problem, whether anyone's home or not. Both have a place — a sensor costs less than a round of drinks, a shut-off valve costs more than most will spend, but is the only thing here that prevents the claim rather than just reporting it faster.

Where this connects to survey work: several escape-of-water claims I hear about trace back to a stopcock nobody could find in time, or one so seized it needed a wrench and both hands while the ceiling came down. If a house survey turns up old or inaccessible stopcocks, that's worth fixing before adding a sensor on top of it.

How I judge a water leak detector

What it can actually see. A point sensor only knows about water where its own probe or pads sit — it says nothing about the rest of the house. A whole-house system fitted to the mains sees every drop passing through, which is a fundamentally bigger job than watching one appliance.

Alert versus shut-off. This is the real dividing line. An alarm is only useful if a person hears it and can act — fine for a leak under the kitchen sink while you're in the next room, less useful for the immersion tank while you're on holiday. An automatic shut-off valve acts on its own, which matters most for the leaks nobody's around to hear.

Whether it depends on a hub. Several sensors here only talk to a separate hub rather than wifi directly, which is one more box that needs power and a network connection to keep working, and one more thing to check after a power cut.

Alarm volume and battery life, not app polish. A dashboard with pretty graphs is worth nothing if the siren can't be heard two rooms away, or the battery's been flat for a month without anyone noticing. The plain, dumb alarm that's simply loud is doing its one job properly.

Whether there's a working stopcock to shut anyway. An automatic valve is only as good as the pipework it's fitted to. In an older property with a seized or inaccessible stopcock, that's the thing to sort first.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Placement beats brand. A single expensive sensor in the wrong spot does less than three cheap ones under the appliances that actually fail: washing machine, dishwasher, boiler, and any cold water tank in the loft. Cover the risk before upgrading the hardware.

An alert only works if someone's there to act on it. A phone notification while at work still means water running for hours before anyone gets home. That's the entire case for an automatic shut-off valve over a sensor, for anyone who's out of the house more than they're in it.

Check the stopcock before buying an automatic valve. A shut-off system, smart or otherwise, is only as good as the plumbing it's fitted to. A stopcock that's seized, buried, or simply unknown to the household needs sorting on its own merits regardless of what gets bolted onto it.

"Works with Alexa" is a smaller deal than it sounds. Voice integration is a nice-to-have; a loud, reliable local siren that works even if the wifi is down is the feature that actually matters when nobody's looking at an app.

A hub is one more thing that can fail. Any sensor that depends on a separate hub stops reporting if that hub loses power, wifi, or simply gets unplugged and forgotten in a house move. Standalone, direct-wifi sensors and dumb, hub-free alarms don't have that extra failure point.

Questions I get asked

Will a leak detector actually reduce my home insurance premium? Sometimes, though it's the exception rather than the rule. Hiscox has supplied a free leak-detection device to buildings-insurance customers, and some insurers reduce the excess on escape-of-water claims while a recognised device is fitted. It's worth asking an insurer directly rather than assuming, since the discount schemes that exist aren't universal or automatic.

Do I need a smart home hub for these to work? Only for the Aqara and Tapo point sensors. Grohe's Sense connects to wifi directly, the GuDoQi-style budget alarm needs nothing beyond its own battery, and the whole-house systems have their own built-in connectivity. A hub is convenient if several sensors already share one, but it isn't a requirement across the category.

What's the real difference between a leak alarm and an automatic shut-off valve? An alarm tells you, loudly or via a phone notification, that water is where it shouldn't be. It does nothing else. A shut-off valve, whether a retrofit clamp on an existing stopcock or a professionally installed mains unit, physically stops the water itself, whether or not anyone's home to hear the alert.

Where should sensors actually go? Under the washing machine and dishwasher, behind or under the boiler, under any cold water tank in the loft, under the kitchen sink where the supply pipes and waste connect, and near an unvented cylinder if there is one. Those are the points that fail most often, well ahead of anywhere more exotic.

A sensor or a shut-off valve only ever answers the question of what happens after something starts leaking — if a house survey or full building survey turns up old pipework, a corroded cylinder or a stopcock that doesn't work, that's the thing worth fixing first.
