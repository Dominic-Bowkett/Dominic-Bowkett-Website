Most of my working day is spent alone, in a loft, under a floor or at the top of a ladder, with the client downstairs and the next appointment an hour away. A cut from a rusty nail or a fall from a loft hatch is unlikely, but if it happens nobody is going to hand me a dressing. A first aid kit in the van is the cheapest part of lone-working safety, and the part most often found out of date.

The kits split into two British Standards. BS 8599-1 is the workplace list, in small, medium and large sizes. BS 8599-2 is the travel and vehicle list, shorter and smaller. The sizes are tied to headcount and risk, not to the quality of the box. A sole trader in a van does not need the large kit.

The ranking below comes from manufacturer descriptions and published user feedback. The practical rule: one BS 8599-1 kit where you base yourself, a BS 8599-2 kit in each vehicle, and a diary reminder to check the expiry dates.

How I judge a first aid kit

The standard on the box. BS 8599-1 for a workplace, BS 8599-2 for a vehicle. A kit that says “HSE style” or “travel pack” with no standard number is a bag of plasters, whatever the price.

Size for the people, not the job. Small, medium and large map to numbers of staff and to risk level. A lone surveyor is covered by the small kit; the medium is worth it if you have a second person or an office.

The box. Vans get damp. A hard, closable case keeps dressings dry; a soft pouch does not. Look for a box you can find with a torch in a dark load bay.

Restocking. Dressings, eyewash and some wipes carry an expiry date. A kit whose parts you can buy separately is cheaper over five years than one you replace whole.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Matters: the British Standard number, a closable hard case, and keeping the contents in date. Know where the kit is and tell anyone you work with.

Doesn’t: the piece count on the front of the box. A 200-piece kit is mostly plasters. Trauma kits, tourniquets and defibrillators matter for specific sites, but they are a decision for a risk assessment, not an impulse buy.

Training. A kit is no use if you do not know what to do with it. A one-day emergency first aid at work course matters more than the upgrade to the dearer box.

Questions I get asked

What is the best first aid kit for a van? A BS 8599-2 travel kit in the cab and, if you carry a bigger load, a small BS 8599-1 workplace kit in the load bay. Choose a hard case rather than a pouch so damp does not ruin the dressings.

Do I legally need a first aid kit as a sole trader? The Health and Safety (First-Aid) Regulations 1981 apply to employers, so a sole trader with no staff is not strictly bound by them. In practice clients, insurers and sensible lone-working habits all expect one, and it costs very little.

What does BS 8599-1 actually mean? It is the British Standard that sets the minimum contents for a workplace first aid kit, in small, medium and large sizes. It does not replace the employer’s own risk assessment of what a particular site needs.

How often should I replace the contents? Check at least once a year, and replace anything past its expiry date. Sterile dressings and eyewash are the usual casualties, and a kit left in a hot van ages faster.
