GPS trackers

A GPS tracker doesn’t stop a van being broken into — a decent lock and an alarm do more of that work — but it answers the question that matters most once it’s gone: where is it now, and is it still moving. For a self-employed surveyor carrying several thousand pounds of thermal cameras, laser levels and diagnostic kit in the back, that’s not a small thing. Here’s what to look for and six trackers worth fitting, from a £25 magnetic unit that lives under a wheel arch to a hardwired system with an optional remote immobiliser.

The back of a survey van is worth more than the van itself. A thermal camera, a couple of laser levels, a damp meter, a borescope, ladders and the odd EPC gadget add up to several thousand pounds of kit that a thief only has to recognise by the badge on the door. A GPS tracker doesn’t stop that break-in — a decent slamlock or lock protector does more of that work — but it answers the one question that actually matters once the van’s gone: where is it now, and is it still moving.

Every tracker in this guide needs an active data connection to do that job properly — a SIM card, built in or supplied, reporting position back over the mobile network in something close to real time. That’s the trade-off against a passive tag: genuine live tracking, geofence alerts and a movement notification the moment the van starts, rather than a last-known position from whoever’s phone happened to walk past. It costs a modest monthly, annual or multi-year plan on top of the hardware, which is worth budgeting for before comparing device prices alone.

None of the units below have been bolted under an actual wheel arch or wired into an actual ignition feed on a job of mine — what follows is built from manufacturers’ specifications and the recurring themes in owner feedback, filtered through what a working surveyor’s van needs rather than what a forty-vehicle fleet needs. How I judge a GPS tracker for a van

Hardwired or magnetic. A hardwired tracker runs off the vehicle’s own power, so it never needs charging and stays live for the life of the van — the trade-off is a proper two-wire install, either done yourself or by a fitter. A magnetic or portable unit is tool-free and can move between vehicles in seconds, but it only works for as many days as its battery lasts and needs remembering to charge.

Subscription cost and how it’s structured. Every tracker here needs an ongoing SIM or data plan to report position live — treat the device price as half the story. Monthly and annual plans cost more per month but keep you free to walk away; long multi-year plans look cheap per month but tie the van’s tracking to one supplier for years.

The alerts that actually matter on a work van. A geofence alert (the van leaves a set area outside working hours), a movement or tow alert, and an unauthorised-start alert do the real work. An instant push notification the moment something happens beats a slicker app that only tells you if you think to open it.

Update frequency, kept in proportion. A tracker reporting every ten seconds sounds precise, but a position every one to two minutes is plenty to follow a moving van in the minutes after a theft. The real difference between trackers is how reliably the signal gets through, not how often it tries.

Battery life and where it has to hide. A magnetic tracker tucked under a wheel arch or chassis rail is only useful for as many days as the battery actually lasts under real movement, not the optimistic standby figure on the box — and the mounting has to survive potholes and pressure washing without working loose.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Hardwired beats magnetic for a daily driver. A magnetic tracker is only as reliable as the person remembering to charge it. For a van that goes out every working day, a hardwired unit that draws power from the vehicle itself removes that entire failure mode — it’s the same reason a hardwired dash cam beats a battery one for parking-mode cover.

Remote immobilisation is a genuine feature, not a gimmick — used correctly. Being able to stop a stationary van restarting is a real deterrent once it’s recovered or during a confrontation, but it’s explicitly a stationary-vehicle feature. No system here is meant to cut an engine while the van is moving, and it shouldn’t be treated as one.

A ten-second reporting interval sells better than it performs. For following a van in the minutes after a theft, a location every one or two minutes is functionally identical to one every ten seconds — what actually matters is whether the signal gets through reliably, not how often the device tries to send it.

A long lock-in contract looks cheap per month and isn’t. Multi-year subscription plans bring the monthly cost down, which is exactly what makes them easy to sell, but they tie a van’s tracking to one supplier’s app and network for years. A monthly or annual plan costs more day to day but keeps the option to switch if the service or coverage disappoints.

None of the trackers here are Thatcham-accredited. Insurer-recognised Thatcham Category S5/S7 trackers exist, cost considerably more, and need professional fitting — a different category aimed at high-value vehicles and fleet insurance discounts. The trackers in this guide are aimed at the practical job of knowing where the van is, not at meeting an insurer’s accreditation list, and are priced accordingly.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a tracker if the van already has good locks and a dash cam?

Yes — they cover three different moments, not one. Slamlocks and lock protectors slow down or defeat the break-in itself; a dash cam with parking mode records evidence at the point of attack; a GPS tracker answers the question neither of those can, which is where the van actually is once it’s gone. Each layer covers a gap the others leave open.

Can I just use an Apple AirTag instead of a proper tracker?

Not really, for van theft specifically. An AirTag relies on Bluetooth and Apple’s crowd-sourced Find My network rather than genuine live GPS reporting, so it works well in a busy city and patchily on a rural lane where fewer iPhones pass by to relay a position. It also has no continuous tracking or geofence alert — you check it, it doesn’t proactively warn you. Worth knowing the other side of that coin too: thieves themselves have started planting AirTags on vehicles they’re scouting, so an unexpected one turning up isn’t always yours.

Do GPS trackers actually get stolen vans back?

They meaningfully improve the odds rather than guarantee anything. A live position reported to the police promptly gives a real chance of recovery before a van is stripped or moved on; a tracker sat unnoticed in a glovebox is easy for a thief to find and bin, while one hidden under a chassis rail or wired in out of sight is much harder to locate and disable in the time a thief usually has. No tracker here is a substitute for good locks in the first place.

Will fitting a tracker reduce my van insurance?

Sometimes, but usually only for the pricier Thatcham-accredited category rather than the trackers in this guide. It’s worth asking an insurer directly what they recognise before buying on the promise of a discount — the practical trackers here are bought for genuine location and recovery, and any insurance saving on top of that should be treated as a bonus rather than the reason to buy.

A tracker is one layer in protecting what’s in the van, not the whole answer — it works best alongside proper van security locks and a dash cam that covers the doors while parked.
