Damp proof paint
and anti-mould paint:
does either actually work?

Every damp patch and every run of black mould eventually sends someone looking for a tin that fixes it in one coat. Nothing reviewed here does that — nothing in a tin does. “Damp proof paint” is one of the more misleading names on a DIY shelf. Most people searching it have just found a patch of damp or a run of black mould and want the single-coat fix the name seems to promise. Read the small print on any of these tins and none of them claim to cure rising damp, penetrating damp, or a leaking gutter — nothing in a tin does that. What they actually do, and do reasonably well when the right one's picked for the right job, is block an old stain from bleeding through fresh decoration, or resist mould regrowing on a wall that's staying slightly damp regardless. That's a real, useful job. It just isn't the job most buyers think they're getting.

On survey work I see the aftermath of this more often than the cause. A bedroom ceiling painted brilliant white over a brown tide mark that's back within six months. A bathroom wall refreshed in anti-mould emulsion while the extractor fan hasn't run in years. The paint isn't lying — the tin says “resists mould growth” or “blocks stains,” not “cures damp” — but it's an easy product to reach for instead of doing the actual diagnosis, and an efficient way to make a problem invisible for a few months, which matters rather a lot if a house is about to go on the market.

There are two places these products earn a genuine spot on a shopping list: as the mould-resistant finish coat once the real cause — usually poor ventilation, sometimes a fixed leak — is actually dealt with, and as part of a proper tanking system on a below-ground wall, where a purpose-built waterproofer is doing structural work rather than decorative work. Below is how to tell those two jobs apart, six products worth having depending on which one applies, and where I think the marketing oversells what's actually in the tin.

How I judge a damp proof or anti-mould paint

What it actually claims, printed on the tin. “Resists mould growth for five years” and “permanently blocks damp stains” are two different, narrower promises than “damp proof.” The honest products here are specific about which one they're making; treat any packaging that implies it cures damp itself with real suspicion.

Decorative finish versus structural waterproofer. This is the single biggest distinction in the whole category. A mould-resistant emulsion is a decorative topcoat doing a cosmetic job on a wall that's basically sound. A tanking-grade waterproofer is formulated to physically resist water pressure through bare masonry and belongs in a below-ground wall system, applied as part of a proper job rather than brushed on as decoration.

The mould-resistance mechanism, and its limits. A biocide built into the paint film discourages mould from colonising the surface, for a manufacturer-stated number of years. It only works on the top, exposed layer — paint over it with an ordinary emulsion later and the protection is gone, which is an easy mistake for a decorator or landlord to make without realising.

Stain-blocking as a separate job from mould-resistance. Some of these products only stop an old tide mark or mould scar bleeding through fresh paint or paper — they carry no claim about resisting future mould at all. That's a genuinely useful, narrow job once the cause behind the mark is dealt with; it says nothing about preventing it coming back if the cause isn't.

What it can go over, and what happens if the real problem isn't fixed. Coverage claims, damp-tolerant application (some can go on a wall that isn't fully dry yet) and substrate compatibility with plaster, masonry or old paintwork all matter for the job in hand. None of it changes what happens if the underlying moisture keeps coming: blistering, peeling, and the same stain working its way back through, whatever's written on the tin.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The paint film only protects itself, not what's under it. Paint an ordinary emulsion over a mould-resistant or stain-blocking product later, whether that's a decorator freshening up a rental or a new owner redecorating, and the protection stops at that layer. It's an easy, invisible mistake, and one reason a “mould-free for six years” guarantee often doesn't survive contact with the next tenant.

“Damp proof paint” has nothing to do with a damp proof course. The name invites the assumption that it works like a chemical injection DPC or a physical membrane. It doesn't — at best it's decorative sealing over a surface. Genuine rising or penetrating damp needs proper diagnosis and treatment; see rising damp treatment costs and damp-proofing costs for what that actually involves.

A cellar or basement wall needs a system, not a coat. Even a genuine tanking-grade product like Watertite is one part of a considered waterproofing scheme — correct preparation, sometimes a full tanking slurry or membrane, proper falls and drainage where water's actively present. A DIY coat on a wet cellar wall without addressing the water table or external drainage tends to fail, or simply pushes the damp sideways to whatever's left untreated.

Condensation-driven mould needs ventilation fixed first, or it comes straight back. Anti-mould paint on a bathroom ceiling with an extractor fan that's broken, or never switched on, delays the regrowth by months rather than years. A dehumidifier and working extraction address the humidity; the paint is what goes on afterwards, not instead.

What salesmen push that you can skip. Multi-tin “systems” bundling a primer, an additive and a topcoat for a straightforward domestic bathroom ceiling are usually paying for marketing rather than performance. A longer stated guarantee — five years versus six — is a smaller factor in whether mould actually stays away than whether the room gets ventilated; that's worth more attention than the number on the tin.

Questions I get asked

Will damp proof paint stop rising damp? No. Rising damp is moisture moving up through a wall from the ground, usually because a damp-proof course has failed or was never installed, and it needs a proper diagnosis and, where confirmed, a chemical injection course or equivalent physical treatment. A tin of paint sits on the surface and does nothing to that mechanism; see rising damp treatment costs for what the actual fix involves.

What's the difference between anti-mould paint and damp-seal or stain-block paint? Anti-mould paint is a finish coat containing a biocide that discourages mould from colonising its own surface for a stated number of years. Damp-seal or stain-block paint is an undercoat that stops an old mark bleeding through fresh decoration — it carries no claim about resisting new mould once something else is painted over it. They solve different, narrower problems than either name suggests.

Can I use anti-mould paint in a cellar? As a cosmetic finish, once genuine waterproofing or tanking has already been done, yes. On an untreated, actively wet cellar wall, no — that needs a tanking-grade product like Watertite applied as part of a proper waterproofing system, ideally specified and fitted by someone who's assessed where the water's actually coming from.

Does a “5 year” or “6 year” mould-resistance guarantee mean anything? It's a genuine manufacturer performance claim under stated test conditions, not a warranty against your particular damp problem returning. It typically assumes correct surface preparation and that the paint stays the exposed, uncoated surface throughout. It says nothing about what happens if the humidity or leak behind the mould was never addressed.
