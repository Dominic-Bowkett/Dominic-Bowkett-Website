The best
safety harnesses
for survey work

Most survey work never goes near a harness — a two-storey semi with sound access doesn’t call for one. Flat roof inspections from podium steps or a scaffold tower do, and so does going up a client’s existing scaffold that’s already standing for other trades. Turning up without one on those jobs means the visit doesn’t happen, or it happens without the protection the situation calls for. Here’s the difference between a fall-arrest harness and a work-restraint harness, and seven worth having in the van for when a stepladder isn’t enough.

A harness earns its place in the van on a specific, fairly narrow set of jobs: a flat roof that needs a close look and can only be reached from a podium or a scaffold tower, a chimney stack inspection where the client’s builder has already got scaffold up, or a retrofit assessment where the insulation being checked is above what a stepladder can safely reach. It is worth being clear from the start about which of two different jobs a harness is being bought for, because they call for genuinely different kit. Work restraint means the lanyard is set short enough that the edge or opening physically cannot be reached — the harness prevents the fall from being possible at all. Fall arrest means getting close to the edge is allowed, and the harness, lanyard and shock absorber catch and limit the fall if it happens. For almost all survey work, restraint is the right category. A genuine fall-arrest scenario near an open roof edge is a situation to avoid being in at all during a routine inspection, not one to plan a rescue from.

The other driver, alongside the physical risk, is what turning up without one actually costs. The Work at Height Regulations put the duty on whoever’s doing the work to assess it properly, and that increasingly shows up before a survey even starts — a scaffold hire firm with a “no harness, no access” policy, an insurer’s expectation that a method statement covers it, or simply not wanting to be the surveyor stood on a flat roof edge with nothing on. None of that means every job needs one. It means the ones that do need it sorted before the van leaves, not improvised on the doorstep.

How I judge a safety harness

Attachment points — one for fall arrest, two for anything more flexible. A single rear D-ring is the minimum for fall arrest and nothing else. A second, front-mounted sternal point adds positioning, ladder-climbing systems or a rescue connection — worth having if the same harness needs to cover more than one situation.

Buckles — quick-release against traditional pass-through. A quick-release buckle earns its keep when a harness goes on and off several times in a day; for something that comes out once a week, a traditional pass-through buckle costs less and does the same job once it’s adjusted.

Fit range across a working week of different people. Universal sizing with a wide adjustment range matters more than a precise fit for one body type, particularly if more than one person in the practice might end up wearing it.

What’s actually in the box. A harness alone needs a compatible lanyard and karabiners bought separately and checked against its instructions; a full kit removes that decision at the cost of a higher price and less flexibility if the lanyard needs replacing on its own.

EN361 conformity and the paperwork that comes with it. Every harness worth buying carries EN361 and CE or UKCA marking — that’s the baseline, not a differentiator. What’s worth checking is the inspection record card or digital log, because that’s what an insurer or a principal contractor actually asks to see.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Fall arrest and restraint are not the same job — check which one is actually needed. A restraint lanyard set short enough that the edge can’t physically be reached is the right tool for a roof inspection. A fall-arrest lanyard that allows getting close to the edge and catches a fall if it happens is a different risk category entirely, and not one worth being in for a routine visual check.

A quick-release buckle earns its cost only if the harness goes on and off more than once a day. For a harness that lives in the van and comes out for one job in a week, the saving over a traditional pass-through buckle is marginal, and the traditional buckle is usually the cheaper, more common option.

The lanyard matters as much as the harness — check what’s actually in the box. A harness-only purchase needs a compatible lanyard and karabiners bought separately and checked together. A kit removes that decision but usually costs more and offers less flexibility if one component wears out before the other.

EN361 marking is the baseline, not a selling point. Every harness in this guide carries it. What’s worth checking is the inspection record — a printed card or a QR-linked digital log — because that’s what an insurer or a principal contractor actually asks to see, not the CE mark itself.

Comfort matters less than it does for a scaffolder wearing one all day. None of the padding differences here will be felt on a ten-minute climb up a scaffold tower — they matter far more on the rare job that keeps someone in a harness for hours.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a harness for a building survey?

For most building surveys, no — a stepladder and a straightforward loft hatch don’t call for one. It becomes necessary when a flat roof or a chimney stack needs inspecting via podium steps or a scaffold tower, or when accessing a client’s existing scaffold put up for other trades. On any job where a harness is the right call, it should be part of the method statement decided before the visit, not worked out on the doorstep when the access turns out to be more than expected.

What’s the difference between fall arrest and work restraint?

Restraint means the lanyard is set short enough that the wearer physically cannot reach an edge or opening — the harness prevents the fall from being possible at all. Fall arrest means the wearer can get close to an edge, and the harness, lanyard and shock absorber catch and limit the fall if it happens. For a domestic survey, restraint is almost always the right category — a genuine fall-arrest scenario near a roof edge is a situation to avoid being in at all, not one to plan a rescue from.

Can any harness be used with any lanyard?

Only if the attachment points and karabiner gate size match what the lanyard is rated for, and the combination has been checked together rather than assumed. A harness bought separately from its lanyard needs both sets of instructions checked for compatibility. A kit like the Miller Titan or the Delta Plus ELARA130V2 above removes that guesswork by supplying a combination already tested and rated together.

How often does a safety harness need replacing or inspecting?

A visual check before every use — webbing for cuts, fraying or chemical damage, buckles and D-rings for cracking or corrosion — plus a formal inspection at least every twelve months by a competent person. Most manufacturers also give a harness a shelf life regardless of visible wear, typically around ten years from the date of manufacture printed inside it. Anything that’s sat in a van through several UK summers and winters is worth checking against that date rather than assumed fine because it looks fine.

A safety harness only earns its place in the van on the surveys where a podium, scaffold tower or existing scaffold means going where a stepladder can’t reach — building surveys with a flat roof to check, or a PAS 2035 retrofit assessment where the insulation’s being inspected from height.
