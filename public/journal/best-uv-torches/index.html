A UV torch isn’t something most surveyors reach for on every visit, but it earns its place for one specific job: making an invisible repair visible. Paint, filler, plaster, mastic and adhesive all fluoresce slightly differently under UV depending on age and formulation, even when a patch has been colour-matched perfectly to the eye. A ceiling skimmed over a crack, a window reveal with fresh mastic run round it, a section of render made good — a UV torch across the surface in a darkened room turns a five-second check into either “nothing to see here” or “worth asking the vendor about.”

The second job is pest and damp evidence in voids — lofts, under-stair cupboards, suspended timber floors. Rodent urine trails and old droppings fluoresce under UV far more readily than they show up under a head torch alone, and it’s a two-minute addition to a loft hatch check that a borescope can’t replicate from a single inspection hole. Some historic leak staining reacts to UV too, adding a second data point alongside a damp meter reading rather than replacing one.

How I judge a UV torch

A genuine UV wavelength, not a violet LED wearing the label. 365nm is nearer the standard used for serious fluorescence work; a lot of torches sold as “UV” actually sit at 395–405nm, which is really the edge of visible violet light. They still make some things glow, but the fluorescence is weaker and harder to distinguish from the torch’s own visible-light spill — the single biggest quality difference in this category, and the one most listings bury in the small print.

Flood versus throw. A wide, even flood beam is what’s needed for sweeping a ceiling or a run of skirting for repair evidence. A tighter, further-reaching beam matters more for scanning a full loft depth or a garden boundary from one spot rather than walking the whole area. A zoomable head that does both is worth the extra cost over a fixed one.

Output and battery life that survives being forgotten about. This is an occasional-use tool, which cuts both ways: a torch that’s flat when it’s finally needed is worse than useless, but a rechargeable cell that’s topped up before every job is no trouble at all. AA-powered torches never leave a surveyor stuck if there’s a spare battery in the van bag; rechargeable ones are more convenient day to day if the habit of charging them sticks.

Size, and whether it actually lives in a pocket. A torch that’s carried every day gets used for the quick checks it’s good for; one that lives in a case at the bottom of the van bag only comes out once it’s already a known issue, by which point a lot of the value of a five-second check has already been lost.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

365nm versus 395nm is the single biggest factor, more than LED count or claimed lumens. A torch with fifty LEDs at 395nm is still a 395nm torch — more LEDs makes a wider, brighter version of the same weaker fluorescence, not a genuine UV upgrade. Listings rarely make the wavelength obvious unless it’s in the product name, so it’s worth checking before LED count or brightness claims.

Darkness matters more than the torch. Even a genuine 365nm beam struggles to show faint fluorescence in daylight or a bright hallway. The best results come from checking a loft, void or room with the lights off and any daylight blocked, not from buying a stronger torch to compensate for a bright room.

A UV torch is a lead, not proof. A repair, a stain or a trail that fluoresces tells a surveyor where to look closer — with a borescope, a damp meter, or simply better light — not what the material actually is or how old it is. It’s a pointer, the same as a stud finder or a cable detector, not a diagnosis in itself.

Battery type is a genuine trade-off, not just a spec line. Rechargeable is convenient until it’s flat on the one day it’s needed. AA or AAA power is old-fashioned but never leaves a surveyor without a working torch if there’s a spare battery in the van bag — worth weighing against how reliably a rechargeable one actually gets put on charge.

Questions I get asked

What does a UV torch actually show a surveyor that a normal torch doesn’t?

Materials and repairs that fluoresce differently under UV even when they’re colour-matched to the eye — mastic, filler, some paints and adhesives, which can flag a recent patch repair before a sale. It also picks up rodent urine trails and old droppings in lofts and voids far more readily than normal light, and some historic leak staining reacts under UV too. None of it is visible under a standard torch or head torch.

Is 365nm or 395nm better for finding stains and repairs?

365nm is nearer the wavelength genuinely used for fluorescence work, and it produces a noticeably stronger glow on faint or old evidence. 395–405nm torches are cheaper and still show up obvious, fresh staining well enough, but they struggle with anything faint, old or subtle — exactly the cases where a UV torch is actually earning its keep over the naked eye.

Can a UV torch replace a damp meter or a borescope?

No — it’s a lead-generation tool, not a measurement one. A damp meter gives a reading, a borescope gives a view inside a void or cavity; a UV torch just flags where on a surface or in a space to look closer with one of those tools, or with better lighting and a longer look.

Are UV torches safe to use indoors, in front of a client?

The UV-A output on handheld torches like these is low power and brief exposure isn’t a real hazard for a quick sweep of a ceiling or a loft — no PPE is needed. The only sensible precaution is the obvious one: don’t shine any torch directly into someone’s eyes at close range, UV or otherwise.
