Window restrictors come up in two very different conversations. One is a parent or a landlord worried about a child falling from a first-floor sash. The other is a fire risk assessor worried that the same restrictor will stop a tenant getting out of a bedroom. Both are right, and the restrictor that answers one can fail the other, which is why the choice is less about brand and more about which window it goes on.

The 100mm figure is the common thread. Approved Document K, covering protection from falling, works on the principle that an opening should not allow a gap of more than 100mm where there is a fall risk, and child-resistant devices are made to BS EN 16281. HMO guidance from local authorities then adds the fire side: an upper-floor window with a low sill may need a limiter, but one an adult can override in an emergency, and a window relied on as a means of escape needs an unobstructed opening of at least 0.33m² with one dimension of at least 450mm. The fire risk assessment cost guide covers who decides which windows are which, the HMO licence cost guide explains where licence conditions come in, and the snagging survey cost guide is relevant if you are checking restrictors on a new build handover.

How I judge a window restrictor

Which window is it for? A window a person may need to climb out of in a fire is a different case from a window over a patio. The restrictor must match, and on an escape window it must be releasable by an adult without a key search.

Does it limit the opening to 100mm? The listing should state the cable length or the opening it leaves. “Restrictor” alone is not a specification, and a slack cable can leave a larger gap than the figure suggests.

Key, keyless or override? Key release is child-resistant and tenant-hostile in a fire. Keyless is quick and less child-resistant. Some HMO conditions say outright that a limiter must be overridable by an adult.

Does it suit the frame? uPVC casements take cables, hinged stays take hinge types, timber sashes need stops and sliders need clamps. The wrong type is a wasted parcel.

How is it fixed? A restrictor is only as strong as the screws in the frame. Thin uPVC profiles and old timber need the right fixing, and the screws supplied are often the weak point.

The picks in detail

01

ERA lockable cable window restrictor

Best overall

ERA is a long-established UK window-hardware brand, and its lockable cable restrictor is the type most landlords mean when they say “window restrictor”: a short steel cable that holds the sash to the frame, released with a key. Manufacturer information describes it as suitable for uPVC, timber or aluminium, and the cable length is what holds the opening under the 100mm figure. The catch for a rented house is the key. A tenant who cannot find it in a smoke-filled room cannot use that window, so it belongs on windows that are not the escape route.

For

  • Fits uPVC, timber and aluminium casements
  • Key release means a child cannot simply undo it
  • Familiar trade brand with straightforward fixing

Against

  • Needs a key to release, so wrong for a designated escape window
  • Check the listing states the opening it limits, not just “restrictor”

03

Keyless cable window restrictor

Best for possible escape windows

A keyless restrictor has a push-button or squeeze release on the cable, so an adult can open the window fully without hunting for a key. That is where this type becomes relevant to fire safety: council HMO guidance tends to say a limiter on an upper-floor window should be capable of being overridden by an adult, and a key-locked cable fails that test on the night. The compromise is that an older child can learn the release, so it suits an adult’s bedroom better than a nursery.

For

  • Opens fully in seconds with no key
  • Keeps a day-to-day restricted opening and still allows escape
  • No key to lose or hang on a hook

Against

  • Less child-resistant than a key lock
  • Quality varies across generic listings, so read the owner feedback

04

Hinge-type window restrictor, pair

Neatest finish

Hinge-type restrictors sit on the friction stay and stop the sash at a set angle, so there is no cable across the glass. On an outward-opening casement they look tidier and are harder to cut or unhook. Sizing is the trap: the stay length and the profile must match, and a wrong guess means a returned parcel. Check the stay arm length against the product before ordering.

For

  • No visible cable across the opening
  • Harder to tamper with than a plain cable
  • Better looking on a modern frame

Against

  • Must match the hinge profile and stay length
  • Fiddlier to fit than a cable

05

Sash window restrictor stops, pack

Best for sash windows

Victorian and Edwardian sash windows do not take cable restrictors well, and the usual fix is a screw-in sash stop that blocks the lower sash at about 100mm. The packs are cheap and quick. In a period house I would also check the sash cords and the state of the timber before screwing anything into a rotten frame; a restrictor does not cure a window that is already failing.

For

  • Cheap and quick on timber sashes
  • Suits the traditional window rather than fighting it
  • Easy to remove if the use of the room changes

Against

Lockable window handle with key

Best window lock

A key-locking handle is a security device, not a restrictor: it stops the window being opened from outside, and keeps a toddler from opening it, but it does nothing to limit the opening once unlocked. The two searches get lumped together, so it is worth saying plainly that they are different jobs. Ground-floor and accessible windows want a lock; upper-floor windows near a low sill want a restrictor. Many landlords need both.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Fire first, then falls. If a window is the way out, a restrictor you cannot release is a hazard. If it is not, the restrictor is doing its job. The fire risk assessment, not the packaging, decides which is which.

Fitting and checking. Fix with screws the frame can hold, check the opening is under 100mm with a rule, and look again after a year, since cables fray and screws loosen. Keep a note with the rest of the compliance paperwork; the first-time landlord kit is the wider checklist.

Security versus restriction. A window lock keeps people out; a restrictor limits how far it opens. Many houses need both, and they are different products.

The rest of the escape picture. A working escape route also needs smoke and heat alarms, fire door closers and strips, emergency lighting and, for access, key safes.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. Brass-effect finishes, multi-packs of identical cables for windows that differ, and “heavy duty” claims with no standard behind them. The restrictor that matters is the one matched to the window and the person.

Questions I get asked

Do I have to fit window restrictors in a rented house? There is no blanket rule for every let. Restrictors are expected where there is a fall risk to children, and some HMO licence conditions require them on upper-floor windows with low sills. A fire risk assessment will say whether a given window should be restricted or kept free as an escape route.

What is the 100mm rule? It is the limit on the gap a restricted window should leave, taken from Approved Document K and widely used in guidance, so that a young child cannot get through. Measure at the narrowest practical point, with the cable pulled tight.

Can tenants still escape through a restricted window? Only if the restrictor can be released quickly by an adult, or the window is not relied on as an escape. A key-only restrictor on a bedroom escape window is the failure I most often see flagged.

Can I fit restrictors myself? Usually yes on a casement, with a drill and the right screws. On old timber sashes, listed buildings or leasehold flats, check the freeholder and the conservation position first, since you are altering the window.

Windows and escape routes are among the first things I look at in a rented house, and a locked bedroom restrictor, or a missing one, is a quick fix before it becomes a licence or insurance problem.
