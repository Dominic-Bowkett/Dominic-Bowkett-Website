Most of the heating controls I see on an EPC are a single room thermostat wired to a two-position relay: call for heat, boiler fires at full output, thermostat satisfied, boiler stops. A smart thermostat swaps that box for one with a screen and an app, but the on/off relay logic often survives underneath unless the unit genuinely supports OpenTherm — a digital communication link between thermostat and boiler that lets the boiler modulate its flow temperature continuously, instead of firing at one fixed setting and cycling. That's the spec worth checking before anything else on this list, and it's the one most affiliate roundups skip past in favour of app screenshots and voice-control claims.

Weather compensation is the other term used loosely. Properly, it means an outdoor sensor feeding the boiler's flow temperature down as the outside air warms, so the system runs a lower, steadier temperature on a mild day rather than blasting full heat then cycling off. A couple of the thermostats below genuinely do this; several others just adjust the schedule against a forecast instead of a live outdoor reading — a real feature, but a different one, worth knowing which you're actually being sold.

None of this replaces a competent installer at the boiler end. Fitting the thermostat is usually straightforward; enabling OpenTherm on the boiler's own board is not always a DIY job, and not every boiler supports it even where the thermostat does. And if the real complaint is individual rooms rather than the whole system, that's a smart radiator valve, not a thermostat — see my smart radiator valves guide for that half of the job. Here's how I'd judge a thermostat, and the seven I'd look at.

How I judge a smart thermostat

OpenTherm confirmed, not assumed. A thermostat that modulates the boiler is a genuinely different product from one that just cycles a relay with a nicer app on top. Check the listing states OpenTherm explicitly, and check the boiler itself has an OpenTherm connection (A/B terminals) and an OpenTherm Association licence — not every boiler on the market does, whatever the thermostat can do.

Weather compensation or weather-adjusted scheduling — which one. True weather compensation reads an outdoor sensor and adjusts the boiler's flow temperature live. A forecast-based schedule tweak is a different, softer feature some brands market under a similar name. Ask which is actually being sold.

Hub cost, and whether it's already in the box. Several thermostats here won't function without a separate hub, a cost that rarely appears in the headline price; others pair directly with a wireless receiver and need nothing else.

Heat pump compatibility stated outright, not implied. Most of this category is still built and marketed around gas and combi boilers. If a heat pump is on the cards, look for a brand that names it explicitly rather than a vague “works with most systems” line.

What it needs from the existing wiring. A battery-powered thermostat talking to a mains-wired receiver is the most common arrangement here; confirm this before ordering if the old unit's cabling is limited.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

OpenTherm needs a compatible boiler, not just a compatible thermostat. The boiler itself needs an OpenTherm connection (A/B terminals) and an OpenTherm Association licence to modulate — check the boiler's manual, not just the thermostat's box. If a boiler replacement is due anyway, see what a new boiler costs and ask for OpenTherm compatibility while you're at it.

Weather compensation and “weather adaptation” are not the same feature. True weather compensation reads an outdoor sensor and adjusts the boiler's flow temperature live as conditions change. A forecast-based schedule tweak, which several brands market under a similar-sounding name, is a real feature but a softer one. Ask which is actually on offer.

Hub cost is often missing from the headline price. Wiser, Aqara-style ecosystems and some Hive bundles need a separate hub before the smart features work at all — factor that in before comparing one price against another.

It's whole-system control, not per-room. A single thermostat's own sensor decides when the boiler fires for the whole house. If the actual complaint is one room running hot while another stays cold, that's a job for a smart radiator valve on the individual radiator, not a cleverer thermostat — see my smart radiator valves guide for that.

What the marketing pushes that matters less than it sounds. A colour touchscreen, voice control and an “AI learning” claim are all pleasant to have but don't change whether the boiler is genuinely modulating. Ask about OpenTherm and weather compensation before the app screenshots.

Questions I get asked

Will a smart thermostat improve my EPC rating? It can, but only if it's a genuine upgrade in heating control — RdSAP recognises specific categories for weather or load compensation, distinct from a plain programmer and room thermostat. An app-controlled thermostat still running a basic on/off relay behind the scenes doesn't move that line item; a genuinely OpenTherm-modulating one can.

What's the real difference between OpenTherm and weather compensation? OpenTherm is the communication link that lets a compatible boiler modulate its flow temperature instead of firing on and off. Weather compensation is a specific way of deciding what that flow temperature should be, based on an outdoor sensor reading rather than a fixed schedule. A thermostat can have one without the other, and marketing regularly blurs the two.

Do I need a heating engineer to fit one? Swapping the thermostat itself is within reach of a competent DIY changeover for most of these. Enabling OpenTherm communication on the boiler's own board, or wiring in a new receiver, is a different job — have whoever's competent with the boiler do that, Gas Safe registered if it's a gas appliance.

Should I buy a smart thermostat or a smart radiator valve? A thermostat controls the whole system from one point; a radiator valve controls a single room. Households with an even mix of occupied and empty rooms get more from valves; one mainly frustrated by a dumb on/off boiler gets more from a genuinely OpenTherm-capable thermostat. Many end up wanting both.

I don’t specify or fit heating controls as part of a survey, but they show up on an EPC as a standard recommendation, and whether OpenTherm or a heat pump is the better next step is exactly what a PAS 2035 retrofit assessment works out before money goes on either.
