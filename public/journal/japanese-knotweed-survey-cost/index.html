Japanese knotweed
survey cost
in 2026

A standalone Japanese knotweed survey typically costs £150–£350 in 2026 — the fee for a specialist to visit, confirm what’s actually there, and put a risk category on it in writing. That’s a different question from what treatment costs if the answer comes back positive, which is worth understanding before you book either.

Published 29 Aug 2026 Reading time · 8 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A standalone Japanese knotweed survey typically costs £150–£350 in 2026 — a fixed fee for a specialist to visit, assess what’s actually growing, and confirm it in a written report. That’s a narrower and cheaper question than what treatment costs if the survey comes back positive, which I’ve covered separately. This one is about the diagnosis, not the cure.

What that fee buys varies more than the number suggests. An independent, PCA-accredited surveyor charging £250–£350 has no financial stake in what the report says. A “free” survey from a company that also sells the treatment plan is a different proposition — often genuinely useful, and not something to dismiss automatically, but worth reading the small print on, because the firm doing the diagnosis is frequently the same one quoting the cure.

I flag suspected knotweed as a matter of course on every building survey I carry out, but formally identifying it and putting a RICS Management Category on it is specialist work I refer out, not something I do myself — so I’ve no stake in which surveyor you use or which way the report comes back. What follows is 2026 pricing for the survey itself, what drives the cost, and how to tell an independent assessment from a sales visit with a clipboard.

What drives the price

Plot size and access. A small garden with clear sightlines takes less time to walk and probe than a large or overgrown plot, boundary hedging, or land backing onto a railway cutting or waterway where knotweed commonly spreads from.

Accreditation. Property Care Association (PCA) membership and a RICS-approved surveyor generally sit at the upper end of the range, around £250–£350. Unaccredited or budget outfits can undercut that, sometimes from under £100, but a cheaper report is only worth having if whoever writes it is qualified to assign a proper RICS Management Category, not just say “yes” or “no.”

Independent vs tied to treatment. A specialist who only earns from the survey has priced the visit on its own merits. A firm offering a “free” survey is usually pricing it into the treatment plan they hope follows — which is why the fee tends to reappear if you don’t proceed.

Region. As with most trades in this series, London and the South East commonly run above the rest of the UK for a comparable visit.

Turnaround and report depth. A same-week report for an approaching exchange date, or one with GPS-mapped infestation boundaries and soil probing rather than a visual check alone, generally costs more than a standard two-to-four-week turnaround.

Do you actually need one?

A HomeBuyer or Building Survey will note suspected knotweed if the surveyor sees it, but that's a visual flag, not a formal identification — it carries no RICS Management Category, and it can miss the plant altogether in winter, when the canes die back and there's little above ground to see. A specialist, PCA-accredited survey is the step that actually confirms species, maps the extent, and produces the category a lender or buyer will want in writing.

You're most likely to need one where: a general survey has flagged suspected growth; the seller answered “not known” to the Japanese knotweed question on the standard TA6 property information form; growth is visible on the property or on land adjoining it, including railway land, riverbanks or waste ground where it commonly establishes; or a mortgage valuer has applied a retention pending specialist confirmation.

RICS moved away from the old “seven-metre rule” in 2022, replacing the blanket flag for anything within 7m of a building with four Management Categories, A to D, based on distance, extent and impact rather than a single fixed radius. Category D specifically covers knotweed growing off the subject property but within about 3m of any boundary — meaning a neighbour's infestation can still be relevant even though nothing is growing on your own land. Categories A and B mean action and an insurance-backed guarantee are expected; Category C means it can be managed rather than requiring action, generally with no impact on a mortgage.

The “free survey” catch

This is the one piece of professional judgement worth more than the number on the invoice. A treatment company offering a free knotweed survey isn't being charitable — it's pricing the visit as the first step of a sale, and the terms almost always specify the fee is only waived if you go on to instruct their management plan. Nothing wrong with that as a business model, and plenty of reputable PCA-accredited firms operate this way. The issue is incentive: the same visit that decides whether you need a £1,500–£5,000 treatment plan is being carried out by the company that stands to earn it.

That doesn't make a free survey wrong, but it's a reasonable moment to get a second opinion, particularly where the finding is borderline — a small patch, growth some distance from any boundary, or a Category C assessment that technically requires no action at all. An independent, fee-paying surveyor with nothing to sell afterwards has no reason to talk a marginal case up into a four-figure treatment plan, and £150–£350 for that reassurance is cheap against the alternative.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • PCA membership and RICS approval confirmed — not just claimed on a website; ask for the membership number and check it.
  • A proper Management Category stated, not just a yes/no on whether knotweed is present — A to D, with the reasoning behind it.
  • Written terms on the “free survey” clause — exactly when the fee becomes payable, and how much, if you don’t proceed with treatment.
  • Report turnaround stated upfront, especially against an exchange or completion deadline.
  • Guarantee terms specified in full if a management plan follows — length (usually 5 or 10 years), and whether it’s insurance-backed so it survives the contractor going out of business.
  • No pressure to sign a treatment plan on the day of the survey — a genuine finding will still be there next week.

Questions I get asked

How much does a Japanese knotweed survey cost in 2026? A standalone survey from a PCA-accredited specialist typically costs £150–£350, depending on plot size and location. Many treatment firms also offer a free survey, but the fee is usually only waived if you go on to commission their treatment plan — read the terms before booking.

Does my house survey already cover Japanese knotweed? A HomeBuyer or Building Survey will flag visible growth as a matter of course, but that's a visual note, not a formal identification or RICS Management Category, and it can miss knotweed entirely in winter dieback. If a general survey raises a concern, or a lender applies a retention, a specialist confirmatory survey is the next step.

What happens if the survey confirms Japanese knotweed? Most lenders want a PCA-accredited management plan with an insurance-backed guarantee in place before completion, typically £1,500–£5,000 or more depending on scale. Full excavation, at £5,000–£20,000+, is rarely necessary for an ordinary domestic case.

Is a free Japanese knotweed survey worth having? It can be a reasonable first read, but the firm doing the diagnosis is usually also the one quoting the cure, so a positive finding is worth a second opinion before signing up to a management plan costing well into four figures. An independent, fee-paying surveyor with no treatment to sell has no reason to find more than what's actually there.
