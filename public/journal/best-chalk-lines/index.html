A chalk line reel earns its place in the bag doing something no digital tool does as fast: snapping a genuinely straight, visible reference line across a surface that’s too big, too rough or too far from anywhere sensible to eyeball accurately. Run a straight edge or a laser across a suspended timber floor before checking it for dip, or snap a line across a damp gable wall before setting out a grid of moisture meter readings, and what was a vague “it looks lower over there” becomes a fixed reference every measurement can be checked against.

Most calls for one come down to the same need: a repeatable starting point rather than a guess. Setting out a damp reading grid at regular centres up a suspect wall means every reading has a recorded position rather than wherever the probe happened to land; marking a datum line before recording floor level deviations gives the readings something fixed to work from; snapping reference lines across a slab before checking it for fall, or marking joist and rafter centres in a loft, are the same job in different rooms. None of it is glamorous, but a bag without one means doing all of that with a tape measure and a pencil, which is slower and less repeatable from one visit to the next.

How I judge a chalk line reel

Line durability matters more than rewind speed. A basic braided nylon line frays and eventually snaps when it’s dragged repeatedly across old brick arrises, screwed studwork or gritty screed — exactly the surfaces most datum lines get snapped across on site. Kevlar-reinforced or heavier braided lines cost more but survive that abrasion for far longer.

Gear ratio, for the days with several lines to mark. A 3:1 crank needs roughly three turns of the handle for every metre rewound; a 6:1 planetary gear does it in about half that and jams less. On a survey with a single datum line it barely matters; setting out a full damp reading grid, the faster gear saves real minutes.

Case material. Aluminium and diecast metal bodies survive a drop down a loft hatch or a knock in a tool bag far better than ABS plastic, though the plastic-cased reels are lighter, cheaper, and adequate for occasional use.

Chalk capacity and how it’s filled. A reel that holds 100g or more and refills through a simple screw cap saves carrying a separate tub and topping up mid-survey; the smallest reels need refilling more often than is convenient.

Line lock and secondary uses. A lock that holds the line taut while it’s pinned against a surface gives a cleaner, straighter snap, and a few reels double as a rough plumb bob via a pointed metal claw on the end — a genuine bonus, but not a substitute for a dedicated plumb bob where the reading actually matters.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Kevlar and premium lines matter if it’s used weekly, not if it’s a rare backup. The abrasion resistance that justifies the OX or the Hultafors is only worth paying for if the reel’s actually getting dragged across brick arrises and screed regularly; for a chalk line that comes out once or twice a month, a basic braided line lasts long enough that the upgrade never earns its price back.

Chalk colour is a bigger deal than gear ratio on a period or listed property. Blue is the safe default and mostly weathers or brushes off; red and other bright colours show up better against pale masonry but stain more stubbornly, and on soft stone, lime render or unpainted plaster a mark that doesn’t fully disappear can be a genuine problem. Test an inconspicuous area first, or use a washable chalk, on anything historic.

A chalk line isn’t a substitute for a laser level or a plumb bob. It puts down a straight reference fast, but it doesn’t measure a slope or confirm vertical on its own — a chalked line across a floor still needs a level laid along it to read the fall, and a claw-end plumb function is a rough site check, not a replacement for a dedicated bob where the reading actually matters.

Fast rewind is a convenience, not an accuracy feature. A 3:1 crank and a 6:1 planetary gear put down an identical line — the only difference is how long it takes to wind back in afterwards. It’s worth paying for on a survey with several lines to mark; it’s not worth paying for on the strength of any accuracy claim, because there isn’t one to make.

Questions I get asked

What’s a chalk line actually used for on a building survey?

Mostly setting out reference points rather than the line itself — snapping a straight edge across a suspended timber floor before checking it for dip, marking a grid of positions up a damp wall so moisture meter readings come from fixed, repeatable points rather than wherever the probe happens to land, or marking joist and rafter centres in a loft before a closer look. The chalked line rarely appears in the report itself; it’s the tool that makes the measurement against it repeatable.

Will chalk mark a wall permanently?

Blue chalk is generally the safest choice and brushes or weathers off most painted and rendered surfaces without much trace. Red and other bright colours show up better in poor light or against pale masonry but are more likely to leave a mark that doesn’t fully disappear, particularly on soft or unpainted stone, lime render or bare plaster. On anything historic or delicate, testing an inconspicuous area first is worth the extra minute.

Is a Kevlar-reinforced line actually worth the extra cost?

If the reel gets dragged across old brickwork, screwed studwork or gritty screed regularly, yes — that abrasion is what actually kills a chalk line, not old age, and a Kevlar or reinforced line survives it for far longer than basic braided nylon. For a reel that mostly stays in the bag as an occasional backup, the extra cost is harder to justify.

Can a chalk line reel replace a laser level or plumb bob?

Not really — it’s complementary rather than a substitute. A chalk line puts down a fast, visible straight reference over a distance a laser or level might struggle to project clearly, but reading a fall or confirming true vertical still needs a level or bob laid against that line. The reels here that double as a plumb bob do it via a simple metal claw on the end, which is a rough site check rather than an accurate reading.

A chalk line earns its keep the same way a plumb bob does — not by measuring anything itself, but by giving every other measurement a fixed, repeatable reference to work from.
