The best
underfloor insulation
for a suspended timber floor

Lift a few boards in an older house and it's still common to find nothing at all under them — just joists, a ventilated void, and cold air moving straight past the floorboards. Underfloor insulation is one of the cheapest, least disruptive retrofit jobs there is, and one of the easiest to get slightly wrong: pack a void too tightly, block an airbrick while you're down there, or choose a material that can't cope with a damp sub-floor, and you've traded a cold floor for a decay problem that costs far more to put right. Here's how the materials differ, the ventilation rule that decides whether the job is safe, and six products worth buying.

A suspended timber ground floor is boards nailed to joists that span between honeycomb sleeper walls, with a ventilated void underneath — the airbricks you see low down on an older house's external walls exist to keep air moving through that void. It's a different animal from a solid concrete slab, and it's the majority of what I find under the boards in anything built before the 1960s. Left uninsulated, it's one of the biggest single sources of a cold floor and a draughty room, and Approved Document L sets a target U-value of 0.25 W/m²K for a floor once it's exposed for other work — a genuinely achievable number with the right thickness of the right material.

There are two ways in: from below, if there's crawl-space or cellar access, draping insulation across the underside of the boards and holding it up with support netting; or from above, lifting the boards and laying rigid insulation between or over the joists before relaying them. Which route makes sense depends entirely on what access you've actually got — a cellar with headroom makes the from-below job straightforward, while a shallow void with no hatch usually means taking the boards up is the only practical option.

The one thing every route has in common is that the ventilated void has to stay ventilated. Push insulation hard against an airbrick, or seal off the sub-floor cross-flow while you're in there, and you've swapped a cold floor for a moisture problem in the timber — the section on ventilation below is worth reading properly before buying anything, not skipping to the picks.

How I judge underfloor insulation

Lambda value against the joist depth you've actually got. A lower lambda (W/mK) hits a given U-value in less thickness — the figure that matters when a Victorian joist is only 100–125mm deep and there's a floorboard to relay on top of whatever goes in.

Whether it can cope with a damp sub-floor void. Mineral wool and PIR are inert and don't hold moisture; sheep's wool is hygroscopic and buffers humidity by design. Neither is automatically the right or wrong answer — it depends on how reliably ventilated the void is and stays.

Fire performance, if the void sits under a habitable room. Euroclass A1 (mineral wool) and sheep's wool's E rating aren't the same thing, and it's worth knowing which you're fitting rather than assuming insulation is insulation.

How it's actually held in place. Rigid board wedges or is fixed between joists; loose-fill or roll insulation needs support netting stapled to the joist sides, not just draped and hoped for. A job that skips this step sags, gaps form, and the insulation stops doing its job within a year or two.

Whether it's sold as a genuine floor product or a repurposed one. Some of what's on Amazon in this category — breather membrane especially — is roofing stock doing the same vapour-open job under a floor rather than something built and marketed for it. That's fine, but worth knowing rather than assuming from the name.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The ventilation rule is the whole job, not a footnote. SPAB's own guidance is explicit that care must be taken not to impede sub-floor ventilation when insulating, warning of timber decay if it is — and it sets a working figure of at least 150mm of clear void kept below the insulation in an externally-ventilated space. Government retrofit guidance, drawn from Approved Document C, gives the underlying airbrick requirement itself: ventilation openings of at least 1,500mm² per metre run of external wall, or 500mm² per m² of floor area, whichever is greater, with cross-ventilation from at least two opposing external walls. If the airbricks are painted shut, blocked by external paving that's crept up over the decades, or there simply aren't enough of them, that has to be fixed before or alongside insulating — not treated as a detail to sort out later.

This isn't a blanket "insulation causes rot" warning — it's a workmanship point. SPAB's own position is that insulation shouldn't be ruled out where adequate ventilation and detailing can genuinely be achieved. The real failure mode is a fitting error: insulation batts pushed hard against an airbrick from the inside, or a membrane draped in a way that blocks rather than guides airflow through the void. Get the detailing right and the ventilation figures above satisfied, and insulating is straightforward.

If you're being assessed under PAS 2035, ventilation gets checked twice. Current retrofit-assessment guidance requires existing sub-floor ventilation to be evaluated before an underfloor insulation measure is specified, and for the finished job to leave the dwelling no worse off against Approved Document C's cross-flow figures than it was beforehand — existing ventilation is treated as inadequate if there's evidence of mould, condensation, or no functioning ventilation route at all.

Thickness only helps if the joist depth allows it without choking the void. A thicker roll crammed into a shallow joist bay can end up pressed against the underside of the boards with no air movement left at all, which defeats a breathable material's whole purpose. Match the product's thickness to the actual joist depth, and don't assume more is automatically better.

Grant schemes are in flux — check what's live before promising a customer anything. The Great British Insulation Scheme closed for good on 31 March 2026. ECO4 has been extended to 31 December 2026, and Local Authority Flexible Eligibility and the Warm Homes: Local Grant remain live routes for floor insulation through a council. None of that is a substitute for getting the ventilation right first.

Questions I get asked

Do I need to keep the airbricks clear if I insulate the floor? Yes — insulating the floor doesn't remove the need for sub-floor ventilation, and blocking or reducing it while the boards are up is the single most common way this job goes wrong. Check the airbricks aren't painted shut, silted up, or buried by external ground levels that have risen over the years, and clear or replace them if they are.

Can I just use ordinary loft roll under the floor instead of a floor-specific product? Yes, in practice — Knauf and Isover's standard glass wool rolls are the same material whether it ends up in a loft or between joists, and Knauf's own literature confirms this product is made for use at standard joist centres. It just has a higher lambda value than stone wool or PIR, so budget more thickness to hit the same U-value.

Is sheep's wool worth paying roughly double for over mineral wool? It's the right call where the sub-floor void is genuinely damp or humidity-variable, since it buffers moisture rather than just sitting inert against it. Where the void is dry and well-ventilated, mineral wool does the thermal job for less money without giving anything up.

Do I need Building Control sign-off to insulate a suspended floor? It falls under the Building Regulations as a controlled thermal element once the floor is exposed for other work, so it's worth notifying Building Control or using a competent-persons scheme installer — the target U-value of 0.25 W/m²K comes from the same Approved Document L that sets it for a full floor replacement.
