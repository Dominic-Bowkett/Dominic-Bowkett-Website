A good many of the jobs that reach me start with the same observation: the floor slopes, the wall has dropped, the patio runs towards the house. Eyes and a spirit level will tell you something is wrong, but not by how much, and not across a whole room, a garden or a drainage run. An optical level, the dumpy level of old, does. You set it on a tripod, level it, and read a staff held at each point; every reading is a height relative to the same horizontal line.

It is an old-fashioned answer in a world of laser levels, and I will say plainly where the laser wins. For a room or a few metres of floor, a laser level is quicker and easier. An optical level earns its place over long distances and in bright daylight, where a laser line vanishes: across a garden, along a drainage run, down a terrace. It needs no batteries, and a staff reading is a number you can write down and check. The topographical survey cost guide shows what a levels survey costs if you would rather pay someone, and the drainage survey cost guide covers the falls side.

One caution first. A level gives you differences in height; it does not tell you why they exist. Settlement, a failed foundation, a sagging joist and a floor that was built out of true all give the same readings. Interpreting them is the survey.

How I judge an optical level

Automatic compensator. Modern levels are automatic: you bubble them roughly level and an internal compensator does the rest. That is what you want. Older manual tilting levels are slower and fussier, and there is no reason to buy one new.

Magnification and range. 20x to 24x is fine for a house and garden. 32x helps on long sights or in poor light. Beyond that you are paying for a precision the staff and the person holding it cannot match.

Stated accuracy. Quoted as millimetres over a distance. For floor levels, a couple of millimetres at 30m is far tighter than the floor itself. For drainage falls it matters more: a 1 in 80 fall over 10m is only 125mm, so a few millimetres of error is small but not nothing.

The whole kit. A level without a tripod and staff is not usable. Compare the price of a complete set, and look at the staff: a 5m aluminium telescopic staff with a vial is the sensible spec.

Weather sealing. IP54 shrugs off rain and dust; IP57 survives a drenching. A level that lives in a van and gets set up on wet ground should have one or the other.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Technique beats the instrument. Most bad readings come from a staff that is not vertical, a tripod that is not firm, or a level that has been knocked between sights. Close the run by taking a reading back on your starting point and check you get the same number. That one habit matters more than a step up in magnification.

Sets beat parts. Buying a level, tripod and staff separately is rarely cheaper and invites mismatches. The 5/8 inch tripod thread is standard, but the sensible route is a set from one seller.

Skip the digital extras. Digital levels that read a barcoded staff cost several times more and are aimed at full-time surveyors. A conventional automatic level with a decent staff covers what a building surveyor needs.

When a level is the wrong answer. If you only want to know whether a floor is flat, a long spirit level or a laser level is quicker. For distances, a measuring wheel does the job. And a level only gives readings; deciding what they mean is the survey.

Questions I get asked

What is the difference between a dumpy level and an optical level? Nothing useful. Dumpy level is the traditional name, from the short, squat telescope of the older instruments. Today it usually means an automatic optical level, set on a tripod and read against a staff.

Is an optical level better than a laser level? They suit different jobs. A laser is faster indoors and over short distances; an optical level works over greater distances and in sunshine, and needs no batteries. Many people end up owning one of each.

How accurate does it need to be? For floor and garden levels, a stated accuracy of a couple of millimetres at 30m is more than enough. The error in how a staff is held is usually larger than the error in the instrument.

Can I check drainage falls with one? Yes, by reading levels at the manholes and working out the fall between them, but you need to take the readings carefully and know what falls are acceptable, so an inexperienced user should ask a drainage specialist.
