The best
telescopic inspection
camera poles

Half the things worth a second look on a roof aren’t worth a ladder for — a hairline crack in a parapet coping, a gutter joint that might be the source of a stain two floors down, a flat roof upstand that looks fine from the pavement but not from directly underneath. A phone on the end of a pole answers that question in thirty seconds without anyone leaving the ground. This isn’t really one product category so much as two: a good heavy-duty pole and a cheap clamp, or a proper wireless camera kit if the phone-on-a-stick approach earns its keep often enough. Here’s what’s actually worth buying either way.

A drone is the right tool for a systematic look at a whole roof, and binoculars are the right tool for reading detail from a distance — but neither is the right tool for “I need to see directly into that valley gutter, right now, without setting up a flight or squinting through an eyepiece.” That gap is what a pole fills: get a phone (or a small camera) up to eaves height, over a parapet, or across a flat roof upstand, and look at the screen from the safety of the ground.

Worth saying plainly before the picks: amazon.co.uk doesn’t sell a mature, purpose-built “inspection camera pole” category the way it does ladders or laser measures. What’s actually there is a handful of genuine wireless camera-and-monitor kits at a serious price, sitting alongside generic heavy-duty telescopic poles built for painters and window cleaners that happen to take a phone clamp on the same universal thread. Both routes work. Which one’s worth the money depends on how often the job comes up.

None of what follows has been used on a live survey under my own supervision — picks are drawn from manufacturers’ and sellers’ own listed specifications, cross-checked against live stock and pricing, filtered through what actually matters for getting a usable photo from three metres up.

How I judge an inspection pole

Reach against control. A longer pole reaches further, but every extra section adds flex and weight held one-handed above head height — past six or seven metres, a light pole starts to sway and the picture blurs before the shutter fires. Buy for the tallest thing actually inspected, not the longest pole on the page.

A genuine 1/4" UNC thread, not a proprietary clip. The standard camera-tripod thread means a phone clamp, a Bluetooth remote and, eventually, a small action camera all fit the same pole. A pole sold with its own bespoke mount locks the buyer into whatever that one mount can do.

A locking clamp, not a squeeze-fit. The phone is the expensive part of this setup. A clamp that tightens with a screw thread and stays put is worth more than a marginally cheaper spring-loaded one that can work loose over a pole’s length of vibration.

A way to fire the shutter without touching the phone. Reaching up to tap a screen three metres overhead defeats the point. A Bluetooth remote or a phone’s own voice/volume-button shutter is the difference between a genuinely one-person job and a two-person one.

Live view against guess-and-check. A phone screen is readable from the ground with the pole held at a sensible angle; it’s a real limitation once the pole’s reaching directly overhead rather than out and up. A wireless monitor solves this properly, at a real cost.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A locking clamp and a remote shutter matter more than the pole’s brand name. Every pole in this guide is, mechanically, an aluminium or carbon tube with sections that lock — the meaningful differences are all in whether the phone stays put and whether the shutter can be fired without reaching up to it.

“Up to Xm” range claims on wireless kits are marketing, not a spec. SkyVac’s own listing asterisks its 50m figure with no visible footnote; treat any wireless range claim in this category as a best-case number, and test at the actual distance a job needs before trusting it for a report.

Carbon fibre is a nice-to-have for weight, not a must. It matters most on a longer pole held one-handed for any length of time; an aluminium pole at 7m is heavier but perfectly usable for the seconds a photo takes.

The phone itself isn’t weatherproof just because the pole is. None of these clamps or poles make a normal phone rated for rain or a knock against masonry — a dropped phone from three metres onto a hard roof or into a gutter is a real risk, not a hypothetical one, and it’s worth using an older or cased phone rather than a daily handset.

What doesn’t matter: a pole’s maximum stated length beyond what the job actually needs. Past six or seven metres, a light pole starts to sway and the picture blurs before the shutter fires — the tallest gutter on the round decides the right length, not the biggest number on the listing.

Questions I get asked

Is a camera pole a substitute for a drone roof survey?

No. A pole answers “what does that one specific thing look like” — a suspect gutter joint, a cracked coping, a flat roof upstand — in the time it takes to extend it and take a photo. A drone survey covers a whole roof systematically, from angles a pole can’t reach, and is the right tool when the brief is the roof as a whole rather than one reachable detail.

Do I need the wireless camera kit, or is a phone and a clamp enough?

For occasional use, a phone and a locking clamp cover the job perfectly well. The wireless kits earn their price where the pole’s used often enough that a proper hands-free monitor genuinely saves time over squinting at a phone screen overhead, or where the work needs an actual saved video file for a report rather than a still photo.

How high up can these realistically be used?

The practical limit is the pole’s own rigidity and how steady it can be held, not the number printed on the listing. Past about six or seven metres, even a light carbon pole starts to sway enough to blur the shot. Treat these as tools for parapets, gutters and flat roofs up to two or three storeys — not a substitute for scaffolding, a cherry picker or a drone on anything taller.

Will a generic pole damage my phone?

Not from the pole itself, provided the clamp is a proper locking type and tightened before extending. The real risk is a drop — from height, onto a hard roof surface or into a gutter — which normal phone insurance doesn’t always cover explicitly. Using an older handset, or one already in a case, is the sensible precaution rather than the newest phone in the house.

A pole answers the question of what’s actually happening on a roof that can’t be reached safely — the same question a full building survey answers properly, with access equipment and time budgeted for it, or a drone roof survey answers from above.
