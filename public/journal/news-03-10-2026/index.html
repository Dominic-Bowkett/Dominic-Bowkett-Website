Surveyor’s notes
3 October 2026:
market cools

House price growth has halved, a survey says landlords are leaving, Right to Rent checks changed on 1 October and a bank has asked landlords about EPCs. Here is what it changes on the ground in the South East.

Published 3 October 2026 Reading time · 6 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A cooling market, a nervous rental sector and one rule change that landlords can be fined for getting wrong. Two of these stories are about people deciding whether to buy, sell or keep a rental, and a third is a reminder that the energy rules are still coming whatever the market does.

House price growth has halved, and buyers will be asking harder questions

Nationwide’s house price index for September shows annual growth of 0.8%, down from 1.6% in August. The release also puts Northern Ireland ahead at 5.9% year on year for the third quarter and East Anglia the weakest at a 0.7% annual fall. According to Valuq’s write-up, the average price slipped to £274,251, a 0.2% monthly fall and the fourth drop in five months. Jeremy Leaf is quoted there calling it “a cooling, not a collapse”.

I would agree with Leaf, with one caveat. A national index averages very different local markets, and I would not read a South East number into a figure I have not seen. What I will say from the survey desk is that a market where homes outnumber serious buyers is one where buyers ask harder questions. They want to know about the roof, the damp and what the EPC says before they offer.

If you are selling an older house in East Sussex or Kent, expect the survey to be used as a negotiating tool more often than it was two years ago. Fix or cost the obvious defects before you list, and have the EPC ready. If you are buying, a cooling market is a reason to commission a proper survey, not to skip one.

Landlords are heading for the exit, and the stock they sell will need work

Landlord Today reports, and Property118 covers, new SpareRoom research on landlord sentiment since the Renters’ Rights Act. The headline figures are that 78% of landlords have no confidence in the lettings market, 36% are reducing their portfolios and 27% are leaving altogether, rising to 44% among single-property landlords. Fewer than 4% are expanding. Neither article gives the sample size or links the underlying research, so treat the percentages as indicative. SpareRoom is also a lettings platform with a view on the outcome.

Even allowing for that, the direction is plausible. The practical consequence for a surveyor is that if landlords do sell, a lot of rented houses will change hands. Ex-rental stock is often tired: dated heating, an old EPC at E, patchy ventilation and damp that was managed rather than fixed. A buyer who treats it as a bargain can inherit all of that. That is my bet, not something the survey measured.

If you are a landlord thinking of selling, a dated condition report and a current EPC protect your price. If you are thinking of staying, the same documents protect you when a tenant complains. If you are buying ex-rental, a building survey is where the real cost shows up.

Right to Rent now has a registered-provider route, and the penalty is not small

The Home Office’s landlord and agent codes of practice carry a note that updated versions come into force on 1 October 2026. Letting Agent Today reports that digital checks must now go through a registered Digital Verification Service provider, and that non-registered providers no longer give a statutory defence. It cites fines of up to £20,000 per occupier. I could not confirm the penalty figure or the provider detail from the Home Office page itself, so check the code before relying on either.

This is not my specialism, and I would not pretend otherwise. But it lands on the same people who read these notes. A self-managing landlord who has been using a cheap identity app needs to check that it is still on the approved list. A manual document check is a different route, but it takes more care to get right.

Barclays asked landlords about EPCs, and the answer is “about £9,000”

As Landlord Today reports, a Barclays survey found 51% of the customers it asked, mostly landlords, had made improvements in the past three years to lift an EPC rating. 38% expect difficulty meeting proposed minimum energy efficiency changes, and the estimated cost is about £9,000 a property. The article does not link the Barclays report, so I would call this a bank’s survey of its own customers, not a market measure.

The £9,000 figure is the interesting one. It is a landlord’s estimate, not a quote, and what it costs in practice depends on what is wrong. A 1960s semi that needs loft top-up and controls is a few hundred pounds. A solid-walled Victorian cottage with an oil boiler is a different order of problem. The average hides that spread.

The other thing the survey suggests is that a lot of landlords have already done something. If you are one of them, find out whether the work actually moved the rating. An EPC that has not been refreshed since the work is just an old number.

What I’d actually do

  1. Sellers of older houses: fix or cost the visible defects before listing, and have a current EPC to hand. A cooling market rewards the seller with no surprises.
  2. Buyers: do not skip the survey because prices are soft. Soft prices are exactly when ex-rental stock with hidden damp turns up.
  3. Landlords thinking of leaving: commission a condition report and refresh the EPC before you market the property.
  4. Landlords staying: read the updated Right to Rent code and confirm that any digital provider you use is registered.
  5. Anyone who has done EPC work: get a new EPC to confirm the improvement registered.
