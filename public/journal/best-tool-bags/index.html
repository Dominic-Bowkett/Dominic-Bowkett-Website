Write out the kit list for an ordinary survey day and it stops looking like a tool kit at all: damp meter, laser measure, borescope, head torch, camera, spare batteries, tablet for the RdSAP software, dust masks, gloves, knee pads, a torch for the torch. Two thousand pounds of instruments and not a single hammer. Yet almost every bag on the market is designed for hammers — one cavernous compartment, a forest of identical pockets, and a base that wicks up whatever the last cellar floor had to offer.

That mismatch is the survey bag problem. Instruments have screens and lenses; they get crushed, scratched and rattled. Small kit — the borescope, the voltage stick, the spare SD cards — migrates to the bottom of a big compartment and has to be excavated by feel, usually at the top of a loft ladder with a client holding the hatch. And the whole lot gets put down on wet grass, damp concrete and worse, several times a day.

So the lens for this guide is simple: a place for everything, so a specific item can be found one-handed in a dark loft; enough padding and structure that nothing gets crushed; a base you can stand in a puddle; and a format — backpack, tote or wheels — that matches how you actually move through a job. On that last point I’ll declare a bias up front: stairs, ladders and loft hatches favour backpacks, because they leave both hands free, and most survey days are made of stairs, ladders and loft hatches.

How I judge a tool bag

Format follows the job. A backpack keeps both hands free for ladders, loft hatches and three flights of a Victorian terrace — which is why it’s the default for survey work. An open tote is faster for van-to-kitchen-table jobs where you’re grabbing and returning tools constantly. Wheels earn their keep on long corridors — blocks of flats, commercial jobs, anywhere with a lift — and are a liability on gravel drives and staircases. Most people eventually run one of each; the question is which one you buy properly.

A slot per instrument. The single biggest difference between a bag that works and a bag that doesn’t is whether every item has one home. Vertical pockets that hold tools upright — the layout Veto built its reputation on — mean you can see every handle at a glance and pull one out without unpacking three others. If the damp meter lives in the same pocket every day, your hand goes to it in the dark. That’s not a luxury; on a winter afternoon in an unlit roof void it is the difference between a survey that flows and one that doesn’t.

Padding where the fragile kit lives. Meters, borescopes and cameras want a padded, structured compartment that holds its shape when the bag is dropped — or at least dedicated pockets that stop them grinding against the SDS bits. Bags with a rigid or semi-rigid shell protect instruments even when a ladder falls on them in the van. Soft sacks protect nothing.

A base for British ground. The bag gets put down on wet grass at eight in the morning and a damp cellar floor by ten. A moulded plastic or hard rubberised base keeps the contents dry and stands the bag upright so it doesn’t slump and spill. Fabric bottoms wick water; it’s that simple.

Zips, stitching, warranty. A loaded tool backpack carries 10–15kg every working day. That kills cheap zips within a year — the failure point owner reviews mention more than any other. Chunky zips, bar-tacked stitching and an honest warranty (Veto’s is five years) are what you’re actually paying for at the top end. The counterweight is the bag’s own empty mass: heavy-duty construction can add 3–4kg before a single tool goes in.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The base and the zips are the bag. Fabric, straps and pockets rarely fail; bottoms and zips do. A moulded or rigid water-resistant base decides whether the bag survives British ground, and zip quality decides whether it survives year two. If you handle a bag in a shop, run the main zip ten times and press on the base — that two-minute test tells you more than the spec sheet.

Discipline beats capacity. The bags that work are the ones that force a place for everything; the bags that fail are the 50-litre voids that let everything fall to the bottom. Counter-intuitively, a slightly smaller bag with more structure carries better and finds faster. If a bag can swallow your whole kit in a heap, one day it will, and that day you’ll be at the top of a ladder wondering where the head torch went.

Weigh the empty bag. Structured protection costs kilograms. A Veto or a rigid Milwaukee starts around 4kg before the first tool; a soft CLC around half that. If your kit is light instruments rather than steel, a lighter bag with good pockets may serve your spine better than armour you don’t need.

What the marketing pushes that you can skip. Pocket-count arms races (a 75-pocket bag where you use twelve), built-in USB charging ports (a battery bank in a pocket does the same job and doesn’t break), laptop sleeves pitched as “tech compartments”, and tactical styling. None of it moves the job on. Spend the money on the base, the zips and the layout — the three things you can’t retrofit.

Questions I get asked

Tool backpack or tool bag — which is better? For anyone whose work involves stairs, ladders or lofts: the backpack, without much argument. Both hands free is a safety point, not a comfort one — the Health and Safety Executive’s guidance on ladder work expects three points of contact, which is impossible with a tote in one fist. Handled bags win where the carry is short and the access is constant: van to kitchen, grab, return, repeat. Most working kits end up with one of each, and the split that works is instruments and valuables in the backpack, heavy hand tools and consumables in the tote.

Are tool bags with wheels worth it? On the right jobs, entirely. Purpose-built flats, commercial buildings, hospitals, anywhere with lifts and long corridors — wheels save your shoulders kilometre after kilometre. On gravel, mud, and staircases they’re dead weight, which is why the convertible format (the DeWalt above) is the one I’d actually shortlist: wheels for the smooth stretches, straps for everything else. If your patch is rural period housing, skip the wheels and spend the difference on a better backpack.

How do I stop instruments getting damaged in a tool bag? Three layers of defence. First, a bag with structure — a rigid or semi-rigid shell stops the crush loads that kill screens. Second, a dedicated slot per instrument so nothing grinds against anything else; padded sleeves or small zip cases inside a general bag achieve the same for a few pounds. Third, habit: meters live in the same pocket every day, batteries out of anything stored over winter, and the bag never travels loose in the van where a ladder can land on it. The most expensive option is a hard case per instrument — justified for a thermal camera, overkill for a voltage stick.

What size tool bag do I need? Smaller than you think. The instinct is to buy the biggest, then fill it, then carry the lot up every staircase in the county. A better method: lay out what one typical job actually uses — for me that’s a dozen instruments and the PPE — and buy the smallest bag that holds that with a slot each. Bulk and rarely-used kit stays in the van in cheap holdalls or crates. As a rule of thumb, if a loaded bag passes 12–15kg it has stopped being a bag and should be two.
