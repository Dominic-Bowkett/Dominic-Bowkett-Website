A plumb bob is the oldest tool in a surveyor’s bag by several thousand years, and it has survived onto site alongside laser levels and digital angle finders for one reason: nothing else settles an argument about vertical as cheaply, or as hard to argue with. A wall that looks like it’s leaning under artificial light, in a dim stairwell, or against a sloping ceiling can be genuinely difficult to read by eye. Hang a plumb bob against it and the answer is a straight line and a number in millimetres, not an impression.

Most calls for one come down to the same job: is that wall actually out of plumb, or does it just look it. A bowed cavity wall over a bay window, a chimney breast leaning into a loft, a retaining wall in a garden, a door reveal that’s been packed out and repainted — a plumb bob run from a fixed point at the top down to a tape at the bottom converts “looks a bit off” into an offset that goes in the report, and that offset is what decides whether it’s old movement worth a note or active movement worth flagging for further investigation.

It’s also the fallback when a laser level’s battery is flat, the site’s too bright for the dot to read, or the wall’s too far from anywhere sensible to justify carrying a laser up a ladder. None of the plumb bobs below have been carried on an actual survey — the notes are built from manufacturers’ specifications and recurring owner feedback, filtered through what a two-minute wall check on site actually needs. How I judge a plumb bob

Bob weight, and how fast it stops swinging. A heavier bob — 300g and up — settles into true vertical faster and resists a breeze on an external check far better than the light 100g bobs sold mainly for wallpaper hanging. On an exposed elevation or up a ladder, a bob that’s still swinging after thirty seconds is a bob that gets the job abandoned rather than finished properly.

How it mounts. A plain hook needs something to hook onto — a nail, a bradawl hole, a helpful gap in brickwork — which isn’t always available on a finished wall a vendor doesn’t want marked. A magnetic base solves it instantly on steel lintels, RSJs and stud partitions with screws in; a retractable pin works on timber and plaster where a magnet won’t grip. The auto-recoil cased tools that offer both are more expensive but genuinely faster to deploy solo.

Line length and material. Most wall and reveal checks need less than 3m, but a floor-to-ceiling or external elevation check can want 5–7m, and a line that’s too short is a wasted trip back to the van. Braided nylon holds a straighter line than twisted string and doesn’t stretch under the bob’s own weight over time.

Point accuracy. A machined, hardened tip gives a genuinely repeatable reference against a tape or a straight edge; a soft cast or moulded tip wears and rounds off, which matters less for a one-off DIY job than for a tool meant to give a consistent reading survey after survey.

Corrosion resistance. A tool that lives in a damp van door pocket for years needs brass, chromed steel or a plated finish — raw mild steel pits and the plating on the cheapest bobs flakes within a season.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Weight matters more than the case it comes in. A 400g bob settles in seconds; a 100g bob can take half a minute and never fully stop in a breeze. An expensive auto-recoil case is a genuine convenience, but it isn’t what makes the reading accurate — the mass hanging on the end of the line is.

A plumb bob tells you the wall is out of vertical, not why. A bowed cavity wall could be decades-old settlement that’s long since stopped moving, or an active wall tie failure that needs an engineer’s opinion. The offset in millimetres is the data point that goes in the report; what it means comes from the rest of the survey — cracking, tie condition, age of the property — not from the plumb bob itself.

A laser level hasn’t made the mechanical bob obsolete. A laser is faster over distance and easier to read in the right light, but it needs a battery, periodic calibration and a dark enough or shaded enough surface to see the dot on. A plumb bob needs none of that, which is exactly why it’s worth keeping in the bag as a backup rather than relying on a laser alone.

Brass over steel for anything that lives in the van long-term. The price difference between a brass and a steel bob of similar weight is a few pounds; the difference in how long each survives being carried around in a damp tool bag is measured in years, not weeks.

Questions I get asked

How do I tell if a wall is actually out of plumb or just looks it?

Hang a plumb bob from a fixed point at the top of the wall — a bradawl hole, a magnet on a lintel, a retractable pin — and let it settle, then measure the gap between the line and the wall at the bottom against the gap at the top. A genuine offset gives a number in millimetres; if the line sits evenly against the wall top to bottom, what looked like a lean was probably the light or the angle it was viewed from.

Is a laser level or laser plumb better than a mechanical plumb bob for checking a wall?

A laser is quicker for repeated checks and easier to read over a longer distance in the right conditions, but it needs a charged battery, occasional calibration and a surface the dot is visible against. A mechanical plumb bob needs none of that — it’s the tool that still works when the laser’s flat, and most surveyors who carry a laser keep a plumb bob as the backup rather than the other way round.

How much lean or bow in a wall is actually a problem?

There’s no single figure that applies everywhere — it depends on the wall’s age, construction and what else is happening around it. A consistent, long-standing lean with no active cracking is usually old movement that’s settled; a bow that’s increasing, or paired with fresh cracking, is the combination that warrants a closer look or a structural engineer’s opinion rather than a number on its own.

Can a plumb bob be used accurately outdoors in wind?

A heavier bob — 300g and up — holds far steadier in a breeze than a light 100g one, though any plumb bob will swing in a genuine gust. On an exposed elevation, waiting for a lull, sheltering the line, or simply using the heaviest bob available all help more than trying to read a reference that’s still moving.

A plumb bob earns its place in the bag for one reason: it settles an argument about vertical that the eye alone can’t always win.
