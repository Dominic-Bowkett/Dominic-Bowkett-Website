A good chunk of my working week happens in the dark. Lofts with no lighting circuit, cellars where the bulb went sometime around decimalisation, sub-floor voids, garages stacked to the ceiling. A head torch goes on before the loft ladder every time — I’ve written about the ones worth buying — but a head torch has a built-in limitation: it lights where you look. The insulation gap, the slipped sarking felt, the historic leak staining round the chimney — the things that matter are usually in the corner you aren’t looking at, and a moving cone of light is a genuinely bad way to search a space methodically.

The other problem is photographs. An RdSAP assessment wants loft insulation evidenced; a damp investigation wants the staining on the cellar wall recorded honestly. Phone cameras have got remarkably good in the dark, but “good” means aggressive processing — noise, smearing, colours that have been guessed. Put a decent work light in the space and the same phone produces evidence, usable in a report. That alone justifies the second light in the bag.

The work light market runs from £10 Amazon specials to £300 site floodlights, and the spec sheets are among the least honest in the tool world. What follows is how I’d cut through it — and seven lights, from a two-for-£12 glovebox pair to proper trade kit, that earn a place.

How I judge a work light

Honest lumens, at a setting you can actually run. For a loft, a cellar or a single room, 500–1500 genuine lumens of diffused light is the useful band — enough to see and photograph the whole space without glare. The catch is that headline figures are usually a boost mode held for minutes before the light dims to manage heat, and the cheap end of Amazon quotes numbers that are simply fiction. A real 1000 lumens for an hour is worth more than a claimed 10,000 for ninety seconds.

Colour temperature and CRI — because the photos are the point. Mould, damp staining, salt bands and timber discolouration photograph most honestly under neutral light, around 4000–5000K, from an LED with decent colour rendering (CRI in the 80s or better; 95+ is detailing-lamp territory). Very cold 6500K light makes everything look slightly blue and clinical; very warm light can make a tide mark quietly disappear. Most work lights sit at the cold end, which is liveable — but if your photographs end up in reports or insurance claims, colour quality is a real specification, not audiophile fuss.

It has to hold itself up. Both my hands are usually occupied — probe in one, phone in the other. A work light earns its keep with a magnetic base that actually grips (steel beams, tanks, van doors), a hook for hanging off a rafter or a nail, and a stand or folding body that aims the light where you want it on a floor that is never flat. This is where the format beats a torch lying on its side pointing at nothing.

Runtime at useful output. Manufacturers quote runtime at the dimmest setting and brightness at the highest, and hope you don’t notice they never coincide. What matters is the middle: a light that can do two or three hours at a genuinely useful output covers a day of inspections between charges. Check the battery capacity (a real 5000mAh cell is a good sign) and read owner feedback on what the runtime actually is.

USB-C, and ideally power both ways. USB-C charging means one cable for the light, the phone and everything else in the van; micro-USB on a new light in 2026 is a small tax on your patience. Some lights add a power-bank function — the light charges the phone that’s taking the photographs, which on a long day is more useful than it sounds. Battery-platform lights (Milwaukee, Makita) sidestep the question entirely: the drill batteries run the light.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Ignore the lumen arms race. Amazon is full of “12,000 lumen” work lights at £25. LEDs and batteries have not repealed physics: that output would flatten the claimed cell in minutes and cook the housing. Treat any figure over about 2000 lumens on a compact rechargeable light as marketing, and read owner feedback for words like “dims after a few minutes” — that’s thermal throttling, the honest spec leaking out.

Diffusion beats brightness in a confined space. A bare hot LED in a small dark loft is glare: your pupils clamp down, and everything outside the beam goes blacker. A broad, even panel bounced off the rafters, or a lantern hung above head height, lights the space rather than a patch of it. This is why the M12 AL’s diffused design and the DML807’s hanging 360° format work so well — and why 600 well-placed lumens beat a 2000-lumen spot at arm’s length.

The gimmicks you can skip. Bluetooth speakers built into work lights exist (Ledlenser’s otherwise respectable iF-series has a “Music” variant); colour-cycling party modes, disco strobes, “emergency beacons” on a light that will live in a loft — none of it earns weight in the bag. Red modes have a genuine roadside use; the rest is shelf appeal. Spend the money on the LED quality, the battery and the sealing.

Think about the battery question once, properly. If your van already runs Milwaukee or Makita batteries, the platform lights are the obvious buy — runtime becomes however many packs you carry. If not, a self-contained USB-C light is simpler and lighter. And a note for anyone heading onto managed construction sites: task-lighting rules there are their own subject, and what’s in this guide is inspection kit, not a substitute for proper site lighting on a distribution board.

Questions I get asked

How many lumens do I need to light a loft? Less than the adverts suggest. A typical domestic loft is lit perfectly adequately by 500–1000 genuine lumens if the light is diffused and well placed — hung centrally or bounced off the underside of the roof. A big loft, or one you’re photographing end to end, benefits from 1500 or so. What you don’t need is a 5000-lumen floodlight at arm’s length: in a confined space that’s glare, and your photos will be a white blaze surrounded by black.

What colour temperature is best for inspection photographs? Neutral white, around 4000–5000K, with the highest CRI you can get. That range is close to daylight without the blue cast of 6500K “daylight” LEDs, so mould reads as the greens and blacks it actually is, and damp staining keeps its tell-tale brown edge instead of washing out. Phone cameras auto-white-balance, but low-CRI light discards colour information that no amount of processing brings back. It’s the reason a colour-match light like the Sunmatch earns a place in a survey bag.

Are the cheap Amazon work lights any good? For occasional domestic use, genuinely yes — with expectations set correctly. The £10–£15 folding COB lights are dimmer than claimed, cold-toned and slow to charge, but the format (magnet, hook, swivel) is right and owner feedback is broadly happy. The reported failure points are the switches and the micro-USB sockets, which at this price you accept. Where I’d draw the line is relying on one professionally: no consistent output, no spares, and the exact product under a given name changes without notice.

Should I buy the work light on my drill-battery platform? If you own the batteries, usually yes — it’s the cheapest runtime you’ll ever buy, and a spare pack means the light never dies mid-job. Two caveats: bare-tool prices flatter the true cost if you don’t own the platform (a battery and charger can double it), and platform lights are bulkier than the folding inspection format, so they tend to live in the van rather than the survey bag. My honest answer is both: a small self-contained light that’s always with you, and the platform light for when the whole space needs lighting for an hour.
