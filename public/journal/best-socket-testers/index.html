The best
socket testers
for survey work

A socket or two on most surveys gets a quick check — reversed polarity, no earth, an RCD that does not trip when it should — and that finding either goes in the report as routine or gets flagged for an electrician to look at properly. Here is what is worth carrying for that, from a fifteen-pound check plug to a proper loop-and-RCD tester, and what each one actually tells you.

Sockets rarely get a survey’s full attention — the roof, the damp readings and the movement cracks take priority — but an accessible socket or two, particularly in an older property or a rental due an EICR of its own, is worth the thirty seconds a plug-in tester takes. It is not an electrical inspection. It is a quick, defensible way to notice the things a competent electrician would want to know about: no earth connection, live and neutral reversed, or an RCD that sits there doing nothing when the test button is pressed. Any of those is a one-line note in a report and a recommendation to get it looked at properly, not something to diagnose further on site.

The category splits on what the tester actually checks. A basic check plug — three or four LEDs, a fold-out or printed fault chart — confirms the socket is wired correctly and nothing more. Step up in price and the same plug-in format starts adding an earth loop impedance test, which gives a rough read on how good the earth path actually is, and an RCD trip-test button, which proves the protective device works rather than merely being present. That second tier is doing a genuinely different job: a socket that lights up “correct” on a basic checker can still sit on a circuit whose RCD does not trip, and only the fuller testers will tell you that.

None of this replaces a proper Electrical Installation Condition Report, done by a qualified electrician with calibrated test equipment and a certificate at the end of it. What a plug-in tester gives you is the evidence to know when that recommendation is worth making, rather than guessing. First, the one I’d buy.

How I judge a socket tester

What it actually tests, not how many lights it has. A basic three- or six-light checker confirms the socket is wired correctly and nothing more. The genuine step up is an RCD trip-test button and an earth loop impedance reading — between them those tell you whether the protective device is doing its job, not just whether the cable behind the faceplate is connected the right way round.

An RCD button that actually trips something. Pressing it should cause a working RCD to trip within the regulation time. If nothing happens, that is the finding worth writing up — not a fault with the tester, and not something to explain away.

Indicators that do not need decoding. A fault legend printed on the case itself beats a fold-out chart that lives at the bottom of the tool bag and gets left in the van on the day it is needed.

A CAT rating appropriate for what is actually live. CAT II 230–300V is the baseline for anything plugged into a domestic socket outlet. It should be stated on the casing, not implied by the price.

Small, robust, and priced for what it does. Most of what separates a £15 tester from a £55 one is capability, not build quality. Buy the capability the job in front of you actually needs, not the most expensive unit on the shelf.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The real upgrade is RCD and loop, not more LEDs. A basic three-light checker and a fancier six-light checker tell you almost the same thing — the wiring is or is not connected correctly. The tester that earns the extra spend is the one with a genuine RCD trip button and a loop-impedance reading, because those are the checks a basic checker cannot do at any price.

A socket tester is not an EICR. It flags things worth writing up — no earth, reversed polarity, an RCD that does not trip — but it does not replace a qualified electrician’s proper testing with calibrated instruments and a certificate at the end of it. Treat a bad reading as the reason for a recommendation, not as the finished diagnosis.

A digital readout is a bonus, not a reason to pay double. An LCD voltage figure is a nice-to-have. A unit with LEDs only that has an RCD button and a loop test tells you more than a unit with a screen that has neither.

Loop-test bands are a guide, not a certified figure. The traffic-light “good/check/urgent” system on a plug-in tester is indicative. If it says urgent, that is the referral to make, not a number to write into a report as if it came from a calibrated loop tester.

Things worth ignoring on a listing: “professional grade” with no stated CAT rating anywhere on the page, and “multi-function” that turns out to mean it also comes with a carry pouch rather than any extra electrical test.

Questions I get asked

Can a socket tester replace a proper electrical inspection?

No. A plug-in socket tester is a spot check on one outlet at a time — it can tell you a socket is wired correctly, that the earth loop reads within a rough band, or that an RCD trips when tested, but it is not a substitute for an Electrical Installation Condition Report carried out by a qualified electrician with calibrated equipment. Its value on a survey is knowing when that recommendation is worth making, not replacing the person who would actually make it.

What does it mean if a tester shows “no earth”?

It means the tester has not detected a continuous earth connection at that socket, which on an older installation can be a genuine safety issue rather than a false reading. It is worth trying a second nearby socket to rule out a one-off connection problem at that outlet specifically, but a repeated “no earth” result is exactly the kind of finding that belongs in a report with a recommendation for a qualified electrician to investigate.

Do I need the RCD test button, or is a basic checker enough?

For a fast sanity check on wiring — is this socket obviously wrong — a basic three-light checker like the Martindale or Draper picks does the job at a low price. Where the extra spend earns its keep is proving that the RCD protecting a circuit actually trips rather than merely being fitted, which a basic checker cannot tell you at any price. On rented property, older installations or anywhere RCD protection is doing real work, that distinction is worth the extra cost.

What is the difference between a socket tester and a voltage tester?

A socket tester is a fixed-format plug-in device that checks one 13A outlet’s wiring, and on the fuller models its earth loop and RCD, in one go. A voltage tester is a handheld probe used to prove a circuit is dead or live before work starts, and covers far more than sockets — junction boxes, light fittings, isolators. The two do different jobs and most electrical work ends up needing both rather than one instead of the other.

A socket tester earns its place for the same reason as most of the kit in this series — it turns a hunch into something worth writing down. On a building survey or a retrofit assessment, that is often the difference between a vague note about “dated wiring” and a specific recommendation an electrician can act on.
