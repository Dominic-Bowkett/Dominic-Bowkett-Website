A knife is the cheapest tool in the van and one of the easiest to get hurt with. A surveyor does not cut much, but the cutting that does come up is awkward: slitting a roll of loft insulation to check its depth, opening a vapour barrier to see what is behind it, trimming a draught strip, cutting a sample of membrane, opening a box of kit on a landing with no flat surface. Most of it happens kneeling, one-handed, in poor light.

The market splits into four designs. A retractable knife holds a trapezoid blade that slides in and out of the handle. A folding knife closes like a penknife. A snap-off knife has a long segmented blade that you break to a fresh edge. A safety knife, usually auto-retract, pulls the blade back as soon as you let go. The right one depends on what you cut, how often, and how much a cut hand would cost you that week.

Keep the legal side in proportion. Under the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 an employer has to assess the risk from work equipment, and the HSE’s guidance on cutting tools favours blade guards and auto-retract designs where they are practical. If you are a sole trader the same sense applies to you. A decent knife, a fresh blade and a pair of cut-resistant work gloves deal with nearly all of it.

How I judge a utility knife

Blade control. The blade should lock where you put it and not creep in or out under pressure. A knife that closes on your fingers mid-cut is worse than none. Think about whether you will be cutting towards your own hand in a cramped void, because that decides whether auto-retract is worth having.

Blade availability. Standard trapezoid blades are sold by every builders’ merchant, so you will not be stranded on a Saturday. Proprietary blades are fine if the maker has a good supply, but check before you commit.

Blade change. A blunt blade makes you push harder, and pushing harder is how people slip. A tool-free blade change gets blades swapped. A screw-fastened body gets blades kept in use for too long.

Where it lives. A knife that sits in a trouser pocket needs to close or retract. A knife that stays in a tool bag can be bulkier. An exposed fixed blade belongs in a sheath or not at all.

Grip. Dust, damp and cold fingers in a loft all reduce grip. A rubber or textured handle helps, and so does a handle wide enough to hold with a glove on.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A sharp blade is the safety feature. Blunt blades cause the accidents, because the knife drags and then jumps. Keep spares in the case and change the blade when it stops cutting cleanly. A box of blades costs less than a plaster.

Cut away from yourself, and away from your other hand. It sounds obvious, and it is the single habit that would prevent most of the injuries. Where you cannot manage it, use an auto-retract knife.

Ignore the tactical styling. A knife marketed with a camouflage handle or a glass-breaker does nothing extra for a survey. Pay for the lock, the blade change and the grip.

Do not use a knife where a different tool is better. A multitool or a pair of snips is a better answer for cable ties and thin sheet. A knife used as a screwdriver or a lever snaps blades and puts fragments towards your eyes, so keep safety glasses on whenever you do anything forceful.

Mind the rules on carrying. In England and Wales it is an offence to carry a bladed article in a public place without good reason, and a tool needed for work is a good reason, but a knife loose on the passenger seat is hard to explain. Keep it in the tool bag or belt and take it out of your pocket before you go into a shop.

Questions I get asked

What is the best Stanley knife? For general use I would choose the FatMax Xtreme retractable for the better body and standard blades, and the 99E if you want the cheapest knife that still works. If you want the blade to return on its own, the Stanley auto-retract is the one to look at.

What is the safest type of utility knife? An auto-retract safety knife, because the blade goes back as soon as you release it. A retractable knife is the next step, and an exposed fixed blade is the least safe. Which is right depends on how often you cut and who is doing the cutting.

Can I use a utility knife on loft insulation? Yes. A sharp blade cuts mineral wool and PIR board cleanly, and a blunt one tears it. Wear a respirator and gloves in a loft, because the fibre and dust are a bigger problem than the knife.

How often should I change the blade? When it stops cutting cleanly, which in insulation or plasterboard can be after a handful of cuts. Do not try to stretch a blade, because the extra force you put in is what causes the slip.
