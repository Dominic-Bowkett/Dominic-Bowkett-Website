Boiler service cost
in 2026

A boiler service typically costs £70–£120 in 2026, rising to £100–£180 for an oil-fired system. It's one of the few checks in this series where skipping it can quietly void your manufacturer warranty as well as your safety — and the more interesting question, once you've priced it, is whether a £200-a-year cover plan on top is actually buying you anything.

Published 25 Sep 2026 Reading time · 10 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

An annual boiler service in 2026 typically costs £70–£120 for a gas boiler, with a straightforward combi at the bottom of that range and a system or regular boiler — with a hot water cylinder and extra valves to check — usually nearer £100–£150. An oil-fired service typically runs higher again, £100–£180, mostly because the engineer inspects the tank and pipework and replaces the nozzle and filter as routine, not just checks for faults. London and the South East, Dom's patch included, generally run 20–30% above the rest of the country.

The fee isn't the interesting number, though. Every major manufacturer — Worcester Bosch, Vaillant, Baxi, Ideal, Viessmann — makes an annual service by a Gas Safe registered engineer a condition of keeping the guarantee valid, and plenty of homeowners assume it has to be the manufacturer's own engineer. It doesn't. And a genuine live question this year is whether a £15–£25-a-month cover plan is worth having at all, given what it's actually protecting you against.

I'm a building surveyor and energy assessor, not a Gas Safe registered engineer: I don't service or repair boilers myself, only a Gas Safe registered engineer can legally do that on a gas appliance. What follows is 2026 pricing cross-checked against current UK trade and consumer-body sources, what a proper service should actually involve, the warranty condition most people misunderstand, and an honest look at whether a service plan is worth paying for.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 25 September 2026 against Checkatrade, MyBuilder and Uswitch cost guides, cross-referenced against a Which? member survey of over 10,000 UK adults published June 2025 — the strongest single source found, being an independent survey rather than an aggregator's estimate.

That last row matters more than it looks. A standalone breakdown call-out — no heating, a leak, a dead pump — typically costs £120–£750, averaging around £300, since it's unplanned and may need parts sourced or a second visit. A fault caught during a routine service is cheaper: Which?'s survey found the average on-the-day fix cost £156. Both figures are genuine; they're answering different questions, and neither is "the" average repair cost on its own.

What drives the price

Boiler type and fuel. A combi is quickest and cheapest to service because everything's in one unit. A system or regular boiler takes longer, since the engineer also checks the cylinder, expansion vessel and additional valves. Oil sits above both — OFTEC-registered engineers are a smaller trade than Gas Safe ones, and a proper oil service includes work gas servicing doesn't (see below).

Whether it's a genuine service or a box-tick. The biggest quality variable, and it doesn't always show up in the price. A proper service takes the best part of an hour; one over in fifteen minutes, with no printed combustion figures handed over and nobody having checked the magnetic filter or inhibitor level, has skipped most of what earns the fee. Ask to see the flue gas analyser readings before the engineer packs up — a real service produces a number, not a verbal "all fine."

Combining it with a gas safety certificate. If you're a landlord, an engineer already on site can usually test every other gas appliance for less than booking the two separately — see my gas safety certificate cost guide.

Region. London and the South East commonly run toward the top of these ranges or above them; the figures above assume a straightforward, accessible boiler rather than one hidden behind boxed-in pipework.

Warranty status, not brand loyalty. Keeping most manufacturer warranties valid only requires any Gas Safe registered engineer, not one from the manufacturer's own network. The exception is at the top end — Worcester Bosch's longest warranty tier, for instance, needs a Worcester Bosch Accredited Installer at the point of original installation — but the ongoing annual service doesn't usually need to go through the brand at all.

What a proper service actually involves

The checklist behind a genuine service is fairly consistent across manufacturers and trade guidance: a visual inspection of the casing, pipework and surrounding area for corrosion or leaks; the flue checked internally and at the external terminal for secure joints and unobstructed airflow; the casing opened and the burner and heat exchanger examined and cleaned; the combustion chamber seals checked; gas pressure and flow rate tested; and, critically, a flue gas analysis with a calibrated carbon monoxide analyser, comparing CO and CO2 readings against the manufacturer's limits. Safety devices are tested and the whole thing logged and signed in the boiler's Benchmark logbook or the manufacturer's digital record — the paperwork a warranty claim will ask to see.

A magnetic filter (if one's fitted) and the system's chemical inhibitor level are worth asking about specifically, since they're cheap to check and easy to skip. A filter catches the black sludge that builds up in a heating system over years and is a common cause of noisy, inefficient running if it's never emptied; inhibitor stops that sludge forming and needs occasional topping up, not a one-off dose for life.

The warranty condition

Every manufacturer I checked directly — Vaillant, Baxi and Viessmann on their own warranty pages, Worcester Bosch and Ideal via consistent secondary confirmation — makes the same deal: register the boiler within 30 days of installation, then have it serviced every year after by a Gas Safe registered engineer, with the service logged. Miss a year and the guarantee can be voided outright; several manufacturers describe a missed service as a common reason claims get refused. None state an exact grace period either side of the anniversary date, so I'd treat "exactly once a year" as the safe rule rather than something to cut close.

The useful correction: this doesn't need to be the manufacturer's own engineer — any Gas Safe registered engineer satisfies the condition on every brand checked, so it's worth not paying a premium for a "manufacturer-approved" service if a competent local engineer, properly logged, does the same job for less.

Gas vs oil servicing

Only a Gas Safe registered engineer may legally work on a gas boiler. For oil, OFTEC registration is the industry-standard scheme and the sensible one to insist on, but it isn't quite the same legal mandate: an engineer outside the scheme can still do the work, provided the job is notified to the local authority's building control department separately. Using an OFTEC engineer is what lets the work self-certify without that extra step — the real reason it's worth insisting on, not a technicality.

The service itself covers more ground: tank, pipework and connections checked for leaks or contamination, and the nozzle and oil line filter typically replaced as routine, alongside the same pump, electrode and combustion checks a gas service covers. That extra scope is most of the reason an oil service costs more, even though a few aggregator sites confusingly price it as cheaper — usually because they're pricing a bare safety check, not the fuller service most OFTEC engineers actually carry out.

Repair or replace, and what a fault actually costs

A standalone boiler repair typically costs £120–£750, averaging around £300, on top of a call-out fee of roughly £60–£100, more like £120–£180 outside normal hours. A straightforward thermostat or minor part sits toward the bottom, a failed pump or circuit board toward the top, though individual per-fault figures vary between trade sources, so I'd treat the overall range as the reliable number rather than any single component price.

The replace decision is less about age alone than the pattern: once a boiler is past 10-12 years old and needs its second or third repair within a couple of years, or parts are hard to source, the maths usually tips toward replacing it — see my new boiler cost guide. Efficiency is the quieter factor: an old, tired boiler can run at 70-80% efficiency against 90%+ for a well-maintained modern one, which is money leaving through the flue whether or not anything's visibly wrong yet.

Are boiler service plans worth it?

Here the honest-broker answer beats the price comparison. British Gas HomeCare runs around £22 a month (£264 a year) for unlimited call-outs and parts and labour minus an excess; Boxt's standalone Boiler Care is cheaper at around £9 a month; others sit in between. Which?'s own survey put the average annual cost of cover at £183, up to £300 for some households — and found that teaser first-year pricing jumping sharply at renewal is a genuine, documented problem: one major provider's basic cover rose from around £150 in year one to over £370 in year two for the same policy.

Set that against what you're insuring: a service costs £80–£150, and even a standalone repair averages around £300 and doesn't happen every year. Paying £183–£300 annually to cover a repair that might cost roughly the same and might not happen at all only clearly pays off if something goes wrong more than once. Which? and MoneySavingExpert have both independently concluded most people are better off paying for the service and self-insuring, with cover earning its keep mainly once a boiler is past about five years old or out of warranty.

That's the point I'd actually act on. While a boiler's still under manufacturer warranty — which already requires the same annual service and typically already covers parts and labour — a cover plan on top is often paying twice for the same few years. Check your warranty end date before renewing a plan, not after; it tells you more than the monthly price does.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Gas Safe (or OFTEC for oil) registration checked, not just claimed — and the categories on the card match your appliance.
  • Printed or recorded combustion analysis figures handed over at the end, not a verbal assurance.
  • The magnetic filter and inhibitor level actually checked, not skipped past.
  • The service logged in the Benchmark book or the manufacturer's digital record — an unlogged service is close to worthless if you ever need to claim on the warranty.
  • Your warranty end date, before you renew a cover plan — not the other way round.

Questions I get asked

How often should a boiler be serviced? Once a year, every year, for as long as you own it — not just while it's under warranty. No manufacturer I checked states a specific grace period either side of the anniversary date, so the safest assumption is exactly once a year rather than banking on any tolerance.

Is a boiler service the same as a gas safety certificate? No. A service is a manufacturer warranty condition covering the boiler itself; a gas safety certificate is a landlord's legal duty under the Gas Safety Regulations 1998 covering every gas appliance in the property. Many engineers do both on the same visit for less than booking separately, but neither one satisfies the other on its own.

Is a boiler service plan worth it? Usually not while the boiler's still under manufacturer warranty, since the warranty already requires the same service and typically already covers parts and labour. Which? and MoneySavingExpert have both independently found most people are better off self-insuring, with cover earning its keep mainly once a boiler is past about five years old or out of warranty.

What happens if I skip a boiler service? The warranty is the first thing at risk — a missed service is a common reason claims get refused. The quieter risk is safety: a service is largely how a failing flue or a creeping carbon monoxide problem gets caught early. Efficiency drifts too, sometimes down to 70-80% against 90%+ for a properly maintained boiler.
