Drying washing indoors puts litres of water into the air, which is why I get called to so many condensation complaints. A heat pump tumble dryer collects that water in a tank instead. Here is how to choose one, what it costs to run against the alternatives, and seven machines worth looking at.

Half the condensation jobs I am called to have the same root: a family drying a week’s washing indoors. A single full load of wet laundry holds a couple of litres of water, and unless it goes somewhere it ends up on the coldest wall, behind the wardrobe on the external wall, or as black spotting on a bedroom ceiling. I do not write up a clothes airer as a defect, but I do write up the mould it feeds. That is the honest case for a tumble dryer: it moves the water out of the air and into a tank you can pour down the sink.

A heat pump dryer does that for roughly a third of the electricity of an older vented or condenser machine. It works like a small fridge in reverse: warm air passes over the wet clothes, a heat exchanger chills the air to condense the water out, and the same air is reheated and reused. There is no vent hose through a wall and no hot damp air dumped into the room. The price you pay is cycle time, usually two to three hours for a full load, and a higher purchase price than a basic condenser dryer.

I should say plainly where a dryer is the wrong answer. If the house has a ventilation problem, with no extractor in the bathroom, sealed-up trickle vents or a damp cellar, a dryer will treat the symptom while the cause carries on. Read the damp survey cost guide if you are not sure whether you have a moisture problem or just a washing problem, and see the heated clothes airers guide if a dryer is not an option in your home. A dehumidifier, covered in the dehumidifiers guide, is the other half of the answer in houses that stay damp.

How I judge a heat pump tumble dryer

Running cost per load, not the sticker price. A heat pump dryer typically uses around 1.5 to 2.5 kWh a cycle against 4 to 5 kWh for a vented or condenser machine, going by the energy labels. At roughly 25p a kilowatt-hour that is about 40 to 60p a load against £1 to £1.25. A household drying four loads a week saves in the region of £100 a year, so a dearer machine takes a few years to pay back.

Condenser care. The heat exchanger clogs with fluff that gets past the door filter. Self-cleaning designs rinse it with the condensate; cheaper machines need you to pull it out and rinse it under a tap every month or so. Skip that and the cycle times creep upwards.

Where it will live. Heat pump dryers want a room between roughly 5 and 35 degrees. An unheated garage in January is outside that, and a cupboard with no air gap will cook the machine. Check the rear and door-swing clearances before you buy, as I check loft hatches before quoting a boarding job.

Drum size against the real wash. Eight kilos is plenty for two adults; a family with duvets wants nine. Sensor drying, which stops the cycle when the load is dry, protects clothes and saves energy, whereas a timer-only machine will over-dry.

Condensate handling. Every machine collects water in a tank that must be emptied, and most accept a drain hose into a standpipe or washing machine waste. If you have a utility room with a waste, fit the hose and never carry the tank again.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A dryer does not cure damp. It stops you drying clothes on radiators and airers, which removes one large source of indoor moisture. It does nothing for a leaking gutter, a bridged damp course or a bathroom with no extractor. If the staining is on an external wall or a chimney breast, get it diagnosed first.

Ventilate the room it sits in. A heat pump dryer gives off heat and a little moisture from the machine itself. In a tight utility room or cupboard, an extractor or an air brick matters; the extractor fans guide covers what to fit. Houses with continuous mechanical ventilation, as in the MVHR installation cost guide, cope better still.

Energy label figures are test figures. The kWh per cycle on the label is for a standard cotton load. Real loads, mixed fabrics and cold rooms push the figure up. Treat the label as a fair way of comparing machines, not a promise of your bill.

What gets pushed that matters less. Dozens of programmes, steam refresh cycles and app control. A nice-to-have in a garage, nothing more. Sensor drying, a clean condenser and a drum large enough for the family wash are what repay you.

Rented homes. Landlords sometimes ban vented dryers because of the hole through the wall; a heat pump dryer needs no vent and is the easier case to make. Check the tenancy before assuming.

Questions I get asked

Are heat pump tumble dryers worth the extra money? For a household drying several loads a week, yes: the running cost is roughly a third of a vented or condenser machine, so the premium over a basic condenser dryer pays back in a few years. For one person drying a load a week, a cheaper machine or an airer is fine.

Do heat pump dryers stop condensation? They stop the moisture from drying clothes reaching the walls, which is the main indoor source in many homes. They do not fix leaks, rising moisture or poor ventilation, and a house with those problems will stay damp. See the rising damp treatment cost guide if you suspect something deeper.

Do they need a vent? No. Water is collected in a tank or piped to a drain, and the air is recycled inside the machine. That is why they suit flats and rented homes where a vent hole is not an option.

How do I look after one? Empty the filter every load, empty the tank or fit a drain hose, and clean the condenser on machines that are not self-cleaning. Leave a gap around the machine and keep the room above freezing.

If damp or mould is the reason you are shopping, a ventilation assessment or a building survey will tell you whether a dryer is the right spend; get in touch if you would like one done properly. For the rest of the home-comfort kit, there are more field-kit guides.
