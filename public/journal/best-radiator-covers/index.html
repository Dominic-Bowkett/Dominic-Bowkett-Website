Radiator covers are one of the first things I notice in a cold hallway. A tidy MDF cabinet with a shelf on top, painted to match the skirting, and behind it a radiator doing about three-quarters of its job. I am usually in the house for an EPC or a condensation call, and the cover is rarely mentioned, because it has been there for years and looks like part of the room.

So here is the honest position. A cover is a decorative choice, not a heating upgrade. Studies referenced by the Energy Saving Trust suggest a poorly designed cover can cut output by 20–30%, and a Liverpool John Moores University study found wooden covers can cut it by as much as 40%. A radiator hidden behind a solid box heats the box and the wall, not the room. If you want a cover for safety around children, to hide pipework, or for the shelf, there are designs that lose much less than others. That is what this guide covers.

I have also included radiator fans, because they are the other thing people ask about when a radiator is blocked by furniture or sits behind a curtain. They are cheap, modest and not magic. Neither a cover nor a fan changes your EPC rating, since the rating is built from the heating system and the fabric, not the furniture in front of it. For what does move it, see the EPC cost guide.

How I judge a radiator cover

How open is the front? This is the whole question. A solid front blocks radiated heat and chokes airflow. A slatted, lattice or mesh front lets both through. As a rough rule, look for 30–50% of the front to be open. If the listing does not say, count the slats.

Is there a gap? UK advice is for at least 5cm of clearance to the sides and 3cm above and in front of the radiator. A cover that touches the radiator traps heat and can warp MDF, and one with no space above stops the convection that carries heat into the room.

What is it made of? MDF is cheap and takes paint, but it absorbs heat and swells if it gets wet. If a valve ever weeps, the cabinet next to it will tell you. Metal and magnetic covers lose less than wood, but cost more.

Does it fit the radiator you have? Measure the radiator including valves, pipes and the thermostatic head. A TRV sitting inside a closed cover reads the temperature of the cabinet, not the room, so the radiator shuts off early. The smart radiator valves guide covers remote sensing.

Can you reach the valves? If you cannot bleed or isolate the radiator without taking the cabinet apart, you will not bother, and the radiator will slowly fill with air.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The cover is not a heating upgrade. If a salesman, or a product listing, claims a cover improves efficiency, be sceptical. At best it loses a little less than another cover. Magnetic and metal covers can reduce the penalty compared with a wooden cabinet, but none of them beats a bare radiator on output.

Reflector foil does more than a cover. If the real problem is heat disappearing into an external wall, foil behind the radiator is the cheap measure, and the draught-proofing kit guide covers it. It works best on solid walls, where the wall is coldest.

Curtains over the radiator are worse than a cover. A curtain hanging in front of a radiator pushes the heat up to the glass and out of the window. Stop the curtain at the sill, or see the thermal curtains and blinds guide for the condensation trap that closing them creates.

A cover will not cure condensation, and can hide it. Black mould on the wall behind a radiator cover is a common find in my reports, and a cover means nobody looks. A hygrometer tells you the room humidity, and the dehumidifier guide covers the symptom.

Drying washing on the cover. The top shelf is tempting, and it loads a damp room with moisture. A heated clothes airer is a better answer.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. Savings claims in pounds per year, and any radiator fan promising to cut your bill by a third. The figures are the maker’s own. A fan that moves warm air off the back of a radiator can help a little in a cold room; it does not make a cold house warm.

The better question first. Before buying anything for a radiator, check it works. Cold at the top means air; cold at the bottom means sludge. A magnetic filter and a bleed key cost less than any cover and do more.

Questions I get asked

Do radiator covers block heat? Yes, to some degree. A solid-fronted cover can cut output noticeably, and published studies put the loss at between 20% and 40% for poorly designed or wooden covers. An open slatted or mesh front with proper clearances loses less, but no cover makes a radiator better.

Do radiator covers save energy? Not directly. A cover does not reduce the heat the boiler has to make. In some rooms it makes the radiator work harder for the same comfort, which costs more, not less. The best you can say is that a well-ventilated cover is a modest penalty for a nicer room.

Do radiator fans work? A little. They move air that would otherwise rise slowly or stick behind a curtain. Owner feedback is mixed, noise is a common complaint, and the savings claims on the packaging are not independently verified. Try one on a single problem radiator before buying several.

Is it safe to put a cover on a radiator? Yes, with clearance. A properly spaced MDF cover is fine on a hot-water radiator at normal flow temperatures. Keep it clear of pipes and valves, never put one over an electric heater, and do not store anything flammable on top.
