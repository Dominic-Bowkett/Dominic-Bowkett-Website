Surveyor’s notes
7 October 2026:
EPC cost gap

What EPC compliance really costs, a conference remark about the Renters’ Rights Act, London’s shrinking rental supply and Scotland’s new damp rules. Here is what each means on the ground in the South East.

Published 7 October 2026 Reading time · 6 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A quieter day than yesterday, but a useful one for anyone who owns older property. A bank has put a number on what EPC compliance really costs, the opposition has said something about the Renters’ Rights Act and then clarified it, and the London rental figures keep refusing to behave. Scotland, meanwhile, switched on its own damp and mould rules this morning.

Barclays says EPC C costs £9,000 a home, not £6,500

Property118 reports on a Barclays study called “Closing the Energy Efficiency Gap”. It puts the typical cost of reaching compliance at around £9,000 per property, against a government projection of £6,100 to £6,800. It also says 38% of landlords expect difficulty meeting the proposed standards, and that 42% name regulatory uncertainty as their biggest barrier. Barclays’ Jatin Patel is quoted as saying “willingness alone will not deliver the scale of change required”. I have not seen the full report, and a lender has its own reasons to stress the need for finance, so treat the headline figure as one data point.

It is a plausible one, though. An average hides a huge spread. A 1990s semi with a loft top-up and a heating controls upgrade may be cheap to move up a band. A solid-walled Victorian terrace or a timber-framed cottage in the Weald is a different job entirely, and cutting corners on that sort of house is how you end up with damp, which is why PAS 2035 exists. My bet is that the real cost curve is steep at the older end of the stock, and that is exactly where small landlords hold their houses.

What to do: do not wait for the final rules to find out where you stand. Get a current EPC, read the recommendations list properly, and have someone who has stood in the loft price the measures against your actual building. A landlord who knows the number in 2026 has options. One who finds out in 2029 has a queue of contractors.

The Conservatives said the Renters’ Rights Act “has got to go”, then clarified

The Negotiator reports that Shadow Housing Secretary Katie Lam, speaking at the Conservative conference, said of the Act: “I don’t care where it comes from. It’s got to go.” The article says the party later clarified that she was referring to broader legislative challenges for landlords, not announcing formal policy. It also covers a pledge to scrap Stamp Duty, which it says would remove a barrier to moving home. Propertymark’s Timothy Douglas is quoted welcoming planning reform but warning that “planning reform alone will not solve the housing crisis”. I covered the Future Homes Standard side of the conference announcements yesterday, so I will not repeat it.

The practical point is that nothing has changed. The Act is in force and the opposition is not in government. The danger for landlords is treating a conference soundbite as a reason to delay. If you are in the middle of a tenancy dispute or planning a sale, you are dealing with the law as it stands today.

Stamp Duty abolition would matter to buyers, who would stop budgeting for it. But a pledge is not a policy, and I would not time a purchase around it. If anything it is another reason to put the money into a proper survey rather than haggling over a tax that may never go.

London asking rents are up 6.1%, and the supply story is the real news

Landlord Today reports that online agency Hello Neighbour found advertised rents in Greater London rose 6.1% year on year in September, to an average of £2,448. It says available rental properties are down 6% on a year ago, and down 13% in inner London, with enquiries per property falling to 34 from 45 a year earlier. The agency’s argument is that supply constraints, not demand, are pushing rents up, and that the Act’s bar on offers above asking price has led landlords to set higher starting rents. A separate Landlord Today piece reports a SpareRoom survey in which 27% of landlords say they are leaving the market entirely and 36% are reducing their portfolios.

Both are agency and platform data, not official statistics, and advertised rents are not achieved rents, as Hello Neighbour itself notes. I would read them as direction, not measurement. What matters for a surveyor is where the sold stock goes. Ex-rental houses coming onto the market in Sussex and Kent are often the ones with the oldest boilers, the original windows and an EPC nobody has looked at in years.

If you are buying one, assume deferred maintenance and get a survey that looks at damp, the roof and the electrics. If you are a landlord thinking of selling, a recent EPC and a clean maintenance record will protect your price better than a fresh coat of paint.

Scotland now has damp and mould repair deadlines, and England is watching

Per Property118, citing Propertymark guidance, Scottish landlords must from 6 October begin investigating reported damp and mould within 10 working days, give the tenant a written summary within three working days of finishing, and start remedial work within five working days where a substantial problem is found. The same change raises the penalty for wrongful termination from six months’ rent to between 3 and 36 times the relevant statutory sum, and cuts the qualifying period for tenancy succession from 12 months to 6.

This is Scotland, so it does not bind anyone in the South East. But it points the same way as Awaab’s Law for social landlords in England, and my bet is that private-sector timescales follow. Damp and mould is the most common problem I am called to, and in most cases the cause is not what the tenant or landlord first assumes. Condensation, a failed gutter and rising damp all look alike on a bedroom wall and need different fixes.

If you let property anywhere, the habit worth building now is simple: a dated record of when a complaint arrived, who looked, what they found and what was done. Whatever the final English rules say, that record is your defence.

What I’d actually do

  1. Landlords of pre-1950 stock: get a current EPC and have the measures priced against the real building, not a spreadsheet average.
  2. Landlords generally: keep operating on the law as it is, not on a conference soundbite.
  3. Buyers of ex-rental homes: commission a building survey and assume deferred maintenance until shown otherwise.
  4. Anyone letting a property: start a dated damp and mould complaint log today.
  5. Homeowners planning retrofit: get a PAS 2035 assessment before insulation, so you fix the cause of damp rather than hide it.
