Drone survey
cost in 2026

A drone survey typically costs £250–£600 for a house roof inspection in 2026, and upwards of £800 for a land or topographic survey — in both cases a fraction of what scaffold or a traditional survey can cost for the same information, though not always the right tool for the job.

Published 31 Aug 2026 Reading time · 9 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A drone survey typically costs £250–£600 for a standard house roof inspection in 2026, rising to £600–£1,500 or more for a larger, more complex or commercial roof. A land or topographic drone survey is a different job again, usually starting around £800 for a small site and running well into four figures for anything larger or more complicated. In every case the appeal is the same: a camera gets somewhere quickly and safely that would otherwise take a person, a ladder, or a great deal more equipment to reach.

What the fee buys, on a roof, is a set of high-resolution photographs of every elevation — ridge, valleys, chimney, flashings, gutters — flown by a CAA-registered pilot and usually written up as a short condition summary rather than handed over as a raw folder of images. On a land survey, the drone instead captures overlapping aerial photographs that get processed into a scaled site plan, contour map or 3D model an architect or engineer can design against — the same job a traditional topographical survey does, but from the air rather than on foot with a GPS rover.

I should say upfront: I fly drones myself and offer roof surveys as part of my own practice, so I have a direct interest in this one that I do not have in most of what I write about here. What follows is still researched against the wider UK market rather than just my own price list, and I have tried to be just as honest about when a drone survey is the wrong tool — when what you actually need is a person on the roof, in the loft, or a scaffold going up regardless — as I am when it is the obviously better option.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 31 August 2026 against current 2026 pricing from UK drone survey operators, cross-referenced against each other and against my own pricing for the roof-survey end of the market. Quotes are usually given plus VAT. As with the rest of this series, London and the South East — Dom’s patch included — commonly trend above the rest of the UK for a comparable job.

A thermal roof survey — useful for spotting missing insulation, damp roof timbers or a failing flat-roof membrane, which all show up as a temperature difference — adds a several-hundred-pound premium to the base figure, since it needs a different sensor, the right time of day to fly, and more processing afterwards. On the land-survey side, day rates and per-hectare pricing are also common: expect roughly £350–£3,000 per hectare depending on terrain, or £450–£850 for a straightforward half-day job, rising sharply if the site needs LiDAR rather than standard photogrammetry.

What drives the price

Size and complexity. Multiple roof elevations, dormers and valleys, or a large or awkwardly-shaped plot, all add flight time and processing time afterwards.

The deliverable. Raw photographs cost less than a written condition report; a photogrammetry site plan costs less than a full contour survey or LiDAR capture.

Access and airspace. Urban sites, tall trees, overhead wires and proximity to an airport’s controlled airspace — this corner of the South East sits under Gatwick’s — all add planning time before the aircraft leaves the ground.

Weather. Drones do not fly safely in rain or strong wind, so a visit occasionally has to move a day, which can carry a small rescheduling cost with some operators.

Pilot registration and insurance. Legitimate commercial drone work requires CAA registration — an Operator ID and a Flyer ID — and insurance meeting EC 785/2004. That is factored into a properly run operator’s price, and it is usually the corner being cut when a quote undercuts everyone else by a wide margin.

Turnaround. Rush delivery in two to three days instead of the standard week or so commonly carries a premium of a quarter to a half on top of the base price.

Travel. A site more than an hour from the operator’s base often carries a mobilisation fee on top of the headline figure.

Drone vs scaffold vs a traditional survey

For a straightforward look at a roof’s condition — before buying, after a storm, chasing a leak, or satisfying an insurer or managing agent — a drone is almost always the cheaper and faster route. Scaffolding to get a person safely onto a roof for a single day’s inspection typically costs £450–£1,100 for a single elevation or an access tower, once erection, a short hire period and dismantling are all included, and a full-house wrap for a longer job runs £800–£2,800 or more across a typical six-to-eight-week hire. A cherry picker or mobile elevating work platform is a lighter-touch alternative at £150–£600 a day, but it still needs a competent operative, safe ground conditions to set up on, and takes longer to arrange than a drone visit booked for the same week. Set against any of that, £250–£600 for a same-day drone inspection is not a close contest.

That calculation changes the moment actual work is planned rather than just an inspection. If a roof needs re-pointing, ridge tiles re-bedding, or flashings replacing, the scaffold or tower is going up anyway to let someone carry out the work — at which point a drone survey beforehand is an optional extra to help scope the job, not an alternative to the access cost itself. The saving a drone offers only really exists when the access it replaces would otherwise be the whole cost of the job, which is the case for a stand-alone condition check and not the case for a repair.

On the land-survey side the comparison is closer, and less one-sided than a lot of drone operators’ own marketing suggests. On a small, straightforward plot — a garden or single house plot — a traditional topographical survey typically costs about the same as a drone-based one: both land somewhere around £400–£900. Drone photogrammetry pulls ahead on larger, more open sites, where it covers ground far faster than a surveyor walking with a GPS rover. It loses that edge on steep, densely vegetated or heavily obstructed sites, where a drone can struggle to see the actual ground through tree cover, and a traditional total-station or GPS survey — or LiDAR, at a real cost premium — is often still the more accurate and reliable choice. Ask which method a quote actually proposes, and why, rather than assuming “drone” automatically means “cheaper”.

When a drone survey isn’t the right tool

A drone roof survey is a visual inspection of the outside of the roof, and a good one is honest about the fact that is all it is. It does not see inside the roof structure — rafters, battens, felt and insulation still need eyes in the loft — and a standard camera drone is not a substitute for a proper structural survey if there is reason to think a roof is actually moving rather than just ageing. It is also weather- and airspace-dependent in a way scaffold is not: once someone is up there, they are up there regardless of a stiff breeze, whereas a drone pilot who will not fly in poor conditions is doing the responsible thing, not making excuses.

The same honesty applies on the land-survey side. A drone-based topographical survey is excellent for producing a site plan quickly, but if a boundary is disputed, or the output needs to carry the legal weight of a proper land-registry-grade survey, that is a job for a chartered land surveyor working to the relevant standard, not a photogrammetry model, however good the imagery looks.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • CAA registration and insurance confirmed — an Operator ID and a Flyer ID, and commercial liability insurance meeting EC 785/2004; ask, and expect a straight answer rather than a vague reassurance.
  • The deliverable specified — raw photographs, a written condition report, or, for land work, a CAD file such as DWG rather than only a PDF.
  • Fixed fee agreed before the visit, and what happens to that fee if weather forces a reschedule.
  • Survey scale and accuracy stated for land work, not just “aerial mapping” as a vague description.
  • Thermal, LiDAR or 3D modelling itemised separately if you need them, rather than assumed as part of a headline photography price.
  • Turnaround stated against your deadline, and the cost of expediting it if your timeline tightens.

Questions I get asked

How much does a drone roof survey cost in 2026? A standard house typically costs £250–£600 for a same-day inspection with a written condition report. A larger, more complex or commercial roof runs £600–£1,500 or more, and a thermal add-on typically adds several hundred pounds on top.

Is a drone survey cheaper than scaffolding? For a stand-alone inspection, yes, usually by a wide margin — scaffold access for a single day’s look typically costs £450–£1,100 once erection and a short hire are included, against £250–£600 for a drone visit. If work is being carried out rather than just inspected, the scaffold is going up regardless, so the comparison stops being meaningful.

Do I need to check anything before hiring a drone survey company? Yes — ask for the operator’s CAA Operator ID and Flyer ID and confirmation of commercial insurance meeting EC 785/2004. Flying for payment without these is illegal, and an uninsured operator leaves you exposed if anything goes wrong over your property or a neighbour’s.

Can a drone survey replace a full building survey? No. A drone survey is a visual check of what a camera can see from the air, almost always the roof. It does not look inside the structure, assess damp, movement or services, or give the wider condition picture a full building survey does — it is a genuinely useful piece of evidence to bring into that bigger picture, not a substitute for it.

A drone gives you a good look at a roof; it does not tell you whether the rest of the building is sound, or whether what is showing on the roof is connected to a problem somewhere else in the structure.
