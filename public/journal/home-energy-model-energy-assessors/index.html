The Home Energy
Model: what it means
for assessors

The Home Energy Model will replace SAP and RdSAP behind every EPC. The certificate goes from one rating to four answers, the survey changes, and most assessors will need training. Here’s what has actually been decided, what is still only proposed, and what you can do now.

What the Home Energy Model is

SAP works out a home’s energy use in twelve monthly steps. The Home Energy Model (HEM) is based on BS EN ISO 52016-1 in its half-hourly form: 17,520 steps a year. That lets it see when energy is used, so it can value solar used on site, batteries, heat pumps on cheap overnight rates and smart controls in a way SAP can’t.

It’s built as a core engine plus “wrappers”: one wrapper for the Future Homes Standard (Part L), another for EPCs. The government’s October 2025 response confirmed a single central, cloud-based engine, delivered through MHCLG as Energy Calculation as a Service. Your software becomes the front end, and every provider gets the same result from the same inputs. The reference code is published openly.

Existing EPCs keep their ten-year validity, and reformed EPCs will too. The partial government response on the wider reforms also says an EPC will be needed at the point a property is marketed, and plans to widen scope to whole HMOs, short-term lets and heritage homes.

The new EPC: four headline metrics

  • Fabric performance – proposed to use Fabric Energy Efficiency, with the C/D boundary close to today’s.
  • Heating system – efficiency and carbon of heating, hot water and cooking. Under the proposals, heat pumps and low-carbon heat networks would always score C or above, and no primary fossil-fuel system would reach a C.
  • Smart readiness – a 1–100 score covering smart meters, solar PV, storage, smart EV charge points, smart controls and the grid connection. A smart meter is proposed as essential for a C.
  • Energy cost – proposed as an estimated annual bill in pounds, not a band.

For landlords, the private rented standard is fabric first, then either heating system or smart readiness, with a £10,000 cost cap.

How the survey is likely to change

RdSAP is all or nothing: it can’t be mixed with full SAP inputs, so better data often can’t be used. The proposal for existing homes is a modular approach. For each part of the home you either enter full, evidenced data or switch to a simplified input with defaults. The first release would have one set of simplified inputs, so switching them all on feels like RdSAP.

Based on the proposed metrics, expect to record more about cooking fuel, smart meters, PV and batteries, EV chargers, the supply phase and heating controls. Appliances are in the model, but in the new-build wrapper they’re standard assumptions, with the input simplified to whether each one is installed. So you won’t be metering fridges.

Will it take longer, or cost more? There’s no official figure for either. Claims online that surveys will take “two to three times longer” don’t trace back to any government source. Time a few of your own jobs now, so you have a baseline when the final data list is published.

Training

Almost certainly, yes. The government says it will work with the sector so schemes and assessors are equipped, and decisions on EPC quality, training and CPD are due in its final response. The only official estimate of training time is for new-build SAP assessors: the Future Homes Standard impact assessment puts it at about 26 hours on the new requirements plus 44 hours on SAP and HEM, 70 hours in all. There’s no published equivalent for DEAs doing existing homes.

What you can do now

  • Keep today’s RdSAP evidence and photos tight; the modular approach rewards evidence.
  • Start noting smart meters, PV, batteries, EV chargers, cooking fuel and supply phase.
  • Learn the basics of heat pumps and solar PV.
  • Read the January 2026 consultation, and watch for your scheme’s HEM updates.
  • On-construction assessors: plan for SAP 10.3 and HEM from March 2027.
