A ratchet strap doesn’t get much thought until the morning it’s needed and the only thing left in the van is a coil of rope that’s seen better days. Most survey kit travels loose in some sense — a ladder laid flat, a podium-step platform against the bulkhead, a tool box wedged between the wheel arches — and none of it stays put once a van’s cornering or braking hard. A properly rated strap, tensioned against a fixed anchor point, is what keeps a heavy or awkward load from becoming a hazard to the driver, the kit, or whoever’s standing nearby when the back doors open.

This overlaps with kit already covered elsewhere. A telescopic ladder or a set of podium steps is the thing being secured, not the thing doing the securing; the straps below hold them in place once loaded, along with a scaffold tower’s frame sections on the occasional job that means moving one by van rather than having it delivered. Anyone running a van storage system will find a couple of these useful for lashing a box down inside it rather than just setting it on the floor.

How I judge a ratchet strap

Safe working load, not the breaking strain printed largest. Every strap in this guide states a breaking strain that’s roughly double its safe working load (SWL) or Lashing Capacity (LC) — the smaller number is the one that should decide a purchase, since it already carries the safety margin built in.

Webbing width matched to what’s actually being secured. A 25mm strap is plenty for a tool box or a single ladder; a 50mm strap spreads load and grips better against a smoother platform like a podium-step deck or a scaffold tower’s boarded section.

Hook type, and whether it stays put. An S-hook that isn’t retained can bounce off its anchor point on a rutted access track or a pot-holed yard; a retention clip, or a coated hook designed not to mark paintwork, is a small detail that matters more in practice than it looks on a spec sheet.

A ratchet mechanism built to survive living in a van. Straps get dropped, sat on and left damp after a wet job; a corrosion-resistant ratchet housing and stitching reinforced around the hooks are the parts that fail first on a cheap strap.

A stated certification behind the numbers. GS Mark, TÜV, or a quoted EN 12195-2 standard turns a manufacturer’s claimed capacity into something independently checked, not just a number on a label.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A longer ratchet handle isn’t a gimmick on a heavier load. More leverage genuinely makes it easier to tension a strap against a bulky item like a scaffold tower frame; on a tool box or single ladder it makes no practical difference, so it’s not worth paying extra for on the lighter straps.

Retractable webbing is a convenience, not a capacity upgrade. It stops a loose tail flapping or marking paintwork, but adds cost and moving parts without adding rated capacity — buy it for tidiness, not because it holds more.

Buying the longest strap on the shelf just means more to coil and stow. Match the strap length to the anchor points actually fitted in the van, not the biggest number in the listing.

Bright or reflective webbing matters on a roof rack, not inside a closed load area. Several straps in this category are sold in high-vis colours aimed at exposed roof loads — irrelevant for anything secured inside a panel van with the doors shut.

A multi-pack of lighter straps often beats one heavy strap for everyday use. Most days involve securing several separate items — a tool box, a ladder, a drone case — rather than one very heavy one, which is why a four-pack tops this guide over any single higher-capacity strap.

Questions I get asked

Do I need proper ratchet straps, or will bungee cords do for securing van kit?

Bungee cords have no stated safe working load and rely on elastic tension that loosens over a journey rather than staying fixed once set. They’re fine for stopping something rattling around inside a box that’s already secure, but not for holding a ladder, a tool box or a scaffold tower section in place on their own. A ratchet strap tensions positively to a known load and stays where it’s set, the difference that actually matters for anything with real weight to it.

What’s the difference between a ratchet strap and a cam buckle strap?

A cam buckle strap is hand-tightened through a spring-loaded cam and typically rated to a lower working load — quick to use, but with far less achievable tension than a ratchet mechanism gives. A ratchet strap uses mechanical leverage to tension the webbing well beyond what a hand pull could manage, which is what makes it the right choice for anything heavier than light, soft cargo.

How many ratchet straps does a typical survey van actually need?

Four lighter straps covers most daily kit — a stepladder, a tool box, a drone case, a podium-step platform — secured individually or in pairs. One or two heavier-rated straps in reserve are worth having for the occasional job moving a scaffold tower’s frame sections or several ladders bundled together, rather than sizing every strap to the heaviest thing it might ever carry.

Can a ratchet strap alone secure a scaffold tower or podium steps for transport?

The strap only tensions the load against a fixed point — it doesn’t substitute for loading it correctly first. Heavier components belong low and packed against the van’s bulkhead or straight sides, not stacked loose and relied on to stay put purely because a strap’s holding the top of the pile.
