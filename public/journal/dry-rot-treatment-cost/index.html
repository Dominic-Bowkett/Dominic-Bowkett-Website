Dry rot
treatment cost
in 2026

Treating dry rot typically costs £1,500–£5,000 for a contained outbreak in 2026, and £5,000–£15,000 or more once it has spread. Wet rot is usually far cheaper, and the two are often confused.

Published 2 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Dry rot is a fungal decay of timber, caused by Serpula lacrymans, and it has a worse reputation than it sometimes deserves. In 2026, treating it typically costs £1,500–£5,000 for a contained outbreak in one room, from about £500 for a small patch at a window or skirting up to £5,000–£15,000 or more once it has reached joists, wall plates or masonry. Wet rot, its commoner and milder cousin, usually costs a fraction of that. The chemical is the cheap part. The money goes on cutting out decayed timber, replacing it, drying the building out and making good.

Two things decide the bill: how far it has spread, and whether the water that caused it has been stopped. Both are diagnosis questions, not treatment questions, and that is where I come in. I diagnose buildings and don’t sell timber treatment, so I have no stake in how big the job turns out to be.

This is the 2026 price, what moves it, and what I would want settled before anyone signs a quote.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 2 October 2026 against several UK cost guides (Housey, Squote and Chat To Your Builder) and the ranges quoted from Checkatrade and MyBuilder in search results; their own pages block automated access, so I have treated those two as supporting only. The sources overlap well on the bands and differ more at the top end, where every job is different. Kent, Sussex, Surrey and London tend to run above national averages, but I could not find a reliable figure for how much, so I have not invented one.

Dry rot or wet rot?

The Property Care Association’s guidance note, Fungal Decay in Buildings, says decay always arises because the timber has become wet, in excess of 20% moisture content. Dry rot is the most serious form. Wet rots, such as cellar fungus and pore fungus, occur more often and are typically confined to the damp timber. Wet rot does not spread into walls; dry rot can send strands through mortar and plaster.

The two look alike to an untrained eye and need different responses. The PCA is blunt about it: a dry rot treatment carried out against wet rot will be effective but unnecessary and costly, and wet rot treatment applied against dry rot is less certain of success. For cellar fungus it says it is not necessary to sterilise brickwork. So a firm that finds “dry rot” in every damp skirting is either unlucky or selling the bigger job. I can’t put a number on how often that happens, but the identification is worth having on paper.

Fix the water first

No timber treatment works while the timber is still getting wet. The PCA says the moisture source has to be eliminated as part of the overall repair specification, and that preservatives should not be relied on where timbers stay persistently wet. Typical sources are a leaking gutter or downpipe, a bridged damp-proof course, a leaking pipe, failed flashing, or an unventilated void. Fixing those is often cheap. I’ve covered how damp is diagnosed in damp survey cost, and why misdiagnosed condensation costs people money in rising damp treatment cost.

Then the decayed timber comes out, and replacement timber should be pre-treated to BS 8417 and isolated from damp masonry. On the walls, the PCA says widespread irrigation can rarely be justified, because it can cause a flush of new growth and prolongs drying, and drying is the primary control. Accepted options include surface biocide, plugs, pastes or gels, and localised treatment. If a quote leads with injecting every wall in the house, ask why.

What drives the price

Extent. A skirting board and a floor structure are different jobs. Dry rot found under a ground-floor bay is a very different quote from dry rot that has reached the wall plates and the next room.

Structural timber. Joists, wall plates, lintel ends and roof timbers need propping or temporary support. One cost guide’s own note is that the chemical is typically under 10% of the total, and the cut-out, replacement and rebuild dominate the cost. That matches what I see.

Access. Lifting floors, removing plaster and getting at a roof void adds labour, and so does working in a lived-in house.

Making good. Replastering, redecorating, carpentry and flooring after the treatment are often priced separately. Check whether they are in or out.

Drying time. Thick masonry walls dry slowly. Redecorating too soon can trap moisture, so a sensible contractor will not paint over a wall that has not dried out.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • An independent diagnosis. Many damp and timber firms offer a free survey. That is a sales visit, because the business model is the treatment. A paid report from someone who does not do the work removes the conflict.
  • The species named. The quote should say whether it is dry rot or wet rot, and on what evidence.
  • The moisture source dealt with, or clearly handed to someone who will fix it.
  • Extent shown on a plan or in a schedule, so you can see what is being cut out and replaced.
  • Pre-treated replacement timber to BS 8417, isolated from damp masonry.
  • A proportionate masonry treatment, not blanket irrigation by default.
  • A written guarantee and who stands behind it if the firm closes. The PCA describes insurance-backed guarantees for this. Read the actual terms; I could not confirm a standard length, and it varies.
  • Making good itemised separately from the treatment.

Insurance and your survey

Buildings insurance typically excludes wear and tear, mould, and dry and wet rot, because rot develops gradually. Rot that follows a sudden event, such as a burst pipe, may be a different matter; the wording of your own policy decides. If you think the rot is linked to an insured event, tell your insurer before work starts.

If you are buying, a Level 3 survey will typically rate active rot as a serious or urgent defect and recommend a specialist investigation before you commit, with written quotes. That is the right order: the diagnosis first, the quotes second, then a decision on the price. What other scary survey findings tend to cost is in survey defect costs.

Questions I get asked

How much does dry rot treatment cost? In 2026, expect roughly £500–£1,500 for a small patch at a window or skirting, £1,500–£5,000 for a contained outbreak in one room, and £5,000–£15,000 or more where it has spread into joists, wall plates or masonry. A specialist survey typically costs £150–£500 and is sometimes deducted from the treatment price.

How much does wet rot treatment cost? Usually far less. Roughly £200–£500 for a single sill or skirting, £500–£3,000 for a localised joist or roof timber, and £3,000–£10,000 or more where a whole room or several structural timbers are involved. Most of the cost is finding and fixing the leak and replacing timber, not chemicals.

Can dry rot be treated without chemicals? The Property Care Association says the primary control is removing the moisture source and letting the structure dry out, with biocides as a supporting measure rather than the cure. Decayed timber is cut out and replaced with pre-treated timber. The PCA says widespread masonry irrigation can rarely be justified.

Does buildings insurance cover dry rot? Usually not. Standard policies commonly exclude wear and tear, mould, and dry or wet rot, because it develops gradually. Cover may be possible where rot follows a sudden event such as a burst pipe, but check your own policy wording and ask your insurer before work starts.
