The best
tool belts
for survey work

Half a survey happens somewhere without a worktop to put anything down — up a loft ladder, in a crawl space, on a half-landing with a torch in one hand and a pad in the other. Whatever needs to be to hand at that moment has to be on the body, not back in the van. Here is what is worth wearing for that, from a modular budget pouch to a leather rig that will outlast the practice, and why most surveyors need far less belt than a carpenter does.

A tool bag does most of the carrying on a normal visit — it comes out of the van, sits by the front door, and gets dipped into between rooms. A tool belt is for the minutes it doesn’t work: climbing a loft ladder with both hands needed for the rungs, crouching through a crawl-space hatch, or working a half-landing where there is nowhere flat to set a moisture meter down between readings. Whatever has to be reachable in exactly those moments — a torch, a meter, a pad and pencil, a phone for photos — needs to be on the belt, not three rooms away in the bag.

That makes a survey belt a different brief to the tool bag covered elsewhere in this series, and a lighter one than most tool belts on the market are built for. The majority of tool belts sold on Amazon are carpenter’s rigs, designed to carry a full day’s framing nails, a hammer and a pouch of fixings across a building site. A surveyor’s load-out is a fraction of that — a damp meter, a tape, a multitool, a phone — which is why the products worth buying here range from a two-pouch modular set to a full leather framer’s belt that is genuinely more rig than most surveys need, with honest reasons given either way.

What separates a good belt from a bad one at this scale has less to do with pocket count and more to do with whether it stays comfortable loaded for a whole visit, and whether a moisture meter actually stays in its pouch going down a loft ladder rather than landing on the landing below. How I judge a tool belt

Comfort loaded, not comfort in the packaging. A thin webbing strap feels fine for the thirty seconds it takes to try it on. Whether it is still comfortable after four hours and a loft ladder is a different question, and padding across the hips is what actually answers it.

Pouches that hold on to what goes in them. A moisture meter or a phone dropped through a loft hatch is not a small inconvenience — deep pouches with a snug fit beat wide, shallow ones every time, whatever the pocket count on the listing says.

Bulk that survives a hatch and a crawl space, not just a building site. A seven-bag framer’s rig is wide enough to catch on a 550mm loft hatch in a way a two-pouch belt never will. For survey work, less belt is often the better belt.

Quick on, quick off. A belt that has to be buckled and adjusted fresh every time it goes on gets left in the van out of sheer friction. A quick-release buckle or a modular clip system gets worn instead.

Material suited to a wet crawl space, not just a dry site. Leather looks and lasts the part but takes on damp and needs the occasional conditioning; synthetic dries out faster after a genuinely wet sub-floor and shrugs off a quick wipe-down.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Pocket count is a carpenter’s spec, not a surveyor’s. A framer’s belt with 27 pockets is built to sort nails by length and keep fixings separated from tools. A survey load-out is a handful of items — a meter, a tape, a pad, a phone — so two or three well-placed pouches beat a high pocket count every time. More pockets than kit just means more bulk to get through a hatch.

Leather earns its price over years, not on day one. A leather rig like the Occidental holds its shape indefinitely and looks the part decades in, but it needs breaking in, takes on damp in a wet crawl space, and costs several times what a synthetic belt does. Synthetic dries faster, needs no conditioning, and is the more practical choice for anyone not planning to wear the same belt for the next twenty years.

Suspenders only pay for themselves once a belt is genuinely loaded. Spreading weight from the hips to the shoulders is a real comfort gain on a heavily loaded framer’s rig. On a two-pouch survey belt carrying a meter and a tape, they are just extra bulk with nothing to justify it.

Modular, clip-on pouches suit a job where the load changes visit to visit. A carpenter wears the same rig all day, every day, so a fixed belt makes sense. A surveyor’s kit changes with the job — a damp-only revisit needs far less than a full building survey — and a system like ToughBuilt’s ClipTech lets the belt match that without buying multiple rigs.

A premium price tag is solving a carpenter’s problem, not a surveyor’s. The Occidental and similar leather rigs are built to survive a framing crew’s daily abuse for a decade. That is a genuine engineering achievement, but it is not the problem most survey kit carrying actually has — which is why the top pick here costs a fraction of it.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a tool belt if I already carry a tool bag?

For most of a visit, no — the tool bag does the carrying and a belt just adds bulk. Where a belt earns its place is the minutes a bag can’t follow: up a loft ladder, through a crawl-space hatch, or anywhere both hands are needed for something other than holding kit. A small, modular belt for those moments alongside a bag for everything else is a more practical combination than trying to make one do both jobs.

What should actually go in a surveyor’s tool belt?

Whatever needs to be reachable without setting anything down: a moisture meter, a tape measure, a pad and pencil, a phone for photos, and perhaps a small multitool. That is two or three pouches’ worth of kit, which is exactly why the modular, smaller-capacity belts in this guide suit survey work better than a full carpenter’s rig.

Leather or synthetic — which actually lasts longer on site?

Leather lasts longer in absolute terms if it is looked after, but it needs conditioning and doesn’t take kindly to a genuinely wet crawl space or a soaking on an external inspection. Synthetic pouches dry out faster, wipe clean, and need no maintenance, which for a job that regularly means damp sub-floors and standing water makes it the more practical material even before the price difference is considered.

Is a full carpenter’s rig like the Occidental worth it for survey work?

Only for someone who wants one belt bought once and never replaced, and is happy to carry more capacity and bulk than the job strictly needs. For most survey work, a modular two- or three-pouch belt does everything a framer’s seven-bag rig does for the kit actually carried, at a fraction of the price and without the bulk fighting a tight loft hatch.

A tool belt earns its keep in the moments a bag can’t reach — which for most surveys is a small, specific list of moments rather than a whole working day. Getting that list right on a building survey or a retrofit assessment matters more than how many pockets are on the belt.
