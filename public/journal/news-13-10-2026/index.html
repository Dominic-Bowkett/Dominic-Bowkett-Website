Surveyor’s notes
13 October 2026:
£8.17 a year

Published 13 October 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

The government has put a price on Awaab’s Law for private landlords, and it is smaller than the panic suggests. Rent repayment orders turn out to be almost entirely a licensing story, and buy-to-let yields have climbed to their highest in fifteen years while some London landlords who tried to sell have quietly changed their minds. I have also added a register reminder, because the South East date is closer than it feels.

The government says Awaab’s Law costs a private landlord £8.17 a year, and that is the wrong number to worry about

Following the consultation I covered on Friday, the numbers are out. The MHCLG impact assessment published with the consultation, which runs to 18 December, puts the net cost to landlords at £42.2 million a year, or £8.17 per property (range £5.64 to £10.69). LandlordZONE reports that headline. The document itself says the repairs in scope are ones landlords are already responsible for, so the sum is the cost of doing them faster and of communicating with tenants, not the cost of the repairs.

The more interesting passage is on landlords leaving. The assessment accepts they might, then argues that the landlords most affected “are those offering the poorest-quality accommodation” and that professional landlords could step into the gap. Property118 leads on the exit risk; the document is more relaxed about it. It also says it does not expect landlords to pass on 100 per cent of the cost to tenants.

My read: £8.17 is an average across millions of rented homes, most of which will never generate a hazard complaint in a given year. The landlord who gets a mould complaint in a 1930s semi with solid walls and a bathroom with no real ventilation will not experience £8.17. They will experience a clock, a duty to write to the tenant, and a bill for the real cause. The average hides that tail, and the document says the benefits to landlords, such as fewer voids, could not be monetised. So the right preparation is not budgeting £8 a door, it is knowing which of your properties would fail a damp inspection tomorrow. If you have a recurring mould property, commission a proper diagnosis now, keep the report, and fix the cause rather than the stain. Respond to the consultation if you have more than a handful of lets; the timescales are the part still open.

Nine in ten rent repayment orders are licensing cases, and the licence is the cheapest protection you can buy

LandlordZONE reports LetCompliance’s analysis of government figures: 237 rent repayment order decisions were published for 2025, up from 194 in 2024, and 93 per cent involved licensing offences. Tenants won 72 per cent of applications, the median repayment was £6,141, and 65 per cent of decisions came from London boroughs, where the median order was £8,908 against £2,819 elsewhere. The same article says prosecutions for Housing Act 2004 offences have fallen 84 per cent since 2017, from 1,067 to 166. LetCompliance’s Erdem Volkan says the typical order rests on “a licence the property needed and didn’t have”.

The picture is that tenants are doing enforcement work that councils are not. That matters more under the Renters’ Rights Act: the article says that since 1 May the maximum order has doubled to two years’ rent, the application window has stretched to two years, and a superior landlord can be named. I have not checked those provisions against the Act itself, so read them as reported. Note too that these are published decisions, not every claim made, and London skews the averages heavily.

For a landlord in Sussex or Kent the lesson is dull and valuable. Check whether each property needs a mandatory HMO or a selective licence from the district council, diarise the expiry date, and keep the paperwork where you can find it in a panic. A property that needed a licence and had none is the easy case for a tenant to win.

Landlords are not leaving as fast as the headlines said, and yields are the reason

Three data points this week point the same way. Paragon Bank’s figures, via LandlordZONE and Property118, put average gross buy-to-let yields at 7.22 per cent in the third quarter, up from 7.0 per cent in the second, led by the East Midlands at 8.10 per cent and HMOs at 8.80 per cent, with Greater London the lowest region at under 6 per cent. The Negotiator reports Chestertons finding that about one in four London landlords who listed a property for sale after the Act came into force have now re-let it. And Property118 carries Savills’ Lucian Cook saying he expects a pre-Budget sales spike “to be less pronounced this time around”, with vacant possession a hurdle.

I would not over-read any of it. Gross yield ignores finance costs, voids and repairs, and the Savills article says landlord profits fell 11.2 per cent in 2024/25. Chestertons is one agent’s book, and Propertymark’s September data has rents falling in nine of eleven regions, which sits oddly beside a story of landlords holding on for rising rents. I have not seen a South East yield figure in any of these sources, so I will not guess one. My bet is that the exit is real but staggered, concentrated among owners whose yield was thin to begin with. If you own a low-yielding flat with an expensive problem hanging over it, a survey before you decide to hold or sell is worth more than any headline.

The landlord register reaches the South East on 15 March, and registering early has a catch

This is older news, but it is still worth your attention. Propertymark’s schedule, published on 9 September, opens the register in the West Midlands on 15 December 2026, with the South East opening on 15 March 2027 and closing on 14 June 2027. The NRLA’s guidance confirms the £65 per property annual fee, civil penalties of up to £7,000 and up to £40,000 for the worse cases, and that you must provide gas, electrical and EPC documents. It also says the renewal date for a portfolio is the date the first property was registered, with later properties paying a prorated first year. Property118’s piece on 12 October draws the sensible conclusion: do not register early just because the system allows it, decide when you want the annual admin to land.

The EPC upload is where my work comes in. If an EPC has lapsed, or never existed, you want it in hand before the window opens, not on the last day. Order it in the winter, when assessors are quieter.

What I’d actually do

  1. Landlords with older or damp-prone stock: pick the two or three properties that would struggle under a repair clock and get a proper damp diagnosis, not a paint-over. Respond to the Awaab’s Law consultation before 18 December.
  2. Every landlord: list each property, whether it needs a licence, and the expiry date. Chase any that are blank this week.
  3. South East landlords: diarise 15 March to 14 June 2027 for the register and choose your first registration date deliberately.
  4. Check your EPCs now: find any that are missing or old so they are ready to upload.
  5. Thinking of selling a low-yield let: get the survey first, so you know what a buyer’s surveyor will find.
