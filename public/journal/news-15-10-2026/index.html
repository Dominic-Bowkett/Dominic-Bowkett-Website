Surveyor’s notes
15 October 2026:
Hove licensing paused

Brighton & Hove has paused a licensing scheme five days before it was due to start, Scotland’s consultation on EPC C for rented homes has drawn more opposition than support, and two sets of rent figures say the lettings market is firm but not frantic.

Published 15 October 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Brighton & Hove has paused a licensing scheme five days before it was due to start, Scotland’s consultation on EPC C for rented homes has drawn more opposition than support, and two sets of rent figures say the lettings market is firm but not frantic. Not a big news day, but two of these stories are close to home for a landlord in Sussex.

Hove’s new licensing scheme was five days from launch, and a legal challenge has stopped it

Property118 reports that Brighton & Hove City Council has paused selective licensing for the Central Hove and Regency wards, which was due to start on 20 October. It quotes housing cabinet member Gill Williams: “We have decided to pause the introduction of the scheme planned for 20 October while we review the next steps.” The council’s own selective licensing page says the expansion has been “paused pending the review of a legal challenge”. Neither source says who brought the challenge or on what grounds, so I will not guess. The existing schemes in Kemptown, Moulsecoomb and Bevendean, Queens Park and Whitehawk and Marina are unaffected and run to 1 September 2029. Property118 notes this follows Telford & Wrekin revoking an additional HMO licensing scheme on 11 September after challenges to its consultation and charges, and Great Yarmouth losing its blanket licence conditions.

My read: councils are discovering that a licensing scheme is only as strong as its consultation file and its fee justification. A pause is not a cancellation, and the council says it is reviewing next steps, so the scheme may well return in some form. If you let in Central Hove or Regency, do not treat this as permission to relax. Keep your gas, electrical and fire paperwork current and your EPC valid, because those are what a licence application asks for. Be wary of anyone who tells you the licence has been cancelled; the sources say paused.

Scotland’s EPC C consultation shows how rented-home standards get diluted, and England should take notes

The Scottish Government’s analysis of responses was published on 6 October and the landlord press picked it up this week, so treat it as still worth your attention rather than breaking news. Of 316 responses, 35 per cent supported the proposed Heat Retention Rating band C requirement, though support among organisations was higher at 57 per cent. Only 24 per cent backed the £10,000 cost cap, with many asking for £5,000 instead. 83 per cent agreed landlords need government help, and grants were preferred to loans. The Scottish Association of Landlords wants at least two years between the EPC reform in April 2028 and any standard coming into force.

The detail that matters for my work is the technical objections. Respondents flagged older stone-built homes, listed buildings and tenements as poor candidates, and warned that upgrades can leave inadequate ventilation and cause damp and mould. 68 per cent supported a property-specific technical suitability assessment as a way to evidence what can sensibly be done. I have been saying for years that a one-size EPC target breaks down on traditional buildings, and this is the same argument arriving by consultation. Property118’s write-up adds the supply worry, that landlords may exit or pass costs on to tenants.

England is on a different track and, as a CPS Homes blog published yesterday says of the announced rules, “none of it is yet legislated for”. My bet is that the exemption and cost-cap fights will look similar here, so prepare without panicking. For a pre-1930 or listed rental, a retrofit assessment now tells you which measures help and which risk trapping moisture, before a contractor sells you the wrong one.

Two rent indices, two different pictures: record highs, but a market that is balancing

Both were published today. Property118 carries Rightmove’s figures: average advertised rent of £1,418 a month, up 2.4 per cent on the year, with outside London above £1,400 for the first time, London at £2,825, available homes down 2 per cent and tenant demand also down 2 per cent. It also quotes a two-year fixed buy-to-let rate of 5.34 per cent. Landlord Today reports HomeLet’s achieved-rent index at £1,387, up 3.3 per cent on the year, with tenants spending an average 32.1 per cent of income on rent. The two measure different things, asking and achieved, which is why the numbers do not match.

The practitioner’s read is that rents are still rising but more slowly than the headlines imply, and supply and demand are falling together rather than one outrunning the other. That fits what I wrote on Tuesday about landlords leaving gradually rather than all at once. With borrowing at those rates a landlord has little room to absorb a surprise repair or compliance bill. Neither source gives a South East figure, so I will not offer one. And tenants stretched to nearly a third of income are quicker to notice a cold, damp flat, and quicker to complain about it.

What I’d actually do

  1. Landlords in Central Hove or Regency: carry on assembling licence paperwork, and watch the council’s selective licensing page for the next step.
  2. Any landlord with traditional or listed stock: have an assessor say which EPC measures suit the building, and keep the report. It is the evidence any future exemption will rest on.
  3. Landlords with a thin margin: price a damp or EPC problem before it arrives, not after the complaint.
  4. Check your EPC dates: a valid certificate is needed for licensing, the coming register and any sale.
