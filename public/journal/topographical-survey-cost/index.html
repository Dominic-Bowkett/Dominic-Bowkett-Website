Topographical
survey cost
in 2026

A topographical survey typically costs £400–£1,500 for a house plot in 2026 — the site-levels survey an architect needs before drawing up planning or extension plans. Larger or more complex sites cost significantly more, and it’s a different job entirely from a measured survey of the building itself.

Published 30 Aug 2026 Reading time · 9 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A topographical survey typically costs £400–£1,500 for a house plot in 2026, depending on the size of the site and how much detail your architect needs. A small, level garden being measured for a straightforward extension sits at the bottom of that range; a larger or sloping plot, or one with trees, retaining walls and awkward boundaries to record, sits nearer the top. A larger parcel of land, or a small development site, moves into four figures fairly quickly.

What the fee buys is a scaled, accurate record of the land as it stands — ground levels, boundaries, trees, drainage, and any structures — tied into the Ordnance Survey grid, usually delivered as a CAD drawing an architect can design directly on top of. It's a different question from what the building itself costs to have measured, which I've covered separately, and a different question again from whether the building is structurally sound, which is what I actually get asked to assess.

I'm a building surveyor, not a land surveyor — I don't carry out topographical surveys myself, so I've no stake in whether you commission one, or which firm you use. What follows is 2026 pricing cross-checked against current UK land-surveying practices, what genuinely drives the cost, and a straight answer on when a topo survey earns its keep versus when it's money you didn't need to spend.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 30 August 2026 against current 2026 pricing from UK topographical and land-surveying practices, cross-referenced against each other. Most firms quote a fixed fee once they know the site area and the detail required, though some still price by the day. As with the rest of this series, London and the South East — Dom's patch included — commonly trend above the rest of the UK for a comparable site.

Where a firm charges by the day rather than a fixed fee, expect roughly £350–£1,200 for a day surveying in the field, plus £350–£650 for a day converting that data into CAD drawings back in the office — a small plot is often a single day of each. Quotes are usually given plus VAT, so check which basis you're being quoted on before comparing two firms against each other.

What drives the price

Site size and boundary length. More ground to walk and more boundary to record means more time on site, though the per-square-metre rate typically falls as the area increases.

Feature density. A clear, mown garden surveys quickly. Overgrown boundaries, dense planting, parked vehicles, retaining structures and multiple outbuildings all slow the walkover down and add detail to record.

Level of detail required. A basic site plan with spot heights costs less than a full contour survey at, say, 0.5m intervals with cross-sections through the site — the latter is usually what's needed where drainage falls, basements or retaining walls are part of the design.

Extras bundled in. A tree survey to BS 5837 standard, or underground utility detection, are sometimes offered alongside a topo survey by the same firm. Useful if you need them, but confirm whether they're included or quoted as an add-on before assuming the headline figure covers everything.

Access and travel. A remote site, or one a local surveyor has to travel some distance to reach, typically costs more than an equivalent plot on their doorstep.

Turnaround. An expedited drawing set against a looming planning deadline commonly carries a premium over standard delivery, usually quoted as 5–10 working days.

Do you actually need one?

Architects generally can't start accurate planning or proposed drawings without knowing the existing ground levels first, and on anything other than a flat, straightforward garden, guessing those levels is how designs go wrong on site. Many local planning authority validation checklists explicitly ask for existing and proposed site levels on applications where they're relevant — and will come back requesting further information, delaying a decision, if a survey wasn't done and the drawings submitted don't show them credibly.

A topographical survey earns its keep in particular where: the plot slopes, even gently; the project includes a basement or lower-ground floor; drainage needs to be designed to fall correctly to a connection point; the site has retaining walls, level changes or a boundary close to trees; or it's a new-build or self-build plot being designed from scratch rather than extended off an existing structure. On a genuinely flat back garden for a simple single-storey rear extension, some architects will measure levels themselves with a spirit level and tape and skip a formal survey — ask directly whether that's realistic for your project rather than assuming either way.

Permitted development work generally doesn't require a topo survey to be submitted to the council, since there's no planning application to validate. That doesn't mean one isn't useful: an architect designing without accurate levels on a sloping PD scheme is still working from guesswork, and a design error caught on site during construction is a far more expensive fix than the survey would have been.

The mix-up with a measured building survey

This is the professional-judgement point worth being clear on, because the word “survey” covers both, and I've seen the confusion cost people money. A topographical survey maps the land — levels, boundaries, drainage, trees, anything outside the building. A measured building survey, which I've written about separately, maps the building itself — floor plans, elevations, room layout. Some firms offer both as a single combined site visit, which is efficient if you genuinely need both, but the two are still priced and delivered as separate outputs, and plenty of projects only need one of them.

An extension onto an existing house on a flat plot might only need a measured survey of the building, with the garden levels judged well enough by eye. A basement dig, a new dwelling on a sloping plot, or a scheme where the garden's fall matters to the design needs both a topo survey of the land and, if the existing building is being altered, a measured survey of it too. Ask explicitly which one, or both, your architect actually needs before agreeing a scope with a surveying firm — “we'll do a full survey” is not a specific enough answer to know what you're paying for.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Fixed fee or day rate stated clearly — and, if day rate, an estimate of how many field and office days your site will realistically take.
  • Contour interval and detail level specified — spot heights only, or a full contour survey at a stated interval; confirm it matches what your architect actually asked for.
  • File format your architect can open — DWG, not just a PDF, unless a PDF is genuinely all you need.
  • OS grid and Ordnance Datum tie-in confirmed — particularly if the survey will be cross-referenced against flood mapping or a neighbouring site.
  • Tree and utility surveys itemised separately if you need them, rather than assumed as part of the headline price.
  • Turnaround stated against your planning deadline, and what an expedited service would cost if your timeline tightens.

Questions I get asked

How much does a topographical survey cost in 2026? A small garden or single house plot typically costs £400–£900. A typical residential site being surveyed for an extension or new dwelling runs £800–£1,500. Larger plots or small development sites cost £1,500–£3,000, and large or complex sites can run £3,000–£6,000 or more.

Do I need a topographical survey for a home extension or loft conversion? Usually yes if the site slopes, involves a basement or lower-ground floor, needs a drainage fall calculated, or sits near retaining walls, trees or awkward boundaries. A simple single-storey rear extension on a level plot can sometimes get away without one, but check with your architect first.

What's the difference between a topographical survey and a measured building survey? A topographical survey records the land: levels, contours, boundaries, trees and drainage. A measured building survey records the building: floor plans, elevations and room layout. Both are priced and commissioned separately, and a project may need one or both.

Can I skip a topographical survey and save the cost? On a small, flat, straightforward plot, some architects will hand-measure levels and skip a formal survey. On anything sloping, tight against a boundary, involving a basement, or where drainage needs to fall correctly, skipping it risks a design that doesn't work on site or a planning refusal for missing information — usually costing more than the survey would have.

A topographical survey tells your architect what the land is doing; it doesn't tell you whether the existing building is sound enough to extend from, or whether that Victorian rear wall you're building off has already moved.
