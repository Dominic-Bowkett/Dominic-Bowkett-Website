Most people buy a laser level to hang a picture straight. On a survey, the same tool finds out why a door in a Victorian terrace won’t stay shut. Set the laser on its little tripod in the middle of the room, let it self-level, and there’s a dead-level green line running around the walls at, say, a metre. Then you walk the room with a tape and measure from the line down to the floor. If the reading is 1,000 mm at the door and 1,032 mm at the bay window, the floor falls 32 mm across the room — and now you have a number, not a feeling. Same trick sideways for an out-of-plumb wall or a dropped lintel over a widened opening.

That’s the job this tool does on a survey: it converts “the floor feels a bit off” into millimetres you can write down, compare with the crack pattern, and monitor. It gets used on nearly every building survey I do, and on plenty of damp jobs too — a sloping solid floor tells you where the water goes.

What separates the good ones from the junk isn’t the number of lines. It’s whether the beam is visible in a bright south-facing room, whether the self-levelling is honest (it must warn you when it can’t level, not quietly draw a wrong line), and whether the claimed accuracy holds up. The good news is that genuinely useful accuracy — around ±0.3 mm per metre — is now available for well under £100. The bad news is that the market is full of unbranded lasers with invented specifications. Here’s what I’d actually buy.

How I judge a laser level

Beam colour and visibility. Green, always, for daytime work. A green beam sits close to the peak sensitivity of the human eye and reads roughly two to four times brighter than a red beam of the same power. In a bright room with the curtains open — which is every occupied house I survey — a red line simply disappears past four or five metres. Red is fine in a dim cellar or a loft; it is not fine across a sunlit living room.

Honest self-levelling. Every laser here has a pendulum that self-levels within about ±4°. What matters is the out-of-level warning: when the unit is tilted beyond the pendulum’s range, it must flash or blink rather than project a plausible-looking wrong line. Every recommendation below does this. Some cheap units don’t, and a wrong line delivered confidently is worse than no line at all.

Accuracy you can trust. The spec to read is millimetres per metre. ±0.3 mm/m means the line could be 3 mm out over a 10 m throw — well inside what matters when the floor you’re measuring falls 30 mm. Claims better than ±0.2 mm/m on a £40 laser should be treated as fiction.

Power and mounting. AA batteries are a feature, not a compromise — spares live in the van door. Platform batteries (DeWalt 12V, Makita CXT) make sense if you already own the chargers. A decent magnetic or rotating mount matters more than people think; you spend more time positioning a laser than reading it.

Survivability. A pendulum lock for transport, an IP54 or better rating, and a body that shrugs off a drop from a window board. A laser that goes out of calibration silently is a liability in this trade.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Green beam: matters. It’s the single biggest usability difference in this category and worth paying for. The trade-offs — slightly higher price, hungrier on batteries — are trivial next to being able to see the line.

Number of 360° planes: matters less than the marketing says. Three planes are lovely for fitting a kitchen. For diagnosis you use one level line at a time, and a plain cross-line with a rotating mount covers ninety per cent of it. Don’t pay for planes you won’t switch on.

Pulse mode and receivers: only if you work outside. Pulse mode lets a handheld detector find the beam in sunlight at long range — essential for groundworks and fencing, irrelevant inside a house. Most lasers here include it anyway; just don’t let a “50 m range” figure sway you when the visible indoor range is what you’ll actually use.

Bluetooth and apps: don’t matter. Nothing that happens on a survey requires a laser to talk to a phone. Salesmen push connectivity because it’s the only thing left to differentiate mature products. Spend the money on a decent tripod instead — a £25 tripod improves every laser more than any app does.

Calibration checks: matter more than the purchase. Whatever you buy, check it twice a year: stand it 5 m from a wall, mark the line, spin the unit 180°, and compare. Then reverse the test at the other end of the room. Ten minutes, and it’s the difference between a measurement and a guess. A laser that’s been dropped gets checked the same day.

Questions I get asked

Green vs red laser level — is green really worth it?

Yes, for anything in daylight. The human eye is most sensitive around the green part of the spectrum, so a green laser line appears roughly two to four times brighter than a red one of the same output power. In practice that means a green line stays readable across a sunlit room where a red line disappears beyond four or five metres. Red still has a place — cellars, lofts, evening work — and red units are cheaper and easier on batteries. But if you buy one laser to do everything, buy green.

How do surveyors check if a floor is level?

With exactly the method in the introduction: a self-levelling laser projects a dead-level datum line around the room, and you measure from that line down to the floor at a grid of points with a tape or rule. The differences between readings map the floor’s slope in millimetres — direction, magnitude, whether it’s a uniform tilt or a local dip. Every Victorian floor slopes; that alone is not a defect. The diagnostic question is whether the movement is historic and stable or recent and progressive, which is where the laser evidence gets read alongside crack patterns, decoration age and, where needed, monitoring over months. A digital level or the old marble across the boards gives you a feel; the laser gives you numbers you can compare next year.

How does a self-levelling laser level work?

Inside the housing the laser diodes hang on a pendulum, usually damped by magnets so it settles in a couple of seconds. Set the tool anywhere within about ±4° of level and gravity does the rest: the pendulum swings true and the projected line is level regardless of the wonky window board you’ve stood it on. Tip it beyond that range and a decent unit flashes or blinks to warn you rather than drawing a wrong line. There’s also a transport lock that clamps the pendulum — use it, because a pendulum bouncing loose in a van is how lasers quietly lose their calibration. Most units let you lock the pendulum and project a deliberately tilted line for staircases.

What does laser level accuracy in mm per metre actually mean?

It’s the maximum error the line can accumulate per metre of distance from the tool. A ±0.3 mm/m laser could be 3 mm out at 10 m; a ±0.6 mm/m consumer unit could be 6 mm out at the same distance. Whether that matters depends entirely on the job. Diagnosing settlement, where floors are out by 20–60 mm, any of these tools is comfortably accurate enough. Setting out tiling or a worktop across a long run, the tighter spec starts to earn its money. What no spec sheet fixes is a laser that’s been knocked out of calibration — hence the twice-yearly check above.

A laser line tells you how much a floor has moved; it doesn’t tell you why, or whether it’s finished moving. If a sloping floor, cracked plaster or a sticking door is worrying you — especially in an older building — a proper inspection puts the measurements in context.
