Roof pitch comes up more often in this job than most non-surveyors would guess. RdSAP needs it to classify the roof and estimate heat loss through a loft or room-in-roof; a Level 3 building survey often needs it recorded honestly rather than eyeballed, particularly when a loft conversion or dormer is being weighed up and headroom against the slope becomes the whole question. "Looks like about 35 degrees" doesn’t hold up in a written report, and lining a spirit level up against a rafter by eye is slower than it needs to be.

Staircases are the other place a digital angle finder earns its keep. Approved Document K sets a maximum pitch for a private stair, and an existing staircase that’s steeper than that is worth flagging with a number attached rather than a description like "unusually steep." The same tool checks a proposed loft stair against the same limit before a client commits to a conversion, and settles an argument about whether a going-and-rise combination on an older property is actually non-standard or just feels that way underfoot.

What follows is what actually matters when buying a digital angle finder for this kind of work — not the woodworking-bench precision most of these are marketed around — and seven worth having in the bag.

How I judge a digital angle finder

Arm length long enough to bridge the surface being checked. A short gauge balanced on one rafter, or perched on a worn stair nosing, reads the local dip or wear rather than the true pitch. A blade of 200mm or more spans that unevenness and gives a number worth writing down.

Absolute and relative (zero) modes, not just one. Absolute mode records the actual pitch for an RdSAP entry or a report photo; relative or zero mode is what actually answers "is this baluster plumb" or "does this going match the others" without doing the subtraction by hand.

Magnetic base only where the substrate is actually ferrous. A magnetic gauge is genuinely quick against a steel purlin or an RSJ, but useless — worse than useless, since it won’t hold — against timber rafters, staircase stringers or masonry, which cover most domestic pitch checks. A two-arm protractor works against anything by bridging both surfaces mechanically rather than relying on a magnet.

Resolution that matches the decision, not the spec sheet. Site work doesn’t need 0.01° precision — the rafter itself isn’t flat to that tolerance, so a laboratory-grade sensor number rarely survives contact with real timber. Anything reading to 0.5° or better is genuinely enough to record a pitch, band it for RdSAP, or check it against a Building Regs limit.

A display that’s readable in a loft with a head torch, not a showroom. A backlit LCD, decent contrast and a lock or hold button that keeps a reading steady while it’s photographed matter more day to day than an accuracy claim that outstrips what a rafter can actually deliver.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

0.01° resolution on the spec sheet is a number, not a benefit. A rafter, stringer or purlin isn’t flat to a hundredth of a degree, so a sensor claiming lab-grade precision is reading the local imperfection in the timber as often as the true pitch. Anything at 0.1–0.5° is genuinely enough for every decision a roof pitch or stair check actually involves.

A magnetic base is a genuine time-saver — on the right surface. Against steelwork it beats a two-arm protractor for speed every time. Against timber, render or masonry it’s dead weight, since there’s nothing for the magnet to grip. Buy for the substrate that’s actually being checked most often, not the substrate on the packaging photo.

Bluetooth and app connectivity are a woodworking-bench feature, not a site one. A handful of these gauges pair with a phone app for logging a series of angles — genuinely useful on a production line setting up a saw, largely pointless for a one-off pitch reading that’s going straight into a report by hand or camera anyway.

Mitre and compound-angle calculation is worth paying for only if valleys and hips come up often. Bosch’s MF models and similar convert a measured slope straight into a mitre setting, which is a real time-saver on a job that involves cutting to that angle. For simply recording what a pitch is, it’s an unused feature on an otherwise more expensive tool.

A lock or hold button matters more than an extra decimal place. Being able to freeze a reading on the display, rather than having it drift as the tool’s repositioned for a photo, is what actually makes a number trustworthy enough to go in a report.

Questions I get asked

How accurate does a digital angle finder need to be for recording roof pitch? Far less precise than most of these tools claim. Roof pitch for RdSAP or a survey report is generally recorded to the nearest degree rather than a fraction of one, so a finder reading to 0.5° is comfortably accurate enough — the limiting factor is usually how flat the rafter or purlin being measured against actually is, not the tool’s resolution.

What’s the maximum pitch allowed for a private staircase under Building Regulations? Approved Document K sets 42° as the steepest pitch permitted for a private stair, measured as the angle between the pitch line and the horizontal. An existing staircase measured steeper than that on an older property isn’t automatically a defect requiring work — many predate current regulations and are assessed differently — but it’s worth recording precisely rather than describing as "steep," particularly where a loft conversion is proposing a new flight.

Is it better to measure roof pitch from inside the loft or from outside on the roof itself? Inside, almost always. Placing a two-arm finder directly against a rafter or purlin gives a clean read off the structural timber, while measuring the roof covering risks reading the profile of the tiles or slates rather than the true pitch, and getting onto an occupied roof slope safely is rarely worth the risk for a figure that’s just as reliable from the loft.

Do I need a magnetic angle gauge, a two-arm protractor, or both? For most domestic roof and stair work, a two-arm protractor is the more versatile default — it works against timber, masonry or steel alike by bridging both surfaces mechanically. A magnetic gauge earns its place as a second tool specifically for steelwork, where it’s genuinely faster, but it’s the wrong first purchase if most of what’s being checked is timber rafters and stair stringers.
