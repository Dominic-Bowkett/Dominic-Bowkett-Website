Environmental search
cost in 2026

A standard environmental search, the kind your conveyancer bundles into the search pack without being asked, typically costs £35–£75 in 2026 — or £90–£165 for an enhanced, insurance-backed version. It's one of the cheapest items on a conveyancing bill, and one of the most skimmed-over.

Published 16 Sep 2026 Reading time · 8 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

An environmental search is a desktop database check, not a site visit. A search provider — almost always Groundsure or Landmark, the two firms that dominate this market — cross-references the property's location against national datasets on contaminated land, flood risk, ground stability and radon, and returns a report within a day or two. The standard version most solicitors order as a matter of course costs £35–£75 in 2026. An enhanced version with more detailed data layers and an insurance-backed guarantee, such as Landmark's Envirosearch or RiskView Residential products, runs £90–£165. Add a coal mining search at £30–£60 if the property sits in a coalfield area, and the whole standard search pack — local authority, water and drainage, environmental, plus a chancel repair indemnity — usually lands at £250–£450 in total.

But the number isn't really the point. This is one of the few items in a conveyancing quote that most buyers never actually read — it arrives as a PDF alongside a dozen other search results, gets a cursory glance for anything in red, and goes in a folder. That's a shame, because what it screens for and what it doesn't is worth understanding properly, and there's a contaminated-land liability angle buried in the small print that genuinely changes who's on the hook if something's wrong with the land later.

I'm a building surveyor, not an environmental consultant, and I don't commission or interpret these searches myself — that's your conveyancing solicitor's job, done as a matter of routine on every purchase. What follows is written because I survey the buildings sitting on top of this land constantly, buyers ask me about it more than you'd expect, and a search result that flags something is exactly the kind of thing worth understanding before you either panic or ignore it. Figures below were checked on 16 September 2026 against Groundsure, Landmark and several conveyancing and property-search comparison sites' published 2026 pricing, cross-referenced against each other.

What you pay depends on which tier of report your solicitor orders, and whether the property sits somewhere that needs an add-on search as well.

The local authority element of that pack varies the most by a wide margin — a straightforward LLC1 official certificate alone can be as little as £15, but the full LLC1 plus CON29 enquiry package commonly runs anywhere from around £75 to over £300 depending on the council, since each authority sets and charges its own fee. The environmental search is, by comparison, the one genuinely consistent, low-cost item in the pack.

What drives the price

Standard versus enhanced. The cheaper tier is a straightforward automated database match against the address. The more expensive tier adds specialist interpretation of borderline results and, critically, an insurance-backed guarantee — if the report says "no risk" and that turns out to be wrong, the guarantee is what pays out, not you.

Provider and product name. Groundsure and Landmark each sell several tiers under different product names — Groundsure's core report against Landmark's Homecheck, Envirosearch and RiskView ranges — and your solicitor's own supplier agreement usually decides which one lands on your bill, not the transaction's complexity.

Whether a mining search is needed. This isn't optional guesswork; it's added automatically where the postcode falls within a recognised coalfield — large parts of the Midlands, the North East, Yorkshire, South Wales, the North West, Scotland's central belt, and a pocket of east Kent. Outside those areas it's usually skipped entirely, which is why two otherwise similar quotes can differ by £30–£60.

Bundling. Some conveyancing firms quote the environmental search as a genuinely separate disbursement; others fold it into a single "searches" line alongside the local authority and water searches, which makes it hard to see what you're actually paying for each element unless you ask for an itemised breakdown.

What it actually checks — and what it can't tell you

The report screens four main risk categories: contaminated land, drawing on historic land-use records, landfill sites and industrial mapping going back well over a century; flood risk, using the same national river, coastal, surface water and groundwater flood data that's freely available on the Environment Agency's own site; ground stability, covering natural hazards like shrink-swell clay, sinkholes and historic mining as well as landslip risk; and radon, a naturally occurring radioactive gas whose risk is mapped by postcode area rather than measured at the property.

What it can't do is tell you what's actually true of your specific plot. It's a database match against a wide search radius, not a site investigation. A flag for "historic industrial use within 250m" might mean a Victorian tannery sat two doors down, or it might mean a long-demolished workshop three streets away that never touched your garden — the report often can't distinguish the two without a human reading the underlying data properly. The radon figure is a postcode-level statistical estimate, not a reading taken in your actual house; two identical semis a street apart can have genuinely different real-world levels depending on the ground beneath each one and how the property's ventilated. Where a flag looks close or specific enough to matter, that's the point at which a proper Phase 1 Desk Study from an independent environmental consultant earns its considerably higher fee — typically £600–£1,500+ for a residential-scale site, reviewing historical maps and site-specific records rather than screening by database, and running past £3,000 for a larger or more complex site. Most buyers never need to go anywhere near that figure; it's only worth it where the initial flag is genuinely close to the property, not a routine follow-up to every report that comes back with anything at all in it.

Why it's worth reading properly, not just filing

There's no law that says you must have an environmental search. It's a mortgage lender's standard condition on most residential loans, not a statutory requirement, which is exactly why some cash buyers skip it to save a modest fee. That's worth thinking twice about, because of how contaminated land liability actually works. Part 2A of the Environmental Protection Act 1990 sets up a strict, retrospective liability regime: if land is formally identified as statutorily contaminated, the local authority looks first for the "Class A" person who caused or knowingly permitted the contamination, and failing that, falls back on the "Class B" person — whoever currently owns or occupies the land — regardless of whether they had anything to do with causing the problem.

The detail that matters for a buyer specifically is what the statutory guidance calls the "sold with information" test. Buying a property with information about a contamination risk in hand, and doing nothing about it — no price adjustment, no further investigation, no remediation condition — can weaken your position as an "innocent" purchaser if the contamination is later confirmed, compared with someone who genuinely had no way of knowing. In practice this rarely ends in a Class A designation for an ordinary homebuyer, enforcement action under Part 2A is uncommon, and most flagged reports turn out to be nothing on closer reading. But it's precisely why the search is worth reading rather than filing: not because you're likely to be pursued over it, but because having read it and acted sensibly, if something genuinely needed acting on, is a materially better position than not having looked at all.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Ask which product tier is being quoted — standard or enhanced — before comparing two conveyancing quotes that bundle "searches" as a single line item.
  • Check whether a coal mining search is included if the property's anywhere near a former coalfield, rather than assuming your solicitor has already added it.
  • Look for Search Code compliance — a provider regulated under the Property Codes Compliance Board's Search Code, which most Groundsure and Landmark products are, gives you a route to redress if the report's genuinely wrong.
  • Read the actual report, not just the summary page, and ask your solicitor to explain any flag in plain terms rather than a boilerplate "for your information" note.
  • Don't commission a full environmental consultant's investigation reflexively off the back of a minor, distant flag — ask what specifically is being followed up and why before paying several hundred pounds more for it.

Questions I get asked

How much does an environmental search cost in 2026? A standard search typically costs £35–£75, an enhanced insurance-backed version £90–£165. Add £30–£60 for a coal mining search in a coalfield area, and the full standard search pack usually totals £250–£450.

Do I need one if I'm a cash buyer with no mortgage? There's no legal requirement either way — it's a lender condition, not a statutory one. Skipping it saves a small fee but removes a cheap early warning on contaminated land, flood and ground stability risk that's genuinely worth having regardless of how the purchase is funded.

What happens if my search flags a risk? Read what's actually flagged before assuming the worst; a wide-radius historic-use match is common and often irrelevant. Where a flag looks genuinely close to the property, the usual next step is an independent consultant's Phase 1 Desk Study, typically £600–£1,500+ for a residential-scale site — worth it for a genuine concern, not a routine follow-up.

Is this the same as a flood risk assessment or a drainage search? No. The environmental search screens flood risk as one category among several, using the same free national flood-mapping data, not a dedicated site-specific flood assessment. The water and drainage search (CON29DW) is a separate paper check of sewer connection records, not a physical inspection — covered in my drainage survey cost guide.
