Air source
heat pump running
cost in 2026

An air source heat pump typically costs £800–£1,300 a year to run in a three-bed semi on the October 2026 price cap, and £600–£900 on a heat pump tariff. The spread is almost entirely efficiency.

Published 10 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

For a typical three-bed semi, an air source heat pump costs £800–£1,300 a year to run on the standard Ofgem price cap from October 2026, falling to roughly £600–£900 on a heat pump tariff. A gas boiler heating the same house costs about £1,060–£1,130 in gas, before its standing charge.

That puts the heat pump somewhere between a little dearer and a good deal cheaper than gas, and which end you land on depends on one number most quotes state optimistically: the seasonal efficiency the pump actually achieves in your house, not the one on the brochure.

I assess houses and design retrofit plans for a living, and I do not sell or fit heat pumps. What follows is the arithmetic, where the published figures disagree, and what I would check before believing a running-cost promise.

The short answer

Prices were checked on 10 October 2026. Ofgem’s cap from 1 October to 31 December 2026 puts a typical household bill at £1,723, with electricity at 26.32p/kWh and gas at 7.97p/kWh as reported by Uswitch; VAT has been removed from domestic electricity, which is why electricity barely moved while gas rose about 8%. The table is my own arithmetic for a three-bed semi needing about 12,000 kWh of heat a year, the demand figure used in the independent cost guides I checked. Standing charges are left out because you pay an electricity standing charge either way.

Heat pump, standard cap, mediocre performance£1,050–£1,270Seasonal efficiency (SCOP) 2.5–3.0 at 26.32p
Heat pump, standard cap, good performance£790–£900SCOP 3.5–4.0 at 26.32p

The published guides land in the same area. Book a Builder puts a typical three-bed at £900–£1,300 at around 25p/kWh. The Eco Experts finds a heat pump costs only about £14 a year more than a new A-rated gas boiler and around £340 less than an old G-rated one. BestBuilders, using the April 2026 cap, found a well-insulated house £120–£380 a year cheaper on a heat pump; gas has risen since then, which helps the heat pump, but its table also shows a larger Victorian house costing £24 a year more. The 18p blended rate in my table is an assumption: BestBuilders quotes heat pump tariffs at 12–18p/kWh off-peak, and you will pay more than that outside the cheap windows.

What drives the running cost

Seasonal efficiency (SCOP). A SCOP of 3 means three units of heat for every unit of electricity. At 12,000 kWh of heat that is 4,000 kWh of electricity; at 2.5 it is 4,800 kWh; at 3.5 it is about 3,430. That swing alone is roughly £360 a year at 26.32p.

Flow temperature. The hotter the water you ask for, the harder the pump works. Radiators sized for a 70°C boiler may need replacing or supplementing so the pump can run cooler for longer. This is the single biggest controllable factor and it is a design decision, not a product feature.

The heat the house needs. Efficiency multiplies a heat demand, and a leaky house has a big one. Your EPC lists an estimated annual space heating demand; it is a rough guide, but it is a better starting point than the installer’s square-footage estimate.

Tariff. Standard-cap electricity costs around three and a bit times as much as gas per unit. A heat pump tariff with cheap windows, plus a cylinder and controls able to shift heating into them, is what closes the gap. Octopus reports Cosy customers saved an average of £224 over a year against a gas boiler at 85% efficiency on its standard variable tariff; that is the supplier’s own fleet data, self-selected and from a company that sells the tariff, so I treat it as indicative rather than a promise.

Region. Kent, Sussex, Surrey and London installation quotes, including replacement radiators, tend to sit toward the top of the national ranges; the running cost itself depends on the house and tariff, not the postcode.

The efficiency number nobody quotes honestly

Most installer quotes and MCS paperwork show a design SCOP of 3.5–4.5. UK field data is lower. The Electrification of Heat demonstration project found a median seasonal performance factor of about 2.8 for air source units, and an analysis of roughly 1,700 Renewable Heat Incentive installations reported a median of 2.69 against a design average of 3.59. Newer installations appear to be doing better, and Octopus reports around 4.1 across its Cosy fleet, but that fleet is self-selected, newer and well-monitored.

My advice is to cost your house at SCOP 3, treat 3.5 as a good outcome and 2.5 as the downside, and ask your installer for the design flow temperature and heat loss calculation behind the figure. If they cannot show them, the SCOP on the quote is a sales number.

Will it actually be cheaper than gas?

In a well-insulated house, usually a little, and clearly so on a heat pump tariff. In a draughty, poorly insulated one, often not. BestBuilders’ own comparison shows the saving shrinking or reversing as insulation worsens, and its blunt conclusion is that a heat pump is cheaper to run in a well-insulated home but rarely cheaper across its lifetime at today’s prices. I agree with the second half; the capital cost is the bigger issue, which is why my air source heat pump cost guide and its grant arithmetic matter as much as this page. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme gives £7,500 in England and Wales, and from 21 July 2026 £9,000 for homes switching from oil or LPG, with funding confirmed to 2029/30.

Three things move the odds in your favour, in the order I would do them:

  • Fabric first. Cutting the heat demand cuts the bill whatever you heat with. My loft insulation and cavity wall insulation guides cover the cheap wins, and a retrofit assessment ranks them for your actual house.
  • Low flow temperature. Ask for a heat loss calculation room by room, emitter sizing to match, and weather compensation set up and tested at commissioning.
  • The right tariff and, if you have it, solar. A heat pump uses most of its power in winter, when solar gives least, so do not buy panels to justify a pump. See my solar panels cost guide.

Compare against the boiler you would otherwise buy, not the one you have now. My new boiler cost guide covers that side honestly, and a heat pump can reduce your gas standing charge to nothing if you disconnect, worth roughly £108 a year at the current rate.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • A running cost promised without a stated SCOP, tariff and heat demand. Ask what electricity price it assumes.
  • No heat loss calculation. Sizing a unit from floor area is how oversized, short-cycling pumps happen.
  • Savings quoted against an old G-rated boiler. The fair comparison is a new A-rated one.
  • No mention of flow temperature or emitters. If the radiators are not discussed, the efficiency is probably optimistic.
  • A tariff presented as guaranteed. Tariffs change, and the cheap windows only help if your heating can use them.
  • Written quotes. Get at least three, with the design SCOP and flow temperature written into each.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to run an air source heat pump per year? For a three-bed semi needing about 12,000 kWh of heat a year, typically £800–£1,300 on the Ofgem price cap from October 2026 (electricity 26.32p/kWh), depending on whether the pump achieves a seasonal efficiency nearer 2.5 or 4. On a heat pump tariff it can fall to roughly £600–£900.

Is an air source heat pump cheaper to run than a gas boiler? Often slightly, sometimes not. On the standard cap a heat pump with a seasonal efficiency of about 3 costs roughly the same as a gas boiler. It becomes clearly cheaper with a good SCOP of 3.5 or more, a heat pump tariff, or solar generation, and in a well-insulated house.

What is a good SCOP for a heat pump? The manufacturer or MCS design figure is often 3.5–4.5, but UK field trials have found real-world seasonal performance nearer 2.7–2.8 on average. A well-designed system in a well-insulated house running low flow temperatures can reach 3.5 or more.

Do heat pumps cost more to run in an older house? They can. A poorly insulated house needs more heat and usually a higher flow temperature, which lowers efficiency. In some cases the heat pump costs more to run than gas, which is why I would look at fabric and emitters before the pump itself.
