The best heated
throws and
electric blankets

Most of the energy advice I give as a retrofit assessor is about the building — loft depth, cavity fill, where the heat's actually escaping. But not all of it needs a spec sheet. On a genuinely cold evening, warming the person sat on the sofa or the bed they're about to get into costs a fraction of warming the whole room around them, and it's the cheapest, least disruptive suggestion I make all winter. Here's how to judge a heated throw or electric blanket, what it actually costs to run, the safety checks worth doing before you plug one in, and seven products worth buying.

A heated throw or electric blanket runs at somewhere between about 35 and 150 watts depending on the setting, against a gas boiler heating a whole house that's commonly costing somewhere north of 80 pence an hour to run at anything like full output. That gap is the entire case for this category: on a cold evening, warming yourself directly is very often cheaper, faster and more comfortable than waiting for the thermostat to catch up, especially in a room you're only using for an hour or two.

It isn't a substitute for a house that's actually cold because it's poorly insulated, badly heated, or damp — that's a fabric-first job, not a blanket's. And it doesn't touch an EPC rating, whatever the marketing on the box implies about "energy saving." What it is, honestly, is a cheap, low-risk way to take the edge off a cold room for the cost of a coffee's worth of electricity, provided it's a product that's actually safe to use and you know when to retire it.

Below is how I'd judge what's on the shelf, the running-cost numbers worked through properly, and seven heated throws and electric blankets worth considering.

How I judge a heated throw or electric blanket

A genuinely published wattage, not just a marketing claim. A "3p an hour" line on the packaging is a manufacturer's own figure, usually unaudited — useful as a rough steer, not something to take as gospel. A published wattage lets you do the sum yourself against your own electricity rate rather than trusting someone else's arithmetic.

Timer duration and what happens when it runs out. A throw for evening sofa use wants a short auto-shutoff, an hour or two, so it can't be left running unattended. A blanket meant to be on overnight needs an explicit "safe all-night" setting, not just a generic overheat cutoff — check the listing says which it is, don't assume.

A safety mark that's actually current. Look for UKCA or CE on the product itself, and treat "BEAB approved" as a bonus where it's shown, not a given — Electrical Safety First's own advice notes marks can be used fraudulently by disreputable sellers, so a badge on a listing photo isn't proof by itself.

Dual control on anything shared. A double or king blanket with one controller heats both sides identically, which fails the moment two people want different settings. Independent left/right zones cost a little more and solve a genuinely common household argument.

Machine washable, properly. Every product here claims it, but the controller always has to be unclipped first — worth checking how easily it detaches before buying, since a fiddly connector is the difference between actually washing it each season and not bothering.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The pence-per-hour numbers, worked through properly. At the current Ofgem price cap of 26.32p/kWh (electricity, Direct Debit, Oct–Dec 2026), a 100W throw costs about 2.6p an hour, a 130W throw about 3.4p. A single-control electric blanket on its lowest setting can run as low as 1p an hour; the same blanket at full power on a 150W double is closer to 4p. Against that, a whole-house gas central heating system commonly runs somewhere between 80p and £1.20 an hour depending on boiler output, home size and how well the fabric holds heat — a genuinely wide range, not a precise figure, but even at the low end it's fifteen to fifty times the cost of warming yourself directly. That's the entire case for this category on a cold evening in one room.

None of this moves your EPC rating. RdSAP, the methodology behind a domestic Energy Performance Certificate, only scores fixed heating systems — a plug-in throw or blanket, however good, isn't a recognised measure the way a new boiler or loft insulation is. If the real problem is a low-scoring certificate rather than one cold evening, that's a different conversation; my guide to EPC costs covers what an assessment involves, and my EPC service page is the next step if it's time for a fresh one.

The ten-year rule is worth taking seriously. Electrical Safety First recommends replacing an electric blanket after ten years, and specifically flags older blankets as a risk — inspect the fabric and cable for damage before first use each winter, especially after months in storage, and stop using it immediately at any sign of scorching, arcing, buzzing or a blown fuse. An old-style BEAB mark, rather than the current UKCA or CE marking, is itself a sign a blanket predates the current safety-testing regime and is likely well past due.

Marketing running-cost claims are a starting point, not a guarantee. "3p an hour" or "from 1p an hour" printed on a box is the manufacturer's own figure, usually for the lowest setting, not an independently audited average. Where a listing publishes an actual wattage — genuinely rare in this category — you can check it against your own tariff; where it doesn't, treat the marketing line as a rough steer and nothing firmer.

This doesn't fix a genuinely cold room. If a bedroom is cold because there's no fixed heating reaching it, the insulation's poor, or there's a damp or draught problem behind the symptom, a blanket treats the person, not the building — useful for one evening, not a substitute for the actual fix. Where that's the underlying issue, my retrofit assessment service is the place to start.

Questions I get asked

Is it safe to leave an electric blanket on all night? Only if the product itself says so — look for an explicit "safe all-night" setting or a genuinely low-wattage overnight mode, not just a generic overheat cutoff. Most heated throws are designed for supervised evening use with a short timer rather than unattended overnight running; check the listing before assuming either way.

Do heated throws or electric blankets affect my EPC rating? No. RdSAP assesses fixed heating systems — boiler, radiators, insulation — not portable plug-in appliances, however efficient. A better throw won't move a certificate; better fabric and fixed heating will.

How old is too old for an electric blanket? Electrical Safety First's guidance is ten years, and that's specifically flagged as a replace-it point, not just a "consider it." Test and inspect it before first use every winter in the meantime, particularly after it's been folded away in storage over summer.

Heated throw or electric blanket — which is cheaper to run? Both are cheap relative to central heating, but a throw is generally the lower-wattage option for evening sofa use, while a blanket on its lowest overnight setting can undercut even that. The bigger cost difference in practice is how long each one gets left running, not the wattage difference between them.
