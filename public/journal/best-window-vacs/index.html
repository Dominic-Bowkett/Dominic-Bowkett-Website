The best
window vacs
for condensation

Wet glass every morning is the first sign most people notice of a damp-air problem. A window vac keeps the water off the sill and the seal; it does not cure the cause. Here is how to tell what you are buying, and seven worth looking at.

Half the surveys I do in winter start with someone pointing at a window sill. Water on the glass, black speckling in the corner of the reveal, a swollen timber sill, a curtain gone mouldy at the hem. The homeowner wants to know whether the windows are failing. Mostly they are not. The glass is the coldest surface in the room, so it is where the moisture in the air shows up first.

A window vac is a squeegee with a motor. It sucks the water off the glass into a small tank, and that is all it does. It does not make the room drier, and it will not fix the cause of the condensation. What it does do is stop that water running onto the frame, soaking into a timber sill and feeding mould on the sealant, which is a real benefit. Used every morning in winter, it is a cheap, practical way of managing the symptom while you deal with the cause.

So read this as a guide to a symptom-management tool, not a cure. If the room is wet because the air is wet, a dehumidifier, a proper extractor fan, trickle vents or positive input ventilation is where the real fix sits; I have written those up separately and link them below. Here are seven cordless window vacs, compared on what matters.

How I judge a window vac

Run time against how many windows you have. Makers quote run time in minutes or in “windows per charge”, and the two are not comparable. A 25 to 35 minute vac is enough for most flats and terraces. A house with a lot of glass, or a conservatory, is where the 100 minute models earn their money. Treat any “120 windows per charge” claim cautiously.

Blade width and reach. A wide blade means fewer passes on a patio door, but it is no use in a small sash pane or a bathroom window. Two blade widths in the box, or a small separate head, solves that. Corners and the bottom edge of a pane are where cheap units leave a line of water behind, and that line is the water that runs onto the sill.

Tank size and how easily it empties. A 100ml tank fills quickly on a wet morning, and a larger tank means fewer trips to the sink. Whatever the size, the tank should lift out and empty without a fight, because a unit that is awkward to empty stays in the cupboard.

Battery: removable or sealed. A sealed battery makes the whole unit disposable when it fades. A removable one lets you buy a spare. For a tool used daily from November to March, that matters more than most spec sheets let on.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A window vac is only half of the answer. The other half is the moisture in the room. Humidity sitting above about 60 per cent, laundry drying indoors, showers with no extractor will all produce wet glass however well you wipe it. A cheap hygrometer tells you where you stand, and a CO2 monitor tells you whether the room is being ventilated at all.

Wipe the sill and the seal, not just the glass. The vac gets the water off the pane. The black mould I see is almost always on the silicone seal at the bottom corner and on the timber or painted sill beneath it. Take the vac along the bottom edge and run a cloth over the sill. Water that sits there for a week is the problem.

When the real fix is something else. If the glass is wet every morning, the fixes are ventilation (an extractor fan, trickle vents or a PIV unit), removing the moisture source (a heated airer rather than laundry draped on radiators) and, in a very damp house, a dehumidifier. Tired single glazing or failed sealed units are a different issue and cost real money; the double glazing cost guide covers that. Thermal curtains can make condensation worse if the room is not ventilated, which I cover in the thermal curtains guide.

What the box pushes that does not matter. Run-time headlines in “windows per charge”, the number of accessories and the cleaning concentrate. Water and a squeegee blade do the job. Check the blade is replaceable and the tank empties easily, and ignore the rest.

Questions I get asked

Do window vacs actually stop condensation? No. They remove the water that has already formed on the glass. The condensation comes back the next night unless the humidity in the room comes down. They are a good way of limiting damage while you fix the cause.

Are they worth it if I only have a few windows? Often yes, if the condensation is a daily winter occurrence. The alternative is ten minutes with a cloth every morning, and a cloth tends to leave the water on the sill. If it is a one-off, a squeegee will do.

Can I use one on moisture between double-glazed panes? No. Water between the panes means the seal has failed. No window vac will clear that, and the sealed unit needs replacing. A surveyor will note it as a defect.

Will a window vac get rid of black mould on the sill? No. It stops the water that feeds it. Existing mould needs treating separately, and persistent mould on a window reveal is often the first sign of a wider ventilation problem.

If the glass is wet every winter and you want to know why, that is a job for an assessment rather than another gadget: a ventilation assessment or a building survey will tell you whether it is the air, the glazing or the house.
