The best
asbestos test kits,
and their limits

A DIY asbestos test kit answers one narrow question honestly: does this specific piece of material contain asbestos. It does not survey your house, and it is not the legal document a duty-holder or a demolition contractor needs. Here’s what a postal lab kit actually does, how to take a sample without breathing anything you shouldn’t, and seven kits worth using when a kit really is the right tool.

The question comes up on more surveys than almost anything else: someone’s about to lift a floor tile, take down an old garage roof, or strip a textured ceiling, and they want to know — before they touch it — whether it’s asbestos. Fair question. Chrysotile, amosite and crocidolite were used across UK construction right up until the 1999 ban, and they turn up in the ordinary places: Artex and other textured coatings, thermoplastic and vinyl floor tiles and their black bitumen adhesive, corrugated garage and shed roofing sheets, soffits and bargeboards, old boiler flues, asbestos insulating board behind fuse cupboards and in partition walls, and pipe lagging in older plant rooms. None of it looks distinctly like anything — you cannot identify asbestos type, or confirm it’s present at all, by eye. That’s what a lab test is for.

A DIY test kit is genuinely useful for exactly that question and nothing wider. You take a small sample of one specific material, following precautions that matter more than the kit itself, post it to a laboratory, and get back a certificate identifying whether asbestos was detected and which type, usually within a day or two of the sample arriving. Most of the kits below use polarised light microscopy — PLM — the standard method for bulk material identification, run by a UKAS-accredited laboratory. That’s a real, useful answer about that piece of Artex or that floor tile. It is not an answer about the loft insulation, the garage roof, or the flue three rooms away, and it is not a document that satisfies anyone’s legal duty to manage asbestos across a building.

That last point is where I see people get caught out, so it gets its own section below rather than a footnote. In short: a single-sample kit is the right tool for a homeowner who wants to know about one specific patch of Artex before they redecorate. It is the wrong tool for a landlord managing a block of flats, and it is not a substitute for the refurbishment and demolition survey the law requires before most invasive building work, whoever owns the property. Seven kits below, from a £20-ish single sample to a £90-plus multi-sample pack for testing several materials on the same job.

How I judge an asbestos test kit

Accreditation, not packaging. The only thing that actually matters is who analyses the sample and under what accreditation. UKAS — the UK Accreditation Service — is the mark to look for on a kit aimed at a UK property; it’s the standard a professional asbestos surveyor’s laboratory works to. A couple of kits sold into the UK market use US-accredited labs (AIHA or NVLAP rather than UKAS) with the same PLM method — not wrong, but worth knowing before you rely on the certificate for anything UK-specific.

What the sample actually is. These are bulk material kits — a physical scraping or offcut, not an air sample. That matches most domestic questions (is this Artex asbestos, is this floor tile asbestos) but won’t tell you about airborne fibre levels, a different and more specialist test no kit here does.

Turnaround once the lab has it. Same-day and next-day results, counted from arrival rather than posting, are standard now. The variable no kit controls is the postal leg either side.

How far one sample stretches. A single sample answers one question about one material. Artex on the ceiling, old floor tiles in the hall and a suspect garage roof is three questions, not one — and three single-sample kits usually cost more than one multi-sample kit sized to the job.

Whether PPE is actually included. Several kits ship with no PPE at all, on the basis that scraping a small sample is low-risk. I’d treat that as a marketing line rather than sampling advice — see the precautions below, which apply whether or not a mask came in the box.

Taking the sample without breathing anything you shouldn’t

Every kit here relies on you disturbing a small piece of a material that might contain asbestos, and the precautions matter more than which kit you buy. Dampen the area first with a plant spray or a wet cloth — a wetted fibre is far less likely to become airborne than a dry one. Never use a power tool, sander or drill; a hand scraper, a sharp blade, or breaking off a small offcut by hand is the right approach for everything on this list. Wear disposable gloves and a properly fitted FFP3 respirator, not an ordinary dust mask — fit matters as much as filter rating. Take the smallest sample that will do the job, seal it, then double-bag it before it goes near the post. Wipe the area afterwards with a damp cloth rather than a dry brush or household vacuum, which just redistributes anything disturbed. Wash your hands before anything else. None of this is included in most of the kits above — it’s worth doing regardless of what came in the box.

What a kit can’t do — and when the law actually requires a survey

A kit tests a material. A survey assesses a building. This is the whole point of this guide, so it’s worth stating plainly. A test kit gives you a yes-or-no answer, and which fibre type, for the one piece of material you sampled. It says nothing about the rest of the house, doesn’t assess condition or risk of disturbance across a building, and isn’t signed off by anyone accountable for the judgement of where to look. A survey does all three.

The duty to manage, and who it binds. Under the Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012, Regulation 4 places a “duty to manage” asbestos on whoever is responsible for non-domestic premises, and separately on the common parts of residential buildings — shared hallways, stairwells, roof voids and plant rooms of a block of flats or sheltered housing, not the inside of an individual flat. In practice this means identifying, recording and managing asbestos-containing materials, which is why a management survey, carried out by a competent surveyor to HSE guidance, exists as a distinct product from a DIY kit. A homeowner sampling their own garage roof isn’t bound by Regulation 4 the way a landlord managing a shared building is.

The refurbishment and demolition survey is the one that catches homeowners out. Regardless of who owns the property, suitable arrangements must be in place before construction, refurbishment or demolition work disturbs the fabric of a pre-2000 building — in practice a refurbishment and demolition (R&D) survey by a competent surveyor before work starts, not a homeowner’s own sample from the one area they thought to check. An R&D survey is intrusive by design: it opens voids, lifts flooring, and looks behind finishes a DIY kit can only sample where already exposed. If a contractor is coming in to do invasive work on a pre-2000 property, that’s the point to commission a proper survey rather than rely on a kit result from elsewhere in the house. Visual identification is not a substitute for either. Chrysotile, amosite and crocidolite — white, brown and blue asbestos — cannot reliably be identified by looking at a piece of Artex, board or floor tile. All three are treated identically under UK regulation regardless of colour; the only way to tell which, if any, is present is the lab test. Assuming “it’s probably fine, it doesn’t look like the photos” is the most common way people end up disturbing something they shouldn’t have.

Questions I get asked

Is a DIY asbestos test kit result legally valid? The lab result is a genuine, accredited analysis of the sample sent — that part is real. It isn’t a survey document. For a landlord, managing agent or duty-holder under Regulation 4, or anyone about to carry out refurbishment or demolition work, a kit result doesn’t satisfy the requirement for a proper survey by a competent surveyor — it’s evidence about one material, not the record a duty-holder needs to keep.

What materials in a UK home are worth testing? Artex and other textured coatings applied before 2000, vinyl or thermoplastic floor tiles and their black adhesive, corrugated cement roofing sheets on garages and sheds, soffits and bargeboards, asbestos insulating board around old fuse cupboards, and lagging on older pipework and boiler flues are the ones I flag most on survey. Loose-fill loft insulation is a rarer case needing specialist handling rather than a postal kit — if suspected, don’t disturb it and get advice first.

Can I just assume Artex has asbestos and skip the test? Treating any pre-2000 textured coating as if it contains asbestos until proven otherwise is a reasonable default. But assuming it’s fine because it doesn’t look like the photos is the risky version of the same shortcut — a £20–£40 test removes the guesswork either way before deciding whether a job is DIY or a licensed contractor’s.

What happens if a sample comes back positive? Nothing that was there before has changed — a confirmed result just means you now know to manage it rather than guess. Undisturbed, sound material in good condition is often safely left in place and monitored. Damaged or friable material, or anything about to be disturbed by planned work, is a job for a licensed or suitably trained contractor to remove, not DIY regardless of how the sample was taken. If the result changes the scope of planned work, that’s the point to bring in a survey rather than proceed on one sample.
