The phone is the most-used piece of kit in a surveyor’s day. Half of what ends up in a report starts as a photograph taken one-handed from the top of a stepladder, with a loft hatch in the other hand and dust in the air. It is also the thing that gets dropped: from a ladder, off a car roof, onto a concrete floor in a boiler room, into the wet at the bottom of a crawl space. A survey is a hard environment for a glass slab.

There are two sensible answers. One is a rugged phone, built with a sealed body, a thick bumper and a screen you can use in gloves. The other is a rugged case on the ordinary phone you already own. A rugged phone is thicker and heavier and usually has a weaker camera; a case keeps the phone you like and costs a fraction of the price. Neither is right for everyone, and I have set out below which suits which kind of work.

This guide sits alongside the one on rugged tablets. If your site notes and photographs go straight into survey software on a tablet, the phone is the backup. If the phone is the only device in your pocket, it carries the whole job.

How I judge a rugged phone or case

Ingress protection (IP) rating. The two digits tell you the protection against dust and water. IP68 means sealed against dust and rated for immersion to a stated depth and time, which in practice covers rain, a dropped phone in a puddle and a quick rinse. IP69K adds high-pressure hot water. Look for the rating on the listing, and treat any rugged claim without one with caution.

Drop standard. MIL-STD-810 is a US military test method, and makers use it as a label. It does not mean the phone is indestructible, and a maker’s own testing is not independent. It is still a better sign than a bare claim of “tough”. Look at the stated drop height and surface.

Screen and glove use. A scratched or cracked screen is the usual end of a phone. A rugged handset with a raised bezel and a hardened glass front protects it better, and one with a glove mode lets you keep your hands covered in a loft. If you use a case, add a tempered-glass screen protector.

Battery and software. A day on site, with photos, notes and a navigation app running, drains a phone. A rugged phone with a bigger battery gets through it. Software updates matter as much: a phone that holds client data should receive security updates for years, not months.

Weight and pocket. A rugged phone is a brick. It is hard to carry in a shirt pocket, and a heavy handset gets left in the van. A slim rugged case on a normal phone is much easier to live with.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A case on a good phone beats a rugged phone with poor software. The camera on a mainstream flagship is usually much better than the camera on a budget rugged handset, and the photographs are the product. If you take hundreds of photos a day, protect the good camera with a case instead of replacing it.

Thermal cameras built into rugged phones are mostly a novelty. A few rugged phones include a low-resolution thermal sensor. For a real thermal survey you want the resolution and calibration of a proper thermal imaging camera, not a sensor the size of a lentil. Treat a built-in thermal camera as a talking point, not a survey tool.

Drop ratings are not a licence to throw it. A phone that survives a 1.8m drop onto concrete in a test may still crack its screen on the corner of a stair. Use a strap or a lanyard, and keep it in a pocket with a button rather than loose in a bag with tools.

Waterproof is not the same as wet-proof. A sealed phone survives rain and a puddle. It does not like a charging port full of grit and damp. Keep the port covers shut, rinse off salt water, and dry the phone before you charge it.

Back it up. Whatever you buy, photographs on a phone that falls off a roof are lost photographs. Set it to upload to the cloud or sync to a laptop at the end of every day. A dictation app on the same phone, or a separate dictaphone, saves your thumbs on the long jobs.

Questions I get asked

Is a rugged phone better than a rugged case? It depends on how you work. A rugged phone suits someone who spends most days on site in wet and dusty conditions and wants a device they do not have to think about. A case on a normal phone suits someone who does a mix of site work and desk work and wants a better camera. For most surveyors, I would start with a good case and a screen protector.

Are budget rugged phones from Doogee or Blackview any good? The hardware is usually sound for the price, and owner feedback on durability is generally positive. The weak points are software updates, the camera and UK support. They make a good second phone for a van or a site tool, and a risky only phone for client data.

What IP rating do I need? IP68 is enough for rain, damp loft spaces and the odd dunking. IP69K is for pressure washing, which is more than a survey needs. Do not pay extra for it unless you work somewhere very wet.

Can I use a rugged phone to take survey photographs? Yes, but check the camera before you rely on it. Many survey reports are built from photographs, and the quality of a budget rugged camera in a dark loft is often poor. Compare sample photographs in the owner reviews, and keep a better camera or your normal phone for the key images.
