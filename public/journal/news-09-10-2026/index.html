Surveyor’s notes
9 October 2026:
Awaab’s Law next

Damp-and-mould deadlines are heading for private landlords, mortgage rates have hit a three-year high, and the Ombudsman has expelled a surveying firm. Here is what each means on the ground.

Published 9 October 2026 Reading time · 5 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Today is about pressure on both sides of the tenancy and the sale. Government wants private landlords on the same repair clock as social ones, lenders are repricing buyers out of the market, and the Ombudsman is reminding small firms that its awards are not optional.

Awaab’s Law is coming for private landlords, so start with the damp

The Negotiator reports that the government has opened a consultation on extending Awaab’s Law from social housing to the private rented sector. It closes on 18 December 2026, and the article says it would take coverage from about 4 million households to about 9 million. It also reports that the consultation asks about temporary accommodation, housing for young people leaving care and supported housing, and that around one in ten private rented homes has a serious Category 1 hazard. Landlord Today carries Housing Minister Matthew Pennycook saying the law is “already helping to drive up standards in the social housing sector”.

No timescales for private landlords have been set yet. That is the point of the consultation, so the real detail, how many days to investigate and how many to start repairs, is still to come. Social landlords were given fixed deadlines when the law began in October 2025, and I would expect the private version to borrow that shape.

My read is that the practical change is evidence. A landlord who is asked to fix mould on a deadline needs to know whether it is condensation, a leaking gutter, rising damp or a failed cavity wall, because the wrong fix, usually a fungicidal wash and a coat of paint, will be back within a season. Most of the damp I see in South East period houses is a ventilation and heating problem layered onto a maintenance one. Landlords should deal with the cause now, before a clock starts.

Who should do what: landlords with older stock should get a surveyor or damp specialist to diagnose any recurring mould, keep the report, and fix extractor fans, blocked gutters and failed seals first. Tenants should report in writing and keep copies. The consultation is open to anyone, and small landlords who say nothing will not be able to complain about the detail later.

The five-year mortgage hit 6%, and buyers will feel it at the valuation

Moneyfactscompare.co.uk reports that the average five-year fixed rate reached 6% on 5 October, the first time in about three years, with the two-year average at 5.98%. Sub-5% deals have fallen from roughly 1,500 in early September to nine, after repeated rate rises from lenders including Barclays, HSBC, Lloyds, Nationwide, Santander and TSB. Rachel Springall is quoted saying it would be “unwise” for lenders to price deals too low when swap rates and wholesale funding costs are elevated. The article puts the swap-rate rise down to Middle East conflict concerns, inflation pressure and a global bond sell-off that has pushed ten-year gilt yields to 18-year highs.

Averages are not what any one borrower pays, and a 5 October snapshot will move. But the direction is clear, and it sits uncomfortably beside this week’s flat Lloyds index. Higher rates cut what buyers can borrow, and lenders then lean on valuations. A down-valuation on a house with an unresolved damp, roof or structural question is where a deal collapses, or where a buyer asks for money off.

If you are buying, do not leave the survey until after you have fallen in love with the house. Get it early, know what the repairs will cost, and use it to negotiate honestly. If you are selling, fix the cheap visible defects, such as gutters, flaking render and a stained ceiling, because every one of them gives a nervous buyer a reason to reduce the offer. If you are a landlord remortgaging, an EPC and condition picture ready before the lender asks will save you a fortnight.

An Ombudsman expulsion that every buyer of a survey should read

The Negotiator reports that The Property Ombudsman has expelled three firms for not paying awards. One is Tothill Residential Surveying Ltd of Mundon, Essex, which the article says did not deliver a survey report that a buyer had paid £500 for, leading to an £800 award. The Chief Ombudsman, Lesley Horton, is quoted: “An Ombudsman award is not optional.”

I am reporting what the article says about a complaint I know nothing else about, and I will not comment on the firm. The general lesson is for the buyer. A survey is a paid-for report, not a vague promise, and you should know before you instruct who is delivering it, by when, under which standard, and which redress scheme or professional body stands behind them.

Before you pay anyone for a survey, ask for the delivery date in writing, check the surveyor’s qualifications and the body they answer to, and keep your instruction email. If something goes wrong, complain to the firm first, then use whichever redress route they belong to.

Rent caps are being priced already, but not yet written

Property118 has run the numbers on the Green Party leader Zack Polanski’s 2 October conference proposal to limit private rent rises to the lowest of CPI, wage growth or 2% for three years. On England’s average rent of £1,459 a month, it calculates a landlord could receive £2,186 less over three years than under 4% annual rises, and says plainly that this is “not a forecast” of any one landlord’s loss. It also notes that the policy does not say whether existing tenancies are covered.

This is an opposition proposal, not law. I mention it because it changes the sums on EPC works. If rent cannot rise, the only way to recover the cost of upgrading is lower running costs or a longer hold. That favours measures that cut the tenant’s bills and keep the property lettable, such as insulation and draught-proofing, over cosmetic ones.

What I’d actually do

  1. Landlords with older stock: get any recurring mould diagnosed by a surveyor now and fix the cause, whether ventilation, gutters or heating, before private deadlines arrive.
  2. Anyone with practical experience: respond to the Awaab’s Law consultation before 18 December.
  3. Buyers: commission the survey early, because rising mortgage rates make down-valuations and renegotiation more likely.
  4. Sellers: fix the cheap visible defects before the first viewing.
  5. Anyone instructing a surveyor: get the delivery date, the standard and the redress scheme in writing.
