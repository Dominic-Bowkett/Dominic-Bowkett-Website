Lintel
replacement cost
in 2026

Replacing a failed lintel typically costs £750–£2,500 for an ordinary window or door opening in 2026, more for wide openings or upper floors. The more useful question is whether the lintel has failed at all.

Published 1 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A lintel is the beam over a window or door that carries the wall above it. When one fails, the usual signs are cracks above the opening, sagging brickwork or a window that suddenly sticks. In 2026, replacing a failed lintel typically costs £750–£2,500 for an ordinary window or door opening, with wide openings, bays and garage doors running from £1,800 to £3,500 or more. The lintel itself is the cheap part, at roughly £50–£300. You are paying for propping the wall, a day or two of skilled labour, and making good the brickwork afterwards.

Prices depend heavily on access. A ground-floor window is a different job from a first-floor one that needs scaffolding, and a Victorian brick arch hiding a rotted timber beam is a different job again. The quotes in the sources below run from under £1,000 to over £3,500 for what sounds like the same repair, so the opening, the wall and the access decide the price.

I diagnose buildings for a living; I don’t install or replace lintels, so I have no stake in whether the job goes ahead. This is a straight read on the 2026 price, what moves it, and the step most guides skip: working out whether the lintel is the problem in the first place.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 1 October 2026 against Book A Builder UK’s 2026 lintel cost guide and Home Repair Guide’s 2026 UK guide, then compared with Ilkley Brickwork’s published pricing for brick-faced work. The first two agree closely at the low end and differ more on large and awkward openings. Checkatrade and MyBuilder both block automated access, so I haven’t relied on them. As with most trades in this series, Kent, Sussex, Surrey and London trend above national figures.

That fits a brick-faced period elevation where the face has to be taken down and matched, which is nearer the top of my range than the middle.

Do you actually need it?

Not always. Faded or chalky uPVC is cosmetic, and a clean and a re-seal of a gutter joint costs very little. Peeling paint on timber is a maintenance job, not a replacement job: scrape, treat, prime and repaint. Replacement earns its money when the board is soft, the board ends have split, the soffit has sagged or gone missing, or the gutter fixings have pulled out of rotten wood.

The sensible order is to find out why the board failed. A fascia that rots at one corner under a leaking gutter joint has a plumbing problem, not an age problem. Renew the board and leave the joint, and the new one will go the same way. Water running down the wall below a failed gutter is also how a roofline problem turns into the damp that gets blamed on rising damp; damp survey cost covers getting the cause identified first.

A worked example, using the overlapping figures: a 3-bed semi in uPVC, boards and gutters renewed together, comes to about £1,800–£3,500 in two guides (check whether scaffold is in each figure), and a third guide puts the same uPVC job lower, at £1,200–£2,500. If you add £200–£500 for rot found on the day, a sensible budget runs from about £2,000 to £4,000. The extra is worth setting aside because a roofline job tends to find something.

What drives the price

Span and load. A one-metre window head and a four-metre garage opening are different jobs. Wider openings need heavier lintels, more propping and longer on site. Roughly £50–£300 of the bill is the lintel itself; the guides I checked put labour at about £300 to over £1,800.

Access. Upper-floor lintels usually mean scaffolding, which the guides put at £400–£1,200 on its own. I’ve covered how that is priced in scaffolding cost. Interior work adds a premium too, because plaster, decorations and furniture all get in the way.

Propping. The wall above has to be held up while the old lintel comes out. Some firms quote temporary support separately at about £300–£800, and others bury it in the labour line. Ask which.

Making good. Brickwork repair, repointing, plastering and decorating are often the second-biggest line. If the brick is old and needs matching, it costs more; I’ve explained the mortar side in repointing cost.

Engineer and approvals. Where the work changes the opening or the load path, expect calculations, which one guide puts at £150–£300 for the basics and another at £300 to over £1,500 for a full report. What a structural engineer charges, and when a surveyor refers you on, is in structural engineer cost.

Is it actually the lintel?

This is where the money is saved. Lintels do fail, and the failure modes are well known. Older mild steel lintels corrode when they get wet, and rusting steel swells and pushes the brickwork apart. Timber lintels, common in older houses, rot or get attacked by insect larvae. Brick arches and soldier courses often hide a deteriorated timber or steel beam behind them. Concrete lintels can crack if they are overloaded or poorly installed. Too little bearing at the ends is another cause: a lintel with too little seating on either side was never going to last. Alterations that add load, such as a new storey or a removed chimney breast, can also overload one that was fine for decades.

The signs that point to the lintel itself are a horizontal crack directly above the opening, stepped cracks running up from the corners, rust staining on the mortar at the window head, a sagging brick arch, and windows or doors that start sticking. Those are worth taking seriously.

But a crack near a window isn’t a diagnosis. Cracks at the corners and heads of openings also come from thermal movement, shrinkage, shallow foundations moving, and tired pointing, none of which a new lintel will fix. Where the cause is foundation movement, you are into the territory covered in underpinning cost, and a new lintel just moves the crack. Where the wall is a cavity wall, corroded ties can mimic lintel problems, which is a separate repair covered in wall tie replacement cost.

The sensible order is: look, then monitor, then repair. A surveyor can usually tell from crack pattern, building age and lintel type whether the lintel is the suspect, and may recommend opening up the internal finishes or getting an engineer in before anyone orders steel. A tell-tale crack monitor across the crack for a few weeks tells you whether it’s still live. A borescope or a small inspection hole above the opening shows what is actually carrying the wall. If a roofer or builder tells you over the phone that you “need a new lintel” before anyone has looked at it, get a second opinion.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • A named lintel type and size. Modern work normally means a galvanised or stainless steel lintel or a proprietary insulated cavity lintel, sized for the span and the load above it.
  • Propping shown as a line item, so you know the wall above is supported while the old lintel is out.
  • Scaffold priced separately and clearly included or excluded.
  • Making good itemised: brickwork, repointing, internal plaster and decoration are different jobs with different prices.
  • A straight answer on building control and an engineer. If the contractor says neither is needed for a structural opening, ask them to put that in writing.
  • Waste removal included. Old lintel, rubble and plaster all have to go somewhere.
  • No pressure to sign on the day, particularly from a firm that found the “problem” during a free inspection for something else.
