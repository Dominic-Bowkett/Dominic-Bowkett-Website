Every building survey I do includes a look at the drains, and every time the same limit applies: I can lift a cover, watch the flow and see the first metre of pipe. Past that, I am guessing. A damp patch at the base of an outside wall, a crack running down from a downpipe, a gully that smells in summer — each can be a leaking joint, a root, a collapsed length of clay or nothing at all. A camera turns a guess into a picture, which is why drainage inspection is one of the few areas where the right tool changes the advice I give.

The market splits in two, and the split is where buyers go wrong. At one end are borescopes with a handheld screen and a cable of a few metres, sold at £60–£100. At the other are push-rod drain cameras on a reel, with a self-levelling head and a transmitter in the camera that a locator can pick up from above ground, priced from a few hundred to several thousand pounds. Both show a pipe. Only one shows you where the defect is, and that matters when the next step is digging. The rods end of this job is in the drain rods guide; the camera is what you reach for when rodding stops giving answers.

One thing before the products. A survey-grade finding on a drain is a CCTV report from a drainage contractor, with footage and a defect schedule, and it costs a few hundred pounds (see what a drainage survey costs). A camera you buy yourself is a tool for deciding whether you need that report. Hold that thought, because it shapes what to buy.

How I judge a drain inspection camera

Cable length and stiffness. A domestic drain run from house to public sewer is often 15–30m, with bends and sometimes a lengthy lateral. A cable shorter than the run only tells you about the first section. Stiffness matters more than length: a flexible cable folds up in a bend, and a camera that cannot be pushed past the first junction has not told you much.

A locator, or not. The head carries a small transmitter, and a handheld receiver picks it up through the ground, so you can mark a defect on the surface. Without it you know the pipe is cracked at “about 9m”; with it you know which paving slab to lift. If the next job is a repair, the locator is the feature that earns its keep.

Self-levelling head. A head that rotates freely keeps the picture the right way up as the camera rolls along the pipe. It sounds like a luxury until you try to read a crack in a picture that keeps turning over. Cheaper units have it more often now, but check.

Picture and recording. Clear footage in poor light is the point. Video recording to card lets you pass the evidence to a contractor, an insurer or a client, and a screenshot tied to a distance counter is far more useful than memory. The counter is easy to overlook and worth having.

How it survives the job. Drain cameras live in muck. Seals, hinges and cable reels fail first on cheap units. Look for a waterproof rating on the head, a head that can be replaced separately, and a reel you can hose down.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Match the camera to the question. If the question is “is there anything in this gully”, a borescope answers it. If it is “why does this drain keep blocking between the house and the road”, you need a push-rod camera or a contractor. Buying the cheapest one and then being disappointed that it does not reach the sewer is the commonest mistake in this category. The general-purpose borescope guide covers the short-cable end properly.

Clean the pipe first. A camera in a full, silted or greasy pipe sees nothing useful. Flush or rod the line first, which is why the rods and the camera are best bought as a pair. The drain unblockers and snakes guide covers what to try before you camera.

What the listings push that you can skip. Megapixel counts, mostly. Resolution in the head matters less than light and a clear lens, and a big megapixel claim on a tiny sensor is marketing. Wi-Fi to a phone is handy but no substitute for a proper monitor in bright light. Spare cables and carry cases in a bundle are rarely worth paying extra for.

Know where the camera stops. A camera tells you what you can see. It will not tell you whether a defect is serious, whether it is the owner’s responsibility or the water company’s, or what the repair will cost. Those are judgements for someone who reads drain reports daily, and that is what you pay a contractor for. In the same way a damp meter helps but does not diagnose, a drain camera shows the pipe and leaves the judgement to you.

Questions I get asked

What is the best drain inspection camera? For occasional use by a surveyor or landlord, a mid-priced push-rod camera with a 30m cable and a locator gives the best balance of capability and cost. For daily trade use, the Ridgid SeeSnake range is what most contractors choose, and you pay for the durability and support. For gullies and short pipes, a borescope is enough.

Can I inspect my own drains with a camera? You can look, and plenty of owners do. What a cheap camera will not produce is a report a conveyancer, lender or insurer will accept, because that needs recognised defect coding and a qualified operator. Use your own camera to decide whether to pay for one.

Do drain cameras work in a full pipe? Poorly. Water blurs the lens and silt hides the pipe wall. Camera work normally follows rodding or jetting to clear the line, which is why contractors quote both together.

How much does a CCTV drain survey cost? Commonly a few hundred pounds for a domestic run, depending on access and length. The drainage survey cost guide breaks it down, and for a single investigation it is usually cheaper than buying a camera.
