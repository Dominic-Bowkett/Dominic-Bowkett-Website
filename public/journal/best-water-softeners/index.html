A water softener is the only fix with decades of proper engineering behind it. An electronic “conditioner” is a different, cheaper and far less certain claim. Hard water isn’t a fault in a building; it’s the water. Most of Kent and East Sussex sits over chalk or the Lower Greensand, and the mains supply drawn from those aquifers carries a lot of dissolved calcium and magnesium as a result — often into the “hard” to “very hard” bracket the water companies themselves publish for the area. None of that is a defect I’d write up on a survey. What I do write up, regularly, is what decades of that water does inside a heating system: a kettle with a crust in the bottom is the visible, low-stakes version of the same mineral scaling on a combi boiler’s plate heat exchanger and around a hot water cylinder’s immersion element, where it reduces heat transfer, forces the appliance to work harder for the same output, and eventually contributes to failure.

A water softener treats the water itself before it reaches the boiler, cylinder or taps, swapping calcium and magnesium ions for sodium through a bed of resin — genuine ion exchange, the chemistry municipal and industrial softening has used for the best part of a century. It’s a permanent, whole-house appliance decision: plumbed into the incoming main, needing space, a drain for the brine waste, and salt topped up. An electronic or electromagnetic “descaler” promises something similar from a strap clipped around the pipe, with none of that plumbing — but it doesn’t soften the water in any way a lab can measure calcium leaving it. At best, it may change the crystal structure of scale that still forms into something looser and easier to rinse away. That’s a difference in kind, not just price, worth being honest about before spending anything.

What follows mixes both categories deliberately: four salt-based softeners and three electronic units, ranked and compared on their own terms. If a scaled system has already cost you a boiler or a cylinder, the new boiler cost and boiler service cost guides cover what that runs to; a magnetic filter and inhibitor deal with a related but different problem — corrosion sludge, not limescale — covered in the boiler filters guide.

How I judge a water softener

Genuine ion exchange, not just a marketing claim. A softener has to physically exchange calcium and magnesium ions for sodium through a resin bed and regenerate that resin with salt. That’s the only mechanism with real evidence behind it. Anything “electronic” or “magnetic” that claims to soften without salt is a different product, whatever the word on the box says.

Demand-initiated regeneration over a blind timer. Better units meter the water actually used and regenerate the resin only when needed, rather than on a fixed clock regardless of household occupancy. It saves salt, saves water, and on a metered supply it saves money.

Resin volume matched to the household and the hardness. A ten-litre unit copes with a couple in a flat; a family home on genuinely hard Kent or Sussex water wants more resin, or it’ll be regenerating uncomfortably often. Manufacturers publish capacity against people and hardness — worth checking against your water company’s published hardness figure, not a guess.

WRAS approval and a proper drinking-water bypass. Every unit plumbed into a UK mains supply needs to meet Water Regulations to prevent backflow contamination, and a decent installation leaves one unsoftened tap for drinking and cooking, since softened water carries more sodium — a real consideration for a low-sodium diet or mixing infant formula.

Space, drainage and access, not just the unit itself. A softener needs a spot near the incoming main, a drain point for the brine discharge, and enough clearance to top up salt without a fight. If none of that exists in the house, the best softener here is still the wrong purchase.

What actually matters — and what doesn't

Salt-based softening and electronic conditioning are not the same claim. This is the single most important thing to be straight about. A salt-based softener genuinely removes calcium and magnesium through ion exchange — measurable, understood chemistry with a century of engineering behind it. An electronic or electromagnetic device does not remove anything; at best, the more credible evidence suggests it changes the physical structure of scale that still forms, making it softer and easier to rinse off rather than preventing it outright. Independent UK trade and consumer-body testing has generally been sceptical of the stronger prevention claims on the packaging. Buy an electronic unit as a lower-cost, lower-certainty experiment, not a like-for-like alternative to a softener.

It won't undo scale that's already there. Neither category reverses existing limescale inside a boiler's heat exchanger or around a cylinder's immersion element. A softener (or, less reliably, an electronic unit) stops the problem getting worse from here; a system already showing reduced hot water flow, kettling noises, or a failed immersion element needs a descale or a service first, alongside whatever prevention you fit afterwards.

A softener is a whole-house plumbing decision, not a DIY afternoon. Beyond the unit cost, it needs space near the incoming main, a drain point for the brine discharge, and Water Regulations-compliant fitting with backflow protection. That's a poor fit for a rented flat where you can't alter the plumbing, a property with nowhere sensible to route a drain, or anywhere the mains supply is already softened upstream, which does happen on some managed developments. An electronic conditioner sidesteps all of that, a genuine point in its favour even setting the evidence question aside.

Running cost is ongoing, not just upfront. A salt-based softener needs regular salt (block or tablet, bought in bags) and uses extra water regenerating the resin, which matters on a metered supply. Electronic units cost nothing to run beyond a trickle of electricity. Factor both into the comparison, not just the price on the box.

What gets pushed that doesn't matter as much as it sounds. Smart apps and Wi-Fi monitoring are genuinely useful for a landlord managing a property at a distance, and largely unnecessary for an owner-occupier glancing at the salt level once a month. “Star ratings” on electronic units are the manufacturer's own scale, not an independent standard, and shouldn't be read as if they were.

Questions I get asked

Do I actually need a water softener in Kent or Sussex? If the water company's published hardness figure for your postcode is in the “hard” or “very hard” band — common across the chalk and greensand catchment — and you're seeing scale on kettles, shower screens and taps, a softener is a legitimate and well-evidenced fix. It's not compulsory, and plenty of households simply live with descaling the kettle every few months instead.

Do electronic descalers actually work? The evidence is mixed and contested, and considerably weaker than for salt-based softening. Some trials report softer, more easily removed scale; others find no measurable difference. Treat one as an inexpensive experiment you can return under guarantee if it doesn't earn its keep, not a proven substitute for ion exchange.

Will a softener fix a boiler that's already noisy or scaled up? Not on its own. A softener (or an electronic unit) only affects water arriving from that point forward. A boiler or cylinder already suffering reduced flow or kettling from existing scale needs a proper descale or service first — see the boiler service cost guide — with a softener fitted afterwards to stop it recurring.

Is softened water safe to drink? Generally yes for most adults, but it does carry more sodium than the untreated supply, which is worth avoiding for anyone on a medically low-sodium diet or mixing infant formula. Most proper installations leave one kitchen tap plumbed ahead of the softener specifically for drinking and cooking — worth confirming with whoever fits it.
