tanking slurries

A cellar wall with white crystals on it, a tide line of salt halfway up and plaster that comes off in your hand is a survey finding I write up often. The usual next step is a homeowner standing in a builders’ merchant looking at a tub marked “tanking slurry” and wondering whether it is the cheap fix they have been hoping for. Sometimes it is. More often it is half of a fix.

Tanking is the technical word for making a below-ground space watertight from the inside. A cementitious slurry is the simplest version: a cement-based paint, brushed on in coats, that fills the pores of masonry and blocks water passing through. In the language of BS 8102, which covers protection of below-ground structures against water ingress, it is a Type A (barrier) system. It stops water at the wall surface and relies on the wall behind it staying put and holding the pressure.

Below is how I would choose one, six worth looking at, and the part the tubs do not say: what happens to the water and the salts you have just shut in. If your problem is a wet floor rather than a wet wall, the sump pump guide covers the other half of a cellar.

How I judge a tanking slurry

Flexible or rigid. A rigid cement slurry is cheaper and bonds hard, but it cracks if the wall behind it moves even slightly. A polymer-modified two-part coat flexes with small movement. In an old cellar with a few hairline cracks I would go flexible, and keep rigid for sound, stable masonry.

Positive or negative side. Positive-side tanking is applied on the wet face, outside, where water pressure presses the coat onto the wall. Negative-side tanking is applied inside, on the dry face, with water pressing it off. Most homeowner jobs are negative-side, and the datasheet should say whether the product suits that and up to what water pressure. Read that line, not the front of the tub.

Published technical data. Coverage per square metre, number of coats, overcoating time, minimum application temperature and the substrate it needs. A brand that publishes these is telling you how to make it work.

What goes on top. Tanking is a coat, not a finish. It needs a sand and cement render, or a manufacturer’s overcoat, to protect it from knocks and give you something to decorate. Check what your chosen product expects, and budget for the render.

Pack size and mixing. Two-part kits suit someone with a paddle mixer. A one-part bag is easier for one person in a cramped space. Buy a little over the quoted coverage, since rough masonry takes more than the maths says.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Preparation is most of the job. Strip old plaster and paint back to bare masonry, rake out loose pointing and repoint, and brush off salt crystals dry. Fill holes and cracks with a repair mortar, and form a cove at the junction of wall and floor so the coat is continuous round the corner. Manufacturers usually ask for a surface that is clean, sound and damp but not running. A slurry on dusty or painted masonry peels away.

The salts do not go away. Water travelling through old masonry carries ground salts with it, and they stay in the wall. Many are hygroscopic, which means they pull moisture from the air. A slurry stops liquid water at the surface, but a wall already full of salts can still look and feel damp. That is why I tell people to strip the old plaster and not skim over it. Conventional plaster over salted masonry blisters whatever is behind it.

Where the water goes. A tanked wall pushes water pressure back onto the structure. If the cellar floor is not also dealt with, water can find its way up at the base of the wall or through a joint you have not sealed. A full tanking system treats the wall, the floor and the junction as one. If the floor is the weak point, a pump and a drained cavity may be the answer rather than a slurry. See the sump pump guide.

When a membrane system is the honest answer. If the cellar will be lived in, or the wall is wet enough to run, a cavity drain membrane (BS 8102 Type C) is usually better than a barrier coat. It does not try to stop water; it gives it somewhere to go, typically to a sump. It costs more and takes room off the walls, which is why a basement conversion often ends up there. The basement conversion cost guide explains where the money goes.

What the tub says that does not matter. “Waterproof to depth” claims are test figures, not site conditions. “Stops rising damp” is not what a tanking slurry does: it is not a damp-proof course, and a cellar wall has water coming from the surrounding ground, not only up from the footing. For the real treatment options and costs, see rising damp treatment cost and damp proofing cost.

Diagnose before you buy. A damp meter tells you how wet the wall is, and a hygrometer tells you whether the trouble is the air in the cellar rather than the wall. If it is condensation on cold masonry, a coat of cement does nothing, and a dehumidifier and ventilation are the answer. Painting over the problem is the other trap, covered in the damp proof paint guide.

Questions I get asked

Can I tank a cellar myself? A small area of sound masonry, yes, if you prepare it properly and follow the datasheet. A habitable basement or a wall holding back ground water is different: a poorly done job can trap water and push damp somewhere else. For anything you plan to live in, get a specialist design to BS 8102 and ask who guarantees it.

Does tanking slurry stop mould? Not directly. It stops liquid water coming through the wall, which removes one source of moisture. Mould in a cellar often comes from condensation and poor airflow, which a coat of cement does not touch.

How long does a tanking slurry last? There is no honest single figure. A well-prepared coat on a stable wall can last for years. A coat on a wall that moves, or on masonry full of salts, can fail within a season. Any manufacturer’s guarantee usually applies to the product and sometimes only to approved installers.

Can I paint or plaster over a tanking slurry? You can render over it, using the system the manufacturer specifies. Plaster directly onto the cured slurry usually fails without the right bonding coat. Check the datasheet before you start.

If you are not sure whether the problem is the wall, the floor or the air, that is a diagnosis job, not a tub-of-slurry job. A building survey will say what is wet and why, a ventilation assessment looks at the air, and getting in touch is the quickest way to arrange either. For the rest of the kit, there are more field-kit guides.

Small print. This guide is general information, not an inspection of your property, and a tanking slurry does not treat the cause of damp. Always follow the manufacturer’s datasheet and safety instructions; cement-based products are alkaline and irritate skin and eyes.
