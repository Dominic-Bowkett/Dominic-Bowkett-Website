A thermostatic mixing valve sits in the middle of an awkward conflict. Hot water has to be stored at 60°C or more to keep legionella down, and at that temperature it scalds in seconds. The valve blends the hot with cold at the outlet so that the water reaching a bath or basin is a safe 44°C or so. It is a small brass fitting that does two jobs at once, and it is often the thing missing when a landlord has fixed one problem and created the other.

In survey work I see them in three states: fitted and working, fitted and never checked, and not there at all in a house with a young family or an elderly tenant. The standards are simple enough. A TMV2 is approved to BS EN 1111 or BS EN 1287 and is the domestic type. A TMV3 is tested to the NHS D08 specification and belongs in healthcare and care settings. The HSE’s HSG274 guidance then says where a valve should go: as close to the outlet as practical, so there is no long run of warm, stagnant pipe downstream for legionella to colonise.

How I judge a thermostatic mixing valve

TMV2 or TMV3? TMV2 is the domestic approval, BS EN 1111 or 1287. TMV3 is the NHS D08 healthcare approval. Buy the one that matches the building, not the bigger number.

Third-party approval, not just a label. The listing should state the approval scheme and number. Unmarked valves sold as “TMV2 style” are common.

Pipe size and flow. 15mm suits a basin or small shower, 22mm a bath. Every TMV has a minimum flow and pressure to hold its set temperature, and a low-pressure gravity system may not reach it.

Position. HSG274 puts the valve as close to the outlet as practical. A valve in the airing cupboard feeding a long pipe run leaves a warm dead leg, which is the legionella risk the valve was meant to avoid.

Can it be checked? Isolation valves, strainers and check valves make servicing straightforward, and a data plate that survives paint is worth having. A valve you cannot test is a valve nobody tests.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Fit, test, record. A valve is only as good as its installation. It should be set to about 44°C at the outlet, tested with a thermometer once fitted and rechecked at least annually. Keep the record. HSG274 expects TMVs to be serviced, and the maker’s instructions say how often.

It does not replace temperature control at the cylinder. Stored water still needs to be at 60°C or more, with hot water reaching taps at 50°C or more within about a minute, as HSG274 sets out. A TMV is for the last stretch, not a licence to turn the cylinder down. If a boiler job (see the new boiler cost guide) changes the cylinder set point, the valves need rechecking.

Rented property. Landlords have a duty to assess and control the legionella risk in rented homes, though for a typical house with a small system that is usually a short, simple assessment. Mixing valves are one of the things it looks at. The CO alarm and smoke and heat alarm guides cover the other compliance basics, and the first-time landlord kit pulls the list together.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. Chrome finishes, “anti-scald” claims with no standard behind them, and TMV3 on a house that needs a TMV2. Adjustable-looking dials on cheap valves also invite someone to turn the setting up.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a thermostatic mixing valve in a rented house? There is no blanket rule that every let must have one. Approved Document G limits the hot water at a bath to 48°C and a TMV is the usual way to do it, and a legionella or fire risk assessment may recommend them where a young child or vulnerable adult lives. Care settings are a different, stricter case.

What is the difference between TMV2 and TMV3? TMV2 is the domestic approval, to BS EN 1111 or 1287. TMV3 is tested to NHS D08 for healthcare and has faster shut-off and stricter testing. Ordinary homes use TMV2.

Can I fit one myself? The work involves hot and cold supplies, check valves and the Water Supply (Water Fittings) Regulations, so I would use a plumber and ask for the commissioning record. A valve fitted without testing can be worse than none.

How often should a TMV be checked? Manufacturers generally say once a year, with a temperature check and a look at the filters and check valves. In a care setting the schedule is stricter. If the water has drifted from about 44°C, have it serviced or replaced.

Hot water is one of those systems where the fix for one risk can create another, and a mixing valve is a good example. A buy to let survey will note whether valves are present and where, and getting in touch is the way to arrange one. For the rest of the kit, there are more field-kit guides.
