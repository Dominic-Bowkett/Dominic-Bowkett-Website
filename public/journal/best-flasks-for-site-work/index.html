A surveyor’s day does not stop for lunch because the heating is off. A lot of the work happens in unheated lofts, cold crawl spaces and empty properties where the boiler was switched off last month and the kettle went with the last tenant. A full inspection of a Victorian terrace, with the loft, the roof from a ladder, the sub-floor void and a walk round the outside, can easily run to four hours without a sit-down. In January, a hot drink is not a luxury; it is how you keep your hands working well enough to hold a camera and write legibly.

The old answer was to ask the client for a cup of tea. That is fine until the vendor is not there, the house is a probate property with no power, or it is a new-build with the water off. A flask in the van solves it, and the choice is wider than it used to be. Steel-bodied classics, wide-mouth bottles that take ice, and food jars that hold soup are all sold as “flasks”, and they suit different days.

This is a short guide to the ones that earn a place in the van or the bag. It sits in the same part of the kit list as thermal base layers and heated gilets, because a warm drink is part of staying warm. If you are buying for a full winter of site work, those guides are worth reading alongside this one.

How I judge a flask for site work

Heat retention that matches the day. Makers quote 12 to 24 hours hot, and the figures are measured in a lab with a filled, pre-warmed flask. In a cold van the real figure is lower. What matters is that a brew poured at 7am is still properly hot at 1pm. Pre-warming with boiling water for a few minutes before filling makes more difference than any extra claimed hours.

Toughness. A flask in a van lives with ladders, drills and boots. A steel outer shell survives; a thin painted or plastic body dents or cracks. Dents are not a failure, but a damaged vacuum liner means the flask is finished, and the fault is not always obvious until the drink is cold.

Closure and leaks. A screw cup with a push-in stopper is fine for a flask that stands upright in a door pocket. If it goes in a rucksack next to a laptop, you want a leak-resistant lid. Be honest about which you need before you pay for it.

Mouth width. A wide mouth makes soup, ice and cleaning easy. A narrow mouth keeps heat better and pours cleanly. Tea drinkers will be fine with narrow; anyone who wants lunch needs wide.

Weight and size. A full 1L steel flask weighs well over a kilo and a half. That is nothing in a van and plenty in a rucksack you are carrying up a loft ladder. Match the capacity to how many cups you drink and whether you share.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The brand matters less than the vacuum. Stanley has had a surge of attention for its tumblers, and prices of some models have risen accordingly. The classic steel bottle has not changed much in a century. A plain Thermos does the same job for less, so do not pay extra for a colour or a collaboration.

Ignore the “ice for 40 hours” claims. They are measured in a lab with the lid closed. On site, you open the lid ten times a day. Look at the hot figure; it is the one that matters in a cold loft.

Ignore the smart bottle gimmicks. Temperature displays and app connections add cost and a battery that fails, and they do nothing for a brew.

Wash it. Tea stains and old coffee oils build up in a narrow flask, and a flask that smells of last month will taste of it. A bottle brush and a soak with bicarbonate of soda sorts it. A wide mouth makes this easier, which is a quiet argument for the Hydro Flask and the food jars.

Think about lone working. A hot drink in a cold property is not just comfort. If you are alone in an empty house in winter, staying warm keeps you alert, and it is worth pairing a flask with a first aid kit and a charged phone. Cold hands also make it harder to use a damp meter or a camera properly.

Questions I get asked

Is a Stanley flask worth the money over a Thermos? For most people, a Thermos does the job for less. The Stanley is tougher and has a better cup, so it is the one for a van that gets knocked about. If you drop things a lot, the extra money is easier to justify.

How long will a flask keep tea hot on a survey? A good steel vacuum flask, pre-warmed and filled to the top, should keep tea hot for a full morning. By the afternoon it will be warm rather than hot. Makers’ claims of 24 hours are lab figures, so expect less in a cold van.

Can I put soup in a normal flask? You can, but a narrow neck makes it hard to eat and harder to clean. A food jar with a wide mouth is the better tool, and the lid on the Stanley jar doubles as a bowl.

Can I take a flask on a roof or up a ladder? Not in your hand. Leave the flask at the bottom, take a drink at ground level and climb with both hands free. A small flask in a bag pocket is acceptable, but it is better to come down for the break than to climb with a drink.
