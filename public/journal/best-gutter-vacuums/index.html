Nearly every survey that flags penetrating damp or staining below a roofline traces back to a gutter that’s stopped doing its job. Moss and leaf litter build up in the run over a few seasons, water starts overtopping the front edge in anything heavier than a light shower, and it tracks down the brickwork or finds its way into a cavity that nobody looks at properly until the render’s already stained or a damp meter’s reading high on an internal wall below. None of that needs a re-roof or new rainwater goods to fix — it usually just needs the gutter cleared before the next downpour makes the problem worse.

The traditional answer is a ladder against the fascia, or a scaffold tower above single storey, and neither is something to do alone on a Sunday afternoon. A ground-level gutter vacuum answers a narrower but useful question: can the moss and leaf mat be shifted from the ground, through a long pole, without anyone leaving the drive? For a bungalow or straightforward two-storey semi, generally yes, provided the motor and pole are actually matched to the job rather than bought on the biggest number on the box.

How I judge a gutter vacuum

Genuine working reach, not boxed maximum length. A pole kit sold as “24ft” is measured extended straight up with nobody holding the far end. In practice, the last metre or two is unusable once the weight and flex of a fully extended pole are accounted for — buy for the property’s actual gutter height with a margin, not the biggest figure in the listing.

Airflow through a long thin pole, not headline wattage. A 3000W triple-motor rating says as much about running cost as it does about suction at the nozzle end of seven metres of narrow tubing. Litres-per-second airflow figures, where a manufacturer publishes them, are the more honest comparison between kits.

Complete system against pole-only attachment. A pole kit alone is the cheaper route only if a wet/dry vacuum with a compatible hose is already owned. Buying one specifically to pair with a pole kit usually costs more in total than buying a complete kit that already includes the motor.

Weight held overhead, not weight in the box. Aluminium poles are lighter to buy and heavier to hold steady at full extension than carbon fibre. Over a whole roofline that difference is felt in the shoulders before the gutter’s even half clear.

What a fitting mismatch actually costs. Several of the cheaper kits use a US-pattern 2½in shop-vac fitting that doesn’t match a UK vacuum’s 32mm or 38mm hose without an adaptor bought separately — worth checking against the vacuum already owned before assuming a kit will simply push on.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Reach on the box isn’t reach that’s usable. A pole rated at 40ft extended straight up with nobody holding it isn’t the same as 40ft of controllable reach angled over a fascia. Buy for the property’s actual gutter height with a sensible margin, not the biggest number in the listing.

Motor wattage is a running-cost figure as much as a performance one. Airflow, measured in litres per second through the length of pole actually being used, is the number that predicts whether wet moss gets shifted or just smeared further along the run — wattage alone doesn’t tell that story on its own.

A camera is a genuine nice-to-have, not something to pay a premium for on a straightforward semi. A bit of rain and a look from the ground usually confirms a gutter’s flowing again just as well as a live feed does, and the saving over the camera-equipped kit buys most of a second, cheaper tool.

Check the hose fitting against the vacuum already owned before buying a pole-only kit. Several of the cheapest kits use a US 2½in fitting that needs an adaptor for a UK vacuum’s 32mm or 38mm hose — an extra step and an extra part to lose on the day it’s actually needed.

Storage is a real cost that doesn’t show up in the price. An assembled pole kit and an 80-litre tank take up more shed or van space than most of the other tools in this guide — worth weighing against how often the gutters actually need doing before buying the biggest system on offer.

Questions I get asked

Can a gutter vacuum actually replace getting someone up a ladder?

For a straightforward bungalow or two-storey semi with gutters in reasonable condition, generally yes — a matched pole and motor combination will shift moss, leaf litter and loose debris from the ground. What it won’t do is fix a gutter that’s sagged away from its brackets, cracked at a joint, or pulled away from the fascia. Those need eyes and hands at gutter height, which is a job for a ladder, a scaffold tower or a tradesperson, not a vacuum.

How often do gutters actually need clearing?

Once a year for most properties without overhanging trees, closer to twice for anything under mature deciduous cover that drops leaves through autumn. A gutter that’s visibly overflowing during heavy rain, or has moss showing over the front lip from ground level, has already gone past due.

Will a gutter vacuum shift moss and tree seedlings, or just loose leaves?

A genuinely powered kit — 2000W upward, matched to a pole that isn’t too long for the motor to pull air through — will generally shift wet moss and compacted debris, not just dry leaf litter. A pole-only attachment run off a smaller domestic wet/dry vacuum will struggle with anything compacted or waterlogged; that’s the gap between the complete systems and the cheapest attachment kits in this guide.

Can any wet/dry vacuum be used with a pole-only gutter kit?

Only if the hose fitting matches and the motor has enough airflow to pull debris through several metres of narrow pole — a small domestic wet/dry vacuum built for workshop cleanup will often struggle once the hose length and pole height are added together. Check the kit’s stated fitting size against the vacuum’s hose before buying, since several of the cheaper kits use a US 2½in pattern that needs an adaptor for a typical UK vacuum.
