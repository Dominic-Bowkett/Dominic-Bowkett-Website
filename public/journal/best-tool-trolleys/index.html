The best
tool trolleys
for survey work

None of the kit for a domestic survey is individually heavy — a damp meter, a laser measure, a borescope, a torch, a tablet, maybe a ladder bag — but stacked together for a walk from the van to a front door at the end of a long unpaved driveway, or a block with no parking outside it, it adds up to two trips where one would do. A tool trolley solves that specific problem: not van organisation, not stairs, just getting an organised kit from the kerb to wherever the survey actually starts without leaving something behind on the first trip. Here’s the difference between a folding job-site trolley and a static van trolley, and seven worth buying.

There are really two different jobs hiding under one product category, and it’s worth being clear which one is actually being bought for before spending anything. A modular job-site trolley — the wheeled base under a stack of PRO-STACK, TSTAK or PACKOUT boxes — folds down flat, lives behind the seats or against the bulkhead, and only comes out to wheel the whole stack up a path, across a gravel drive or through a communal hallway. A static van trolley is a different animal entirely: two lockable drawers on castors, built more like a small roll cabinet than a folding platform, that stays wedged in the back of the van or a garage and gets rolled out rather than carried in. Both get called a “tool trolley”. Only one of them is meant to leave the vehicle.

The case for either starts the same way: a survey day rarely involves one trip. An EPC or RdSAP assessment on a rural property with a hundred-metre gravel drive and no vehicle access to the door, a retrofit assessment in a third-floor flat where the lift’s out of service, a building survey where the loft hatch, the cellar and the garden boundary are all being checked in one visit — each of those means carrying the full kit somewhere the van can’t follow, more than once, without a convenient spot to leave the first load while the second one goes back. A trolley that folds flat for the drive over and unfolds to take the weight in one go turns that into a single walk instead of three.

How I judge a tool trolley

Folds flat for the van, not just wheels well once it’s out. A trolley that’s brilliant crossing a gravel drive but won’t collapse small enough to fit behind the seats just becomes permanent van clutter. The fold matters as much as the roll.

Wheel size and type for real ground. A block-paved drive, a cracked path, a kerb up to a Victorian terrace’s front step — small hard castors built for a workshop floor catch and tip on all of it. Bigger, softer-tyred wheels cope with the ground a survey actually happens on.

Modularity is a lock-in decision, not just a feature. Buying into Pro-Stack, TSTAK or PACKOUT means every future storage box has to match the system already owned. Worth deciding deliberately rather than by accident.

Load capacity against what’s actually being carried. Most survey kit is genuinely light — instruments, not power tools. A 100kg rating is rarely the limiting factor; stability on a slope with a top-heavy stack is the more common failure.

Lockable, if it’s doubling as static storage. A trolley that stays in the van overnight on a residential street needs a lid that locks. A trolley that only ever comes out for the walk from kerb to front door doesn’t need to bother.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Folding matters more than the load rating. Every trolley in this guide is rated well beyond what a survey kit weighs. The number worth checking is the folded size — whether it actually fits the van once it’s not in use, not the 100kg figure on the box.

Modularity is a decision about every future purchase, not just this one. Buying a Pro-Stack, TSTAK or PACKOUT trolley means the next storage box, and the one after that, needs to match. Worth choosing the system deliberately rather than picking whichever trolley happened to be cheapest that week.

Wheel size beats wheel count. Four small castors sound sturdier than two big wheels, but it’s the diameter and tyre material that decide whether a trolley catches on a kerb or a broken flag, not how many there are.

A lockable lid only matters if the trolley lives in the van overnight. For something that only ever comes out for the walk from kerb to door and goes straight back in a locked vehicle, it’s a feature that adds cost without adding much.

A trolley isn’t a substitute for a sack truck on stairs. Wheels help across a driveway or a flat hallway; they do nothing on a communal staircase with no lift. That’s a job for a dedicated stair-climbing sack truck, not a trolley with castors.

A trolley isn’t van organisation either. It solves getting kit from the van to the door, not keeping the van itself tidy between jobs — that’s a separate problem, covered in the tool boxes and van storage guide.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a tool trolley if the van’s already organised?

Organised van storage and a tool trolley solve two different problems. A well-organised van means knowing which box the borescope’s in without digging around in the dark; a trolley means getting that box, and several others, from the van to the survey without three separate trips. A van can be immaculately organised and still leave someone carrying a tripod, a tool bag and a moisture meter case in one go down a hundred-metre gravel drive. The two are complementary, not alternatives.

What’s the difference between a tool trolley and a sack truck?

A sack truck — particularly a dedicated stair-climbing one — is built to move a genuinely heavy or awkward load up and down steps, using a cluster of small wheels that walk step to step. A tool trolley is built for flat or gently sloped ground: a driveway, a car park, a hallway. Taking a folding job-site trolley up a flight of stairs means lifting and carrying it, wheels and all, which defeats the purpose. For a stairwell with no lift, a stair-climbing sack truck is the right tool; for getting a kit from the kerb to a ground-floor front door, a trolley is faster and less to carry.

Will a tool trolley cope with a gravel drive or a broken driveway?

It depends almost entirely on wheel size and tyre material, not the trolley’s branding or price. Small, hard plastic castors — the kind fitted to budget wheeled boxes — catch on loose gravel and cracked paving and need lifting over the worst of it. Larger wheels with a softer tyre, like the air-filled rears on the DeWalt or the 228mm all-terrain wheels on the Milwaukee PACKOUT trolley, roll over the same ground without much drama. Worth checking wheel diameter specifically if the usual walk to a front door isn’t a smooth driveway.

Can a tool trolley double as static van storage?

The static-style trolleys can — the Sealey and Draper in this guide are built to live permanently in a van or garage, with lockable drawers doing the organising job a toolbox would otherwise do, and castors mainly there for repositioning within the van rather than travelling any distance. A folding modular trolley like the Stanley or DeWalt is the wrong tool for that job: it’s built to carry a stack of boxes a short distance and then get out of the way, not to sit loaded as a permanent fixture.
