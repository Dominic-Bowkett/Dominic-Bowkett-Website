Half of an invasive inspection is getting in without leaving a mess. Lifting floorboards, opening up a boxed-in pipe run, pulling a loft ladder down over a pale landing carpet: each of those puts dust, grit or something worse where the client will see it after you have gone. A dust sheet costs less than a sandwich and is the cheapest way to look careful.

They are not all the same thing. A polythene sheet is waterproof and slippery. A plain cotton sheet grips and absorbs but lets a spill through. A polythene-backed cotton twill does both jobs reasonably, which is why it is the trade standard. Then there is the runner for stairs, and the throwaway sheet for jobs that leave soot or droppings behind.

The ranking below is built from manufacturer descriptions and published user feedback. The practical rule is simple: one backed sheet for floors you walk on, one cheap polythene for furniture and dirty work, and a runner if your jobs involve stairs.

How I judge a dust sheet

Backing. Cotton alone lets liquid through. If there is any chance of a spill, whether a bucket, a pipe you have just disturbed or a flask, you want a polythene layer underneath. Plastic alone is fine for covering furniture, not for floors.

Grip underfoot. A sheet that slides on laminate or tile is a trip hazard for you and the client. Cotton on top is the safer surface for walking and for a ladder foot; loose polythene on a hard floor is not.

Size and weight. The sheet has to fit in the van with everything else, so heavy canvas is rarely worth the space. A 12ft x 9ft class sheet covers most of the room you are working in; anything larger is awkward to lay in a hallway.

Cleanability and disposal. Fabric sheets can be shaken out; polythene is thrown away. On a dirty job, disposable wins because it does not carry the dirt to the next client’s house.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Matters: a waterproof backing on anything that goes on a carpet or good floor; a grippy top face; and a stair runner if the loft is up two flights.

Doesn’t: fabric weights quoted in grams are rarely comparable between brands, and colour is irrelevant. Heavy canvas sold as “trade” is tougher than most inspection work needs, and a pain to carry.

Taping. Whatever you buy, a roll of low-tack masking tape to pin the edges on a stair or doorway does more for safety than the sheet itself. A sheet that rucks under a ladder is a hazard.

Questions I get asked

What is the best dust sheet for protecting carpets? A polythene-backed cotton twill or cotton mix. The cotton grips and takes up a drip, and the backing stops it going through to the carpet. Plain polythene slides about on carpet and plain cotton lets spills through.

Are polythene dust sheets any good? For covering furniture and for one-off dirty work, yes: waterproof, light and cheap. They are poor to walk on, tear easily and are slippery on hard floors, so do not use them as a floor protector in a hallway.

How big a dust sheet do I need? A 3.7 x 2.7m sheet (roughly 12ft x 9ft) covers most of a small room or a landing. For stairs use a dedicated runner, around 7m long and 0.9m wide.

Can I wash a cotton dust sheet? Fabric sheets can usually be shaken out and laundered, but a polythene backing may crack or peel in a hot wash. Check the label; for a plaster-dusty sheet, shaking it out outdoors is usually enough.
