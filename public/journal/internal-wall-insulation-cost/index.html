Internal wall
insulation cost
in 2026

Internal wall insulation typically costs £60–£120 per m² and £6,000–£15,000 for a 3-bed solid-wall house. The price is the easy part; keeping the old wall dry behind it is the job.

Published 14 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Insulating solid walls from the inside typically costs £60–£120 per m² installed, £1,500–£3,500 for a single room and £6,000–£15,000 for a whole three-bedroom house. Those are 2026 figures, and the South East sits toward the top of each range.

But the quote is the least risky part of the job. Internal insulation makes the original wall colder, and a colder wall is where moisture condenses. Fitted well, on a dry wall, it works. Fitted badly, it can create the damp problem it was meant to help with.

I am a building surveyor and retrofit assessor. I do not fit insulation, so I have no stake in whether you buy it. This is what the 2026 figures say, what drives them, and what I would settle before you sign.

The short answer

Prices were checked on 14 October 2026. BookaBuilder, Trade2Base (published 14 June 2026) and The Eco Experts all publish 2026 guides, and their per-m² bands overlap closely even where whole-house totals differ. The spread in totals is real: it depends on how many external walls you treat and how much has to be moved and made good.

Whatever the headline, a quote below about £50 per m² for a fully finished stud system should make you ask what has been left out.

What drives the price

The system. Insulated plasterboard (a board bonded to insulation, fixed straight to the wall) is the cheapest route at roughly £50–£90 per m² supplied and fitted. A timber or metal stud frame with insulation, boarding and skim runs about £70–£120. Rigid PIR, phenolic and EPS are cheapest; wood fibre and mineral wool cost more and move moisture better.

The extras. These are where quotes quietly grow. Window and door reveals need their own insulation, around £50–£100 an opening. Radiators have to come off the wall and go back out into the room, around £200–£500 a room. Sockets and switches have to be extended, roughly £300–£800 across a house, and skirtings, architraves and cornices come off and go back.

Making good. The finish is a skim and decoration, so budget for painting every treated room afterwards.

Disruption. It is done room by room, with the room emptied. Most people stay put, but a kitchen or bathroom on an external wall is a bigger job than a bedroom.

Where you live. Kent, Sussex and Surrey labour rates sit above the national figures, so I would plan on the upper half of each band.

What it takes from the room

A typical system takes 60–100mm off every wall you treat. One supplier’s guidance puts 75mm of PIR on all four walls of a 4m × 5m room at a loss of about 1.2m², from 20m² to roughly 18.8m². In a small Victorian bedroom that matters, and it is why many people treat only the external walls and leave the party walls alone.

Thinner wood fibre systems exist: one supplier lists 40mm of board plus 25mm of batten and insulation for a U-value of about 0.46 W/m²K. Thinner means less loss and a smaller improvement.

Do you actually need it?

Internal insulation is the fallback for solid walls when external insulation is not an option, usually because the front elevation is brick you want to keep, or you are in a terrace, a conservation area or a listed building. If you can use the external route, compare it with my external wall insulation cost guide, which covers the render failures that have hit government-funded work. If your walls turn out to have a cavity, cavity wall insulation is far cheaper and I would look at that first.

On payback, be realistic. The Energy Saving Trust puts savings at around £300–£500 a year for a semi-detached home with its solid walls insulated, and another guide works out a break-even of just under 22 years for a typical three-bedroom house. That is a long time for a measure that cannot easily be undone, so I would justify it on comfort, on fixing a cold room, or on doing it while you are redecorating anyway, rather than on bills alone.

In a listed building, the picture is harder. Local authority guidance notes that there are unlikely to be many opportunities for internal wall insulation in historic buildings, and permanently removing historic features such as skirting boards is not regarded as acceptable. You will need consent for most of it, so read my listed building consent guide first.

The real risk: moisture behind the boards

An old solid wall stays dry partly by drying out into the room and the air. Insulate it from inside, and the masonry behind the boards becomes colder and has fewer ways to dry. Warm, moist room air that gets through to that cold face can condense there, which is interstitial condensation. It is out of sight, which is the problem: you learn about it from mould on the surface, or from rot in the joist ends that bear into the wall.

The risks are condensation at the junction between masonry and insulation, especially at joist ends, cold bridges where the insulation stops at floors, party walls and reveals, and a vapour control layer that has been fitted in the wrong place. Bath and North East Somerset’s retrofit guidance recommends carrying insulation partly across the party walls to reduce thermal bridging, and breathable paints and finishes so the wall can keep drying. It also discourages cement-based products.

The point I make to every client is that insulation does not treat damp. If the wall is wet, because of a leaking gutter, a bridged damp course or failed pointing, insulating it can hide the problem or worsen it. Settle the cause first. My damp survey cost guide covers what a diagnosis costs, and rising damp treatment is worth reading before anyone sells you a chemical fix.

Under PAS 2035, internal insulation of a solid wall is classed as a higher-risk measure, so on a funded job a retrofit coordinator should design and oversee it, and a condensation risk assessment is part of the design. That is a good standard to ask for on a privately funded job too. My retrofit assessment cost guide explains what that assessment costs.

Grants and VAT in 2026

Installations of insulation in homes are currently zero-rated for VAT until 31 March 2027, after which the rate reverts to 5%. That relief applies when materials and installation are supplied together by a VAT-registered contractor, so check your quote shows it. ECO4 funding is quoted as running to the end of 2026 for those on qualifying benefits, with the Great British Insulation Scheme no longer a route for new applicants. Funded jobs go through a PAS 2035 assessment rather than a single-measure quote.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • A moisture survey first. A firm that quotes without checking the wall is dry, and why, is selling boards, not a solution.
  • A named system and thickness. “Insulation and plastering” is not a specification.
  • Vapour control and ventilation. Ask where the vapour control layer goes and how the rooms will be ventilated afterwards. A new extract fan can be £150–£300 (see my extractor fan guide).
  • Junction detailing. Reveals, floors and the return along party walls should be on the spec, not left to the day.
  • Extras itemised. Radiators, electrics, skirtings and decoration priced separately, so you can compare three quotes like for like.
  • Written quotes. At least three, and check accreditation (TrustMark, and PAS 2035 compliance for anything grant-funded).

Questions I get asked

How much does internal wall insulation cost? Typically £60–£120 per m² installed, £1,500–£3,500 for a single room and £6,000–£15,000 for a whole 3-bed solid-wall house. Radiator moves, electrics, window reveals and redecoration push it toward the top.

How much room does it take up? A typical system takes about 60–100mm off each treated wall. One source puts 75mm of PIR on all four walls of a 4m × 5m room at a loss of roughly 1.2m² of floor.

Is it worth it? Sometimes. Savings of £300–£500 a year for a semi against £6,000–£15,000 means a long payback, one guide says just under 22 years. It earns its place mainly when you are redecorating anyway, or want one cold room fixed.

Can it cause damp? Yes, if it goes over a wall that is already damp or without a moisture strategy. Insulation makes the old wall colder, so moisture can condense behind the boards, especially at joist ends. It does not cure damp and can hide it.
