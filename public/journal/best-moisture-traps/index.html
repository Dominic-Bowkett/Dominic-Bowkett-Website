The best
moisture traps,
for cupboards, wardrobes and caravans

A moisture trap is a pot of crystals doing a genuinely useful job in a genuinely small space — and a fairly useless one anywhere air is being replaced. Here’s what these passive absorbers actually do, where that stops, and the seven worth having in a cupboard, a wardrobe or a caravan locker.

A moisture trap comes up in roughly the same conversation every time: a client opens a wardrobe against an external wall, or a static caravan that’s stood empty since October, and there’s a faint smell and a film of grey fuzz on a shoe. Nobody’s asking for a survey at that point — they want to know if the pot of white crystals from the supermarket actually does anything, or if it’s just an expensive way to feel like you’re doing something about it.

What’s inside is calcium chloride, sometimes blended with other salts — a hygroscopic material that pulls water vapour out of the air around it and turns it into a saline liquid that drips or pools into a tank below. No electricity, no moving parts, nothing to plug in. It works, within a genuinely small volume: a wardrobe, a cupboard under the stairs, the cabin of a caravan sealed up for winter. Ask it to dry out a bedroom with people breathing and showering in it, and it loses that fight quietly, every time.

That’s the distinction worth making before you buy one. A moisture trap treats air that’s already trapped — it has nothing to say about rising damp, a leaking gutter, or a bedroom where the extract fan hasn’t worked in years; those need proper diagnosis, not a pot of crystals. What it earns its keep doing is the smaller, unglamorous jobs: keeping condensation off tools in an unheated shed, stopping a caravan smelling of a wet dog by Easter, or buying a wardrobe against a cold gable wall a few more months before proper insulation work sorts the actual cause. Here’s what’s on the market and which ones are worth the space they take up.

How I judge a moisture trap

Real capacity against the claim on the box. “Removes up to a litre of moisture” is doing a lot of work on packaging that doesn’t say over what time, in what size space, or starting from what humidity. What matters in practice is the tank or bag size in front of you — bigger tank, fewer trips to empty it.

Spill-proofing. These things sit unattended in a wardrobe, a caravan locker or the back of a van for weeks at a time. A loose lid or an open pot that tips over turns a damp problem into a stained-carpet problem. A grille, a sealed base and a level window matter more than the marketing photo suggests.

Refill economics. A cheap device with expensive, hard-to-find refills costs more over a year than a pricier one with refills on every supermarket shelf. Work out the cost per month, not the price on the box.

Matched to the space. A 500ml tub is wasted in a shoe cupboard and hopeless in a caravan cabin. Hanging bags suit a wardrobe rail; a sealed device with a bigger tank suits a static caravan or boat cabin closed up for months; a reusable bag suits somewhere you’ll remember to check every few weeks.

An honest signal that it’s spent. A level window, a colour-change indicator or a bag that visibly swells tells you when to act. Without one, you’re relying on memory — and crystal that’s fully saturated stops absorbing anything at all.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A moisture trap treats a sealed volume, not a room. This is the whole honest-limits point. In a wardrobe, a caravan cabin closed up for winter, or a car boot, the air isn’t being replaced — whatever moisture is in there stays until something absorbs it or a door opens. In a bedroom or bathroom people actually live in, air is being replaced constantly through gaps, trickle vents and extract fans, and a moisture trap is competing against that turnover and losing. Condensation on a bedroom window is a ventilation problem, not a moisture-trap problem — see the guide to anemometers for ventilation testing for how that actually gets measured.

It has nothing to say about rising or penetrating damp. If a wall’s wet because of a failed damp-proof course, a blocked gutter or a render defect letting water in, no amount of crystals in a nearby pot will dry it out — the water’s coming from outside the room, not condensing out of the air inside it. A damp meter reading against the skirting tells you which problem you actually have before you spend money on either fix.

The box’s coverage claim assumes a sealed room. “Absorbs up to a litre” or “covers 20m²” is measured, reasonably, in a closed test chamber — not a room with a door that opens six times a day and a trickle vent left cracked. Treat the stated coverage as a best-case ceiling, not an expectation.

What the marketing pushes that doesn’t matter much. Scent options, colour ranges and design-led shells are decoration once the crystal quantity and tank size are sorted — the Absodry and the Osmo both look better on a shelf than a supermarket own-brand tub, and that’s a legitimate reason to buy one, just not a functional one.

A quiet caravan or boat over winter is the genuine sweet spot. Closed up for months with no heating and no ventilation running, that’s precisely the sealed, cold, humid volume a passive absorber is built for — which is exactly why so many of the products on this list market themselves that way.

Questions I get asked

Do moisture traps actually work? Within a small, enclosed, unventilated space — yes, genuinely; the liquid collecting in the tank after a few weeks is real evidence of water pulled out of the air. In a room with normal ventilation and air changes, the effect is far smaller than the packaging suggests, because fresh, more humid air keeps replacing whatever’s just been dried.

Will a moisture trap stop mould in a caravan or wardrobe? It reduces the humidity that mould needs to establish, which helps, but it won’t remove mould that’s already growing, and it won’t fix a leak or a cold bridge feeding moisture in faster than the trap can pull it out. Treat it as prevention for a space you’re about to close up, not a cure for one that’s already gone black.

Moisture trap or electric dehumidifier — which do I actually need? For a room people live in — a bedroom with condensation on the windows, a bathroom without an extract fan doing its job — an electric dehumidifier, or better, fixing the ventilation, is the right tool; see the guide to dehumidifiers. A moisture trap is for the spaces an electric dehumidifier can’t reach or isn’t worth running: a wardrobe, a caravan locker, an unheated shed.

How often does the refill actually need changing? Ignore the stated “up to 90 days” figure and watch the level window or indicator instead — a cold, humid space in winter will saturate a tab in weeks, while the same device in a dry summer cupboard might genuinely run the full quarter. A hygrometer left in the same space tells you whether it’s still working faster than the level window will.
