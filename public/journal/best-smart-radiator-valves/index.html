The best
smart radiator valves
for room-by-room heating

A fair few of the EPCs and retrofit assessments I do turn up the same heating controls line: one thermostat for the whole house, radiators either fully open or shut by hand, and a household paying to heat rooms nobody's in. A smart radiator valve replaces the manual head on an existing TRV and gives each room its own schedule from a phone. It's a genuine fix for that specific waste — and, on a heat pump especially, it can just as easily cause a different problem if it's the only control fitted. Here's how I'd judge one, and the seven I'd look at.

A thermostatic radiator valve has always done one job — close off a radiator once its own local sensor reaches the set temperature. A smart version adds a motor, a battery and an app on top of that same mechanism, trading a dial you turn by hand for a schedule, a geofence, and a reading of what each room is actually doing. In a house with one thermostat running every radiator at once, that's real money: a spare room heated to living-room temperature all day, because there's no other way to turn it down.

It's a heating-cost and comfort product, not a damp or ventilation one, and I'd be wary of buying it expecting the second thing. A smart TRV can stop a room going stone cold when it's empty, which helps condensation risk at the margin, but it does nothing about moisture generated elsewhere in the house or a cold bridge in the fabric. That's a ventilation and insulation question, covered in my PIV units guide and dehumidifiers guide, not a heating-controls one.

The other honest caveat, and the one most affiliate roundups skip: on a heat pump, several smart TRVs closing at once can do more harm than good. More on that below, after the seven valves I'd actually look at.

How I judge a smart radiator valve

Hub-free or genuinely hub-locked. A Thread or Matter valve can run through a border router you may already own; several others flatly won't function without their own proprietary hub, which is an extra £30–£90 that rarely gets mentioned in the headline price.

Geofencing that actually adjusts the temperature. Some apps only alert you that you've left the heating on; genuine automatic geofencing turns it down itself. Worth checking which behaviour you're getting before assuming the feature exists.

What valve body it actually fits. Almost everything here is built around the M30x1.5mm thread standard, but a large share of UK radiators — especially anything fitted with Danfoss RA, RAV or RAVL valves — needs an adapter. Some brands ship a full adapter set in the box; others officially support M30x1.5 only and leave you sourcing your own.

Heat pump readiness, not just a compatibility badge. A brand stating it "works with heat pumps" isn't the same as a system designed so it won't restrict flow when several valves close together. Ask specifically how it handles that, not whether it pairs with the app.

Battery type and realistic life. Rechargeable USB-C cells save a yearly trip round every radiator with a screwdriver and a battery pack; plain AA cells are cheaper to replace but that trip still has to happen, typically once a year.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

On a heat pump, more zoning isn't automatically better. A boiler blasts short bursts of high-temperature water, so a few closed TRVs barely register. A heat pump runs continuously at a low flow temperature through a large water volume; several smart valves closing at once restricts that flow, which can force the flow temperature up against the efficiency the heat pump exists for, or cause pipe noise as it pushes water through mostly-shut valves. Trustworthy installers generally recommend a buffer tank sized to the heat pump's output, or leaving roughly a quarter of the radiators permanently unrestricted. If you're weighing a heat pump alongside smart controls, that sizing question belongs in the same conversation — see what an air source heat pump costs.

They're a heating-cost fix, not a damp fix. Keeping a spare room from going stone cold helps at the margin, but a smart TRV does nothing about moisture generated by cooking, showers or drying laundry indoors, and nothing about a genuinely cold-bridged wall or window. If that's the actual complaint, it needs a ventilation or fabric answer, not a heating-controls one.

Treat the manufacturer savings percentage with real scepticism. Headline figures around 20–25% are common in this category's marketing, calculated against a household that currently heats empty rooms and leaves the heating running when out. A household that already uses a decent programmer and turns radiators down by hand is realistically looking at something more modest, and sometimes much less.

Check the valve body before ordering, not after. Most UK TRVs are the M30x1.5mm standard, but a lot of older housing stock — particularly 1980s to 2000s builds — runs Danfoss RA, RAV or RAVL valves needing a specific adapter. Some brands ship a full set in the box; Wiser notably doesn't guarantee support beyond M30x1.5. Unscrew the existing head and check the fitting before you buy.

A new boiler with OpenTherm does more for genuine efficiency than the valves alone. Weather compensation — a boiler modulating its own flow temperature against outdoor conditions — and room-by-room zoning are solving different problems, and the biggest efficiency gains tend to come from getting the boiler side right first. See what a new boiler costs if that's the piece still missing.

What the marketing pushes that matters less than it sounds. A vendor's own "AI learning" claim is a headline feature, not a measured one. A colour display is genuinely nice to have but doesn't change how well the valve heats a room. None of it substitutes for hub cost, valve-adapter compatibility and whether the geofencing is actually automatic.

Questions I get asked

Will smart radiator valves fix the condensation in my bedroom? Not directly. They can stop a room being left stone cold, which reduces one condensation risk factor at the margin, but the moisture itself comes from cooking, showering, drying laundry and everyday breathing, and needs proper ventilation to get out. If that's the actual problem, look at extract ventilation or a PIV unit rather than heating controls.

Do smart TRVs work with a heat pump? They can, but "compatible" isn't the same as "won't cause a problem." The risk is several valves closing together and restricting system flow. Ask specifically how a system handles that — a buffer tank, a set of permanently unrestricted radiators, or software that manages it — rather than trusting a general compatibility badge.

Will I really save 20–25% on heating, like the adverts say? Only if your starting point is heating empty rooms and leaving the heating on when you're out. If you already use a decent programmer and turn radiators down by hand, expect a more modest saving, and budget the hub cost most brands don't put in the headline price.

Do I need a hub, and will one fit my existing radiators? Wiser, Aqara and Hive won't function at all without their own hub, adding to the real cost; Tado, Eve and the Matter version of Bosch can run hub-free through a Thread border router you may already own. Separately, check your radiator's existing valve body: most are M30x1.5mm, but Danfoss RA/RAV/RAVL valves need a specific adapter not every brand includes.

I don’t specify heating controls as part of a survey, but they show up on an EPC as a standard low-cost recommendation, and heat pump readiness is exactly what a PAS 2035 retrofit assessment is built to answer before money goes on either the valves or the heat pump. If condensation rather than a heating bill brought you here, that's a different fix — see PIV units and dehumidifiers instead.
