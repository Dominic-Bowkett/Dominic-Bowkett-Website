Measured building
survey cost
in 2026

A measured building survey typically costs £350–£2,500 in 2026, depending on the size of the property and the drawings you need. It isn’t the same thing as a condition survey — and mixing the two up is the mistake I see most often.

Published 27 Aug 2026 Reading time · 9 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A measured building survey typically costs £350–£2,500 in 2026. A flat or small terrace scoped for simple floor plans sits at the bottom of that range; a larger detached or period house wanting full elevations, sections and a roof plan sits near the top; and a listed building with awkward, non-standard geometry can push past it altogether. Size and the level of drawing detail you ask for do almost all the work in deciding where you land.

The bit that actually trips people up isn't the price, though. It's the word “survey” itself, which in this country gets used for two entirely different products. One records what a building looks like — its dimensions, its layout, its geometry — and hands that over as scaled drawings for an architect or a planning application. The other assesses what condition it's in — damp, movement, defects, what needs fixing — and hands that over as a report you'd use before buying or renovating. Both get called “a survey”. They have almost nothing else in common.

I do the second kind, not the first — I diagnose condition, I don't produce scaled drawings for an architect's design work, so I've no reason to talk you into a measured survey you don't need or wave you off one you do. What follows is 2026 pricing cross-checked against current UK measured-survey providers, what actually drives the fee, and a straight answer on which of the two “surveys” you're actually after.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 27 August 2026 against current 2026 pricing from UK measured-survey and draughting practices, cross-referenced against each other. Most are quoted as a fixed fee once your surveyor knows the size and the deliverables you need, not an hourly rate. As with the rest of this series, London and the South East, Dom's patch included, trend 10–25% above the rest of the UK.

Those figures are for standard 2D CAD drawings. Ask for a 3D or BIM model on top — increasingly requested by architects working in Revit — and the fee can rise substantially, sometimes several times the 2D-only price depending on the level of detail. A separate topographical survey, recording ground levels and site boundaries rather than the building itself, is a different product again and is usually quoted and commissioned separately.

What drives the price

Size, but complexity more than raw floor area. A large open-plan barn conversion can be quicker to measure than a small Victorian terrace chopped into a warren of little rooms with non-square walls.

What you actually ask for. Floor plans alone cost less than floor plans plus elevations, sections and a roof plan — agree the deliverables before you get a quote, because “a measured survey” means different things to different practices.

Survey method. A 3D laser scanner has a higher day rate than a tape measure or total station, but on a complex or larger building it often works out cheaper overall because less time is spent back in the office reconciling manual measurements.

Access and occupation. A furnished, lived-in house takes longer to measure around than an empty shell, and a tenanted or commercial building may need access arranged in advance.

Location. London and the South East commonly sit 10–25% above the rest of the UK, in line with most trades in this series.

Turnaround. An expedited drawing set, needed for an urgent planning deadline, typically carries a premium over standard delivery.

A measured survey isn't a condition survey

This is worth being precise about, because the confusion is genuinely common and genuinely costly if it goes the wrong way. A measured building survey captures geometry — how big the building is, how it's laid out, what it looks like — using a laser scanner, total station or tape measure, then turns that into scaled CAD or BIM drawings. It says nothing at all about whether the roof is sound, whether there's damp in the walls, or whether the foundations have moved. It isn't a valuation, and it doesn't settle a boundary dispute either — it draws the building as it stands, not where the legal line between you and next door actually falls.

A building survey — what most people mean when they say “I'm having a survey done” before buying or renovating a house, and what I do for a living — is the opposite. I'm not measuring up for an architect's drawing set; I'm assessing condition: is the roof covering near the end of its life, is that crack in the render structural or cosmetic, is the damp meter reading rising damp or condensation. Two genuinely different jobs, done by different people, priced differently, and neither one substitutes for the other. If you've had a measured survey done for planning and think that covers you on condition too, it doesn't — and the reverse is equally true.

Who actually needs one

Architects and designers preparing a loft conversion or extension drawing set almost always need an accurate measured survey of the existing building before proposed drawings can start, particularly if the property's existing plans are old, missing, or don't match what's actually been built over the years — which, on an older house, is more often than not. Planning applications typically require existing and proposed floor plans and elevations submitted side by side, so the measured survey is often the first paid step in a project, well before a builder's quote is on the table.

Beyond that: interior designers laying out a refurbishment, developers assessing a conversion, and owners of listed buildings needing accurate drawings to support a listed building consent application all commission these routinely. What doesn't need one is straightforward due diligence before buying a house — that's a house survey assessing condition, not a measured survey recording dimensions, and paying for the wrong one wastes money without answering the question you actually had.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Deliverables agreed in writing before instruction — floor plans, elevations, sections, roof plan: state exactly which ones you need, because scope creep on drawings is easy to argue about after the fact.
  • Fixed fee, not an open-ended hourly rate — most reputable providers quote fixed once they know size and deliverables; be wary of one that won't commit to a figure upfront.
  • File format confirmed — check your architect or designer can actually open what you're sent (DWG, RVT, PDF), not just that drawings exist.
  • Survey method disclosed — laser scan, total station or tape, and the accuracy tolerance that comes with it, particularly if the drawings will feed into structural calculations.
  • Heritage detail included or extra — on a listed building, ask explicitly whether mouldings, cornicing and other period detail are covered in the quoted fee.

Questions I get asked

How much does a measured building survey cost in 2026? For a flat or small terrace, roughly £350–£800. An average 2-3 bed semi or terrace typically runs £800–£1,500, rising to £1,500–£2,500 for a larger detached or period house, and £1,200–£5,500 or more for a listed or heritage building. A 3D or BIM model on top of standard 2D drawings adds further cost.

What's the difference between a measured survey and a building survey? A measured survey records geometry — scaled floor plans, elevations, sections. A building survey, or condition survey, assesses the fabric: damp, movement, defects, what needs fixing. They answer different questions and one doesn't substitute for the other.

Do I need a measured survey for a loft conversion or extension? Usually, yes. Most architects won't start proposed drawings without an accurate existing survey first, and planning applications typically need existing and proposed drawings submitted together.

Can a measured survey replace a condition survey when I'm buying a house? No. It only records dimensions and layout, not condition. If you want to know what you're taking on before you buy, that's a Home Survey Level 2 or Level 3, not a measured survey.
