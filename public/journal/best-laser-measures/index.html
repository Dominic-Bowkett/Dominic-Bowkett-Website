An RdSAP assessment lives or dies on floor area. Get the measurements wrong and everything downstream — the heat demand, the rating, the recommendations — is wrong with them. So the laser measure is not an accessory in the survey bag; it is the bag. It gets used in every room of every house I visit: ceiling heights in loft conversions, external runs down the side of terraces, room dimensions taken from the doorway while the owner’s dog investigates my boots.

The good news is that this is a mature product category. The measuring engine inside a £30 unit and a £500 unit is doing broadly the same job, and both will tell you a bedroom is 3.42 m wide. What separates them is everything around the measurement: whether you can read the screen in a bright conservatory, whether the thing survives being dropped from a loft hatch, whether it will fire 25 m down the outside of a building and find its target, and whether the numbers get into your floor-plan software without being typed in twice.

What follows is a features comparison built from manufacturers’ specifications and the long tail of published user feedback, read with a surveyor’s eye for what the job actually demands. The list runs from a £25 unit I’d happily recommend to a homeowner up to the DISTO that surveyors reach for when the job gets awkward.

How I judge a laser measure

Accuracy where it matters. Almost everything on the market claims ±1.5–3 mm. For floor plans, any of those numbers is fine — RdSAP conventions round to two decimal places and a house is not built to millimetre tolerances anyway. What matters more is repeatability: fire three shots at the same wall and a good unit gives the same answer three times. Cheap ones sometimes don’t, especially at range or against dark surfaces.

Continuous measure and a proper reference edge. Continuous (tracking) mode — where the reading updates live as you move — is how you find the true perpendicular distance in a room that isn’t square, which is most rooms built before 1950. And the unit must let you measure from its back edge, front edge, or a flip-out end-piece hooked over a reveal. This is used constantly.

The calculations that earn their keep. Area and volume, addition and subtraction, and a Pythagoras function for heights you can’t reach — a gable peak, a ridge line from the pavement. Anything beyond that is mostly menu clutter.

Bluetooth that actually connects. If you draw floor plans on a tablet — magicplan, CubiCasa, Leica’s own DISTO Plan, Bosch MeasureOn — a measure that pushes each shot straight into the drawing saves real time and removes transcription errors. But only if the connection is reliable. Leica and Bosch have this right; most budget brands either don’t offer it or offer it badly.

Survivability. Loft hatches, ladder work, wet Februaries. An IP rating and a rubber overmould are worth more than an extra 50 m of range you’ll never use.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Range is the most oversold number in the category. A 100 m or 150 m rating is achieved in laboratory conditions against a reflective target. Indoors, in a house, the longest shot you will realistically take is the length of a hallway or an open-plan ground floor — call it 15 m. Even measuring external elevations rarely goes past 30 m. Paying extra for range alone is paying for a number on a box.

Accuracy claims are ISO-condition figures. ±1.5 mm assumes a co-operative target at moderate distance in reasonable light. Real houses have dark feature walls that absorb the beam, gloss tiles and mirrors that scatter it, and net curtains that catch it early. The practical skill is knowing when to distrust a reading — if a room comes back 6.8 m and your eye says five, the laser has gone through a window. Good units fail loudly (error message); cheap ones sometimes fail quietly (a confident wrong number).

The tilt sensor is quietly the most useful “extra”. Smart-horizontal mode — measuring over a parked car or a hedge and letting the unit trigonometrise the true horizontal distance — is the feature I’d rank first after plain distance. Conversely, the two-shot and three-shot Pythagoras heights are only as good as your pivot discipline: both shots must be taken from exactly the same point, which is harder than the manual implies.

Bluetooth is either essential or irrelevant. If you draw plans digitally — and every RdSAP assessor increasingly does, since the software wants floor areas and the auditors want evidence — measure-to-app transfer is worth real money. If you don’t, it’s a battery drain you’ll switch off. Decide which workflow you run before you pay for it.

What the salesmen push that you can skip: painter’s area modes, stake-out functions (that’s setting-out kit, not survey kit), “199 measurement functions”, and green lasers on indoor units — a green beam is easier to see and worth having outdoors, but indoors at domestic distances a red one is perfectly visible and the green premium buys you little.

Questions I get asked

How accurate are laser measures?

The honest answer is: more accurate than the building. Decent units are rated at ±1 mm to ±2 mm under standard conditions, budget ones ±2 mm to ±3 mm, and all of them degrade somewhat at long range, in bright light, or against dark and shiny surfaces. For floor plans, EPC floor areas and survey work, any of those figures is comfortably inside what the job needs — a Victorian wall isn’t straight to 3 mm anyway. Repeatability and knowing when a reading is suspect matter far more than the headline tolerance.

Laser measure vs tape measure — do I still need a tape?

Yes, and I carry both every day. The laser wins for anything over about two metres, anything overhead, anything across a room you can’t walk through, and any measurement you take fifty times a day. The tape wins for short work — reveal depths, joist spacings, skirting heights — for anything curved, and for anywhere the laser has no clean target. A 5 m pocket tape plus a laser covers everything a domestic survey throws at you; neither replaces the other.

Can laser measures do floor plans?

The measure itself just produces numbers, but paired over Bluetooth with an app it becomes the front end of a floor-plan workflow. Leica’s DISTO Plan and Bosch’s MeasureOn are free with their respective hardware, and third-party apps like magicplan and CubiCasa accept measurements from most Leica and Bosch units. Shots drop straight onto the sketch as you walk the house, and the plan exports to PDF, DXF or into RdSAP and survey software. It’s the strongest practical argument for buying Leica or Bosch rather than a budget brand.

Do laser measures work outside in sunlight?

They measure fine; the problem is your eyes. The instrument can usually still detect its own reflected beam, but past 15–20 m in bright daylight you can’t see the dot, so you don’t know what you’ve measured — which is worse than no reading. Options, in ascending price order: shoot to a shaded surface, use a red target plate, buy a green-beam unit (visibly better in daylight), or buy a unit with a camera pointfinder like the Leica DISTO D5, which shows the target on screen and makes long outdoor shots genuinely dependable.
