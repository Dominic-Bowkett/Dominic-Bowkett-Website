Gas safety certificate cost
in 2026

A gas safety certificate — landlords still often call it a CP12, after the old record form the name stuck to — typically costs £60–£150 in 2026, depending on how many appliances need testing. It's a legal duty backed by a criminal penalty, not a box to tick, and the invoice is rarely the number that ends up mattering.

Published 18 Aug 2026 Reading time · 9 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A gas safety certificate in 2026 typically costs £60–£90 for a straightforward property with a boiler and one other appliance, rising to £80–£120 where there's a boiler plus a hob or fire, and £90–£150 for a house with three or more appliances needing testing individually. That's the fee for a Gas Safe registered engineer to inspect and test every gas appliance, flue and pipework connection in the property and issue the record. Combine it with the annual boiler service, which most landlords do anyway, and the two jobs done on one visit typically come to somewhere around £85–£140.

But the certificate fee is the easy number. The one that decides whether this costs more isn't on any price list — it's what the engineer finds. And unlike most of the checks in this series, a gas safety check that goes wrong doesn't end with a quote landing later in the week. An appliance classed Immediately Dangerous or At Risk gets disconnected there and then, labelled, with written notice of the danger, before the engineer leaves.

I'm a building surveyor and energy assessor, not a Gas Safe registered engineer: I don't carry out gas safety checks or issue the certificate myself, only a Gas Safe registered engineer can legally do that. What follows is 2026 pricing cross-checked against current UK trade sources, the legal duty every landlord is under, what an Immediately Dangerous or At Risk finding actually means, and what I'd want to see in a fair quote.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 18 August 2026 against Checkatrade's published price guide and several independent letting-compliance and trade sources' 2026 figures, cross-referenced against each other. As with the rest of this series, London and the South East — Dom’s patch included — run well above the national figures quoted here: London commonly 30–50% above average, the South East 15–20% above.

Two figures worth having alongside that table. If an appliance is disconnected and the engineer needs to order parts or come back to finish remedial work, a second visit typically adds £45–£100 on top. And the remedial work itself — priced separately from the certificate fee — ranges from a straightforward repair at £50–£150 up to several hundred pounds where a part or appliance has to be replaced outright.

What drives the price

Number of appliances. Pricing scales with how many separate appliances need testing — boiler, gas hob, gas fire, gas water heater — each adding roughly £10–£20 to a base fee built around the boiler alone.

Region. London commonly runs 30–50% above the national average, the South East 15–20% above; the North of England, Wales and Scotland trend at or below it.

Bundling with a boiler service. An engineer already on site to service the boiler can usually test everything else at the same time for less than booking the two jobs separately.

Fuel and appliance type. Not every Gas Safe registered engineer is registered for every category of work — mains gas, LPG and commercial catering appliances each need separate registration — so a property with an unusual appliance, an LPG range or an older gas fire, may need a specialist rather than the first generalist you find.

HMO and licensing requirements. Licensed HMOs sometimes fold the gas safety check into a wider licence-renewal inspection, and with several kitchens or bedsits on one meter, the appliance count — and the price — adds up faster than a single-family house.

The landlord's legal duty

Every landlord letting a property with a gas appliance has a duty under Regulation 36 of the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 to have each appliance and flue checked for safety within 12 months of installation, and at intervals of no more than 12 months after that. You can renew up to 2 months early without losing the original anniversary date — useful for keeping a portfolio's renewal dates aligned rather than creeping earlier each year. Records have to be kept for 2 years, a copy given to an existing tenant within 28 days of the check, and to a new tenant before they move in.

Miss it, and the exposure isn't a fixed penalty like the £30,000 civil fine under the electrical safety regulations I covered in EICR cost — gas safety is enforced criminally by the Health and Safety Executive, which can prosecute for a substantial fine and/or a custodial sentence, and in the worst cases, where a tenant has died from carbon monoxide poisoning in a property without a valid check, landlords have faced gross negligence manslaughter charges. There's also a quieter but more common consequence: since the Deregulation Act 2015, a landlord who can't show the tenant had a valid gas safety certificate before they moved in cannot serve a valid Section 21 notice, and the courts have taken a strict line on this — it isn't something a late certificate fixes retrospectively.

Owner-occupiers are under no equivalent legal duty. It's still sensible to have gas appliances serviced roughly annually — most boiler warranties require it anyway — and worth doing before you rely on a house you're buying that has an appliance you can't confidently date.

What a failed check actually means

A gas safety check doesn't produce a graded report the way an EICR does — it's closer to binary. Under the current Gas Industry Unsafe Situations Procedure, an appliance is classed Immediately Dangerous (ID) if it poses an immediate risk to life, or At Risk (AR) if a fault could become dangerous, and the engineer's response to both is the same: turn it off, label it, and give written notice explaining the danger, before they leave. There's no lesser advisory category any more — a category called Not to Current Standards existed until 2016, but it was removed specifically because it diluted the safety message; today an appliance is either safe or it isn't.

An appliance that's capped off doesn't end the job either — the engineer still has to check the capped pipework itself for leaks, since disconnecting an appliance doesn't make the gas pipe behind it safe on its own. If parts need ordering, or the fix is more involved than an on-the-day repair, expect a second visit fee on top of whatever the remedial work itself costs.

The findings that come up most often on the older gas installations I see, behind an ageing boiler or a gas fire that's clearly original to the house: corroded flues, inadequate ventilation to a room with an open-flued appliance, and pipework that's been boxed in or extended without being tested since. None of that means the whole system is a write-off — usually it's a specific fault, fixed and re-tested — but it's exactly the kind of thing that turns a £70 certificate into a job costing several times that.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Gas Safe registration checked, not just claimed. Ask to see the engineer's ID card and confirm the categories of work on it match your appliances — mains gas, LPG, catering — since only a registered engineer can legally work on a gas appliance at all, unlike the "recommended" registration schemes elsewhere in this series.
  • The appliance count stated up front, not a flat any-property price — that's what the fee should genuinely scale with.
  • Whether a re-visit is included if something needs following up, or charged separately.
  • The actual written record, with a unique reference number and every appliance listed, not a verbal "it's fine."
  • No guaranteed pass before testing. An engineer who promises a clean certificate before they've tested anything is telling you what you want to hear, not what's actually in the property.

Questions I get asked

How often do I need a gas safety certificate? At least every 12 months from the date of the last check, or within 12 months of a new appliance being installed. You can have the check done up to 2 months early without losing the original anniversary date, which is worth doing if you want renewal dates to line up across a portfolio rather than creeping earlier each year.

What happens if the engineer finds a fault? The appliance is classed Immediately Dangerous or At Risk, disconnected on the spot, labelled, and you're given written notice of the danger before the engineer leaves. Nothing gets a quiet advisory note any more — Not to Current Standards was removed from the classification system in 2016. Remedial work is quoted and charged separately from the certificate fee, and if parts need ordering, expect a second visit fee of roughly £45–£100 on top.

Do homeowners need a gas safety certificate? There's no legal duty on owner-occupiers — the Regulations apply to landlords letting a property, not people living in their own home. It's still worth an annual boiler service regardless, partly because most manufacturer warranties require it, and I'd always want appliances checked before relying on a house I'm buying that has old or unusual gas fittings.

Can I be prosecuted for not having a gas safety certificate? Yes. It's a criminal offence under the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998, enforced by the Health and Safety Executive, and prosecution can mean a substantial fine and/or a custodial sentence. Separately, a missing or late certificate can invalidate a Section 21 notice, and in the small number of cases where a tenant has died from carbon monoxide poisoning in a non-compliant property, landlords have faced gross negligence manslaughter charges.
