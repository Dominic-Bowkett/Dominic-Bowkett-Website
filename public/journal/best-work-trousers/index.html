The best
work trousers,
for kneeling, crawling and holster tools

A trouser built for an office and one built for a building site fail in exactly the same place on a survey — the knee, first, and then whatever’s meant to be reachable from a pocket while crouched under a suspended floor. Kneeling and crawling around the edges of a house is routine rather than occasional in this work, and the trouser doing it either has a knee pocket that sits where a knee actually lands and a holster pocket a hand finds without looking, or it doesn’t. Here’s what separates the two, and eight pairs worth having in the van, from a premium Scandinavian-cut trouser through to a genuinely cheap holster-pocket pair that does the basics properly.

A fair share of what happens on an inspection happens below waist height — kneeling to lift a corner of carpet at a suspicious skirting, crouching under a suspended timber floor with a torch and a damp meter, working back out of a crawl space on elbows and knees with a phone in one pocket and a laser measure in the other. What’s being worn while that happens either survives the visit or it doesn’t, and a blown-out knee seam or a holster pocket that tips a tape measure into a dusty void mid-survey is the sort of thing that’s mildly infuriating rather than serious — right up until it happens on the day it actually matters.

Ordinary trousers get the fabric right and stop there. A trouser built for this kind of work earns its keep on three things specifically: a knee pocket positioned where a knee genuinely lands when kneeling, rather than where a pattern-cutter assumed it would sit standing up; holster pockets angled so a hand finds a torch or a compact laser without looking down; and enough stretch through the seat and knee that repeated crouching and climbing in and out of a loft hatch doesn’t strain a seam by lunchtime. None of that shows up on a hanger. It only shows up after a few weeks of actually crawling around in the things.

What follows is eight pairs that get those specifics right for site and survey work, from a Scandinavian-cut trouser built for daily wear through to a genuinely cheap holster-pocket pair that does the basics without pretending to be anything more. Whichever ends up in the van, it’s worth pairing with a proper set of knee pads — reviewed separately — since a knee pocket is only ever as good as what’s actually inside it.

How I judge a pair of work trousers

Where the knee pocket actually sits. A pocket printed on a spec sheet as a “knee pad pocket” is only useful if it lines up with where a knee lands when kneeling, not where it sits when standing upright in a shop mirror. The better trousers here let the pocket travel a little, or cut it low and wide enough that it doesn’t matter; the worse ones need the whole leg pulled up before a pad sits where it should.

Holster pocket placement, not pocket count. Eleven pockets on a spec sheet sounds generous until none of them are where a hand actually falls reaching down mid-kneel. A properly angled holster pocket means a torch or laser measure comes out one-handed without looking; a flat side pocket in the same spot just means patting yourself down.

Stretch through the seat and knee, not just the marketing. “Stretch fabric” on a label can mean anything from a genuine four-way stretch panel at the knee to a fractionally more forgiving weave everywhere else. The first matters for repeated deep kneeling and crawling; the second is closer to a comfort claim than a functional one.

Fabric at the points that actually take the abrasion. Ripstop and canvas both survive a loft hatch edge or a rough brick reveal better than plain poly-cotton, but what matters more is whether the reinforcement — usually CORDURA — sits specifically at the knee and hem rather than being a headline claim about the whole garment.

Whether the fit holds up through a full day of bending. A waistband that needs constant hitching after every kneel, or a knee that doesn’t articulate cleanly through a deep crouch, turns into a background irritation by mid-afternoon that a five-minute trial in a changing room won’t reveal.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Knee pocket position beats knee pocket marketing. “CORDURA-reinforced knee pad pockets” appears on nearly every trouser in this list; whether the pocket actually lines up with a real kneeling position varies a lot more than the spec sheets suggest. The Helly Hansen solves it with genuine adjustment; most others get it right through simple cut and positioning — worth checking owner feedback on fit before assuming the feature works as described.

A knee pocket is nothing without a pad inside it. None of these trousers protect a kneecap on their own — the pocket only holds a CE-certified knee pad insert, bought separately in nearly every case. Buying the trouser and stopping there is a common and easy mistake.

Pocket count is a weaker signal than pocket placement. Eleven pockets sounds like more than seven until it’s the seven that are actually reachable while kneeling. A trouser with fewer, better-placed holster and cargo pockets beats one with a headline pocket count and half of them under a flap that needs both hands to open.

Rip-stop and reflective piping are situational, not universal. Reflective detailing genuinely matters working roadside or on an active construction site; it’s decorative on a domestic loft inspection. Ripstop fabric resists snagging better than plain weave but isn’t automatically tougher at the points — knee, seat, hem — that actually take the wear, which is where CORDURA reinforcement matters more than the base fabric claim.

Stretch percentage on a label isn’t the same as stretch where it’s needed. A trouser can be marketed as “stretch fit” on the strength of a slightly more forgiving weave through the leg while the knee and seat — the parts doing the actual work during a crawl-space visit — stay rigid. Four-way stretch specifically through the knee and crotch gusset, as in the Helly Hansen and Snickers, is the claim worth checking for rather than “stretch” on its own.

Questions I get asked

Do the knee pad pockets come with pads included? Rarely. Every trouser on this list is sold as the pocket only — the CE EN 14404-rated foam or gel insert that actually protects a kneecap is a separate purchase, covered in the knee pads guide. Check the pocket’s dimensions against whichever pad is being bought; not every pad fits every pocket.

What’s the actual difference between a holster pocket and a cargo pocket? A holster pocket is angled and usually bellowed, sitting lower on the thigh so a hand finds a torch, tape or compact laser without looking down mid-kneel. A cargo pocket sits flatter and larger, better suited to bulkier items that don’t need one-handed access — gloves, a folded dust sheet, spare fixings. Most trade trousers carry both because they solve different problems.

Does stretch fabric wear out faster than traditional canvas? At the level of pure abrasion resistance, generally yes — a stretch weave gives up a little durability for the range of movement. The better trousers here close that gap by reinforcing specifically the knee, seat and hem with CORDURA rather than relying on the base fabric to survive the wear on its own, which is why checking where the reinforcement actually sits matters more than the fabric’s headline stretch percentage.

How many pairs does a working surveyor actually need? Two as a practical minimum — one in the wash or drying doesn’t mean a missed day. Trousers built for daily kneeling and crawling pick up dust, insulation fibres and the occasional loft-hatch tear faster than office wear, and rotating a pair keeps the reinforced points from wearing through before the rest of the garment does.
