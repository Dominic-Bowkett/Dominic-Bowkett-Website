The best
bathroom extractor fans
from a £20 swap to app control

A dead fan, a fan wired straight to the light with no overrun, or one sized for a downstairs cloakroom doing duty over a power shower — I see all three most weeks. The bathroom fan is the smallest, cheapest piece of condensation control in the house and the one that gets blamed last. Here's what Approved Document F actually asks of it, when a humidistat earns its keep over a plain timer, and the eight fans I'd look at.

On a lot of surveys, the bathroom fan tells you as much as the loft does. Pull the cover off and it's either seized solid, wired to nothing, or connected straight to the light with none of the run-on that clears the air after the shower's finished. The fan is rarely the whole story, but it's often the first thing worth fixing — cheap, quick, and something a competent electrician can swap in an afternoon.

What it needs to do is straightforward: clear the warm, moisture-laden air a bath or shower generates before it finds a cold surface and condenses. Approved Document F sets a minimum intermittent extract rate of 15 litres per second for a bathroom (6 l/s for a separate WC), or a continuous alternative running low and slow all the time instead. Most fans sold today clear that bar on paper; where they fall down is duct runs that are too long, control that doesn't suit the wiring at the switch, or a rate too low for a room that's grown a power shower since the fan went in.

A working bathroom fan fixes moisture generated in that room. If damp or mould is spreading well beyond it — landing ceilings, bedroom corners, wardrobes on external walls — that's usually a whole-house question, and a PIV unit is the more likely fix. And if the pattern on the wall doesn't behave like condensation — there when it's cold, gone in summer — get that looked at as rising damp instead of buying a fan for it. With that sorted, here are the eight I'd look at.

How I judge a bathroom extractor fan

Extract rate against the actual room, not just the spec sheet. 15 l/s clears Part F for a standard bathroom, but a long duct run, extra bends or an ensuite serving a large power shower all eat into what a fan delivers in practice. A rate with headroom over the minimum is worth more than one that scrapes it.

Control that matches the wiring already at the switch. A timer or humidistat needs three wires reaching the fan — permanent live, switched live and neutral. Plenty of older bathrooms only ever had two, which is the real reason plain pull-cord fans are still sold: sometimes it's the only option without opening up a wall.

Humidistat versus timer versus continuous dMEV. A timer clears the air after use but does nothing between showers; a humidistat reacts to moisture whenever it rises; a continuous dMEV unit runs low and slow all the time and never has to catch up.

Noise quoted honestly. A dB(A) figure at a stated distance and speed is useful; a fan that only sounds quiet because the figure was taken at the lowest of several speeds isn't the same as one that's quiet at the rate the room actually needs.

IPX4 and how it gets wired in. Every fan here is rated IPX4 or better, the minimum for bathroom zones 1 and 2 under BS 7671. What matters more is that it's connected via a fused spur or isolator sited outside those zones — never plug-and-socket in the room — which is an installer question, not a spec-sheet one.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

No window in the room means the overrun isn't optional. Approved Document F requires an intermittent fan to keep running for at least 15 minutes after the room is vacated where there's no openable window — which rules out a plain pull-cord fan with nothing else providing overrun in a windowless bathroom or WC.

A continuous dMEV fan isn't automatically cheaper to run than an intermittent one. The marketing angle is pennies a year in electricity, and that's true on its own terms — but a cheap timer fan that's off most of the day can cost less over a year than one running 24 hours. The real case for dMEV is steadier moisture control, not a smaller bill.

The bathroom fan is one line in a whole-dwelling sum. Part F's continuous ventilation rates aren't just a per-room figure; the total across every room has to clear a whole-dwelling minimum. A single fan swap rarely changes that picture on its own.

IPX4 and where the isolator sits matters more than the fan's own spec. Every fan here meets the bathroom zone rating, but the wiring has to as well — a fused spur or ceiling switch sited outside zones 0–2, never plug-and-socket in the room itself. That's a job for a competent electrician, not a DIY afternoon.

What the marketing pushes that matters less than it sounds. A boosted maximum airflow figure isn't what the fan runs at day to day, and app control is a convenience, not a performance feature. None of it substitutes for real headroom on extract rate and a control type that suits the wiring at the switch.

Questions I get asked

Do I need Building Control sign-off to swap a bathroom fan? Replacing a fan like-for-like on its existing cabling generally doesn't need notifying to Building Control. Changing the ducting route, moving the fan, or converting a passive vent into a powered one is more likely to raise a question — ask whoever fits it to confirm.

Humidistat, timer or continuous dMEV — which one for my bathroom? If the wiring only gives two wires at the switch, a pull-cord fan is the practical answer regardless of preference. With three wires available, a timer suits predictable use, a humidistat suits a bathroom that steams up unpredictably, and a dMEV suits one in heavy daily use that never quite dries out between showers.

Will a low-wattage continuous fan push my electricity bill up? Not by much — a few pounds a year is typical — but it isn't necessarily cheaper than a fan switched off most of the day either. Buy a dMEV unit for steadier moisture control, not to save on the bill.

Can I fit a bathroom extractor fan myself? The fan and duct can be DIY, but the electrical connection sits inside a bathroom's special zones under BS 7671 and is notifiable work under Part P in most cases. Get a competent registered electrician to make the connection.
