The best
tool boxes and storage
for keeping a van organised

Most of what goes wrong with van storage has nothing to do with the boxes themselves — it’s the ten minutes lost every morning working out which box the borescope’s living in this week, or the damp meter that’s stopped reading because it spent a fortnight rolling around loose next to a leaking sample bag. A van that’s organised properly pays for itself in time, not just tidiness. Here’s what actually matters when buying tool storage for a working van, and seven products worth having in the back of one.

A survey van carries a strange mix of kit compared with most trade vehicles — a thermal camera and a borescope worth genuinely protecting, alongside damp meters, laser measures, ladders, sample bags, a stack of report paperwork and whatever site waste came off a loft hatch that morning. None of it is especially heavy. All of it needs to survive being driven over a pothole at 40mph and still be findable in the dark with a torch in one hand.

That’s a different brief to what most van storage is designed around. Tool box marketing leans hard on load ratings and site-abuse durability aimed at someone hauling a cordless combi kit and a mitre saw; a lot of that is simply irrelevant to a bag of RdSAP kit and a moisture meter. What matters more is weatherproofing that protects electronics rather than just resisting a hammer drop, quick visual identification of what’s inside without unpacking the van on a driveway, and security that’s proportionate to sitting on a residential street overnight rather than a building site.

What follows is what actually matters when buying tool storage for this kind of work, and seven products worth having in the back of the van — from modular stacking boxes through to a proper secure vault.

How I judge tool storage for a van

Weatherproofing that matches what’s actually inside. A box carrying a thermal camera or laser measure needs a real IP-rated seal against condensation and dust; a crate of spare batteries or report forms doesn’t. Buy the seal rating for the electronics, not the marketing.

Modularity that saves time loading and unloading — within one ecosystem. Stacking, cleat-locking systems genuinely speed up a morning load if everything’s the same brand. Mix Milwaukee, DeWalt and Bosch boxes and none of them lock to each other, so the time saving disappears the moment a second brand enters the van.

Contents identifiable at a glance. Clear crates, labelled boxes or cup-tray organisers all beat opening three opaque boxes to find one damp meter. This matters more on a job with a client stood at the door than it ever does in a workshop.

Security proportionate to where the van actually sits overnight. A locked box slows an opportunist thief looking through a side window; it won’t stop someone with an angle grinder and ten minutes. Match the spend to what’s realistically at risk in and around the van.

Weight one person can lift alone. Most loading and unloading on this job happens solo, often up a flight of stairs into a flat. A box that needs two hands and a straight back to shift is a box that doesn’t get carried in, which defeats the point of buying it.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Load ratings marketed for power tools rarely apply to instrument kit. A box rated to carry a heavy cordless combi set and a full accessory kit is massively over-specified for a damp meter and a laser measure. Buy for the weatherproof seal and the stacking system, not the weight figure on the box.

Picking a modular ecosystem is a bigger decision than it looks. PACKOUT, TSTAK and L-BOXX don’t stack on each other, so the first box bought effectively decides every box bought afterwards. Worth thinking one step ahead — if any power tools in the van are already Bosch or DeWalt, matching that brand’s storage system avoids buying two incompatible sets.

An IP rating on the box doesn’t mean the lid’s been tested shut in a downpour. IP54 and IP65 describe the seal design, not a guarantee against every condition a van interior throws at it — condensation from a cold night is still worth guarding against separately, particularly for anything with a screen or a battery.

Wheels and trolleys only earn their keep past about 15–20kg of kit. Below that, a wheeled case is more often something extra to trip over getting out of a van than something that actually saves effort — a carry handle and a manageable box size beats a trolley for most of what a surveyor carries.

A padlock eye is a deterrent, not a lock. It slows someone glancing through a window; it does little against someone who’s decided to break in anyway. Genuine security means a Van Vault-type product or a fitted racking system with a lockable door, not a padlock through a plastic hasp.

Questions I get asked

Do I need to commit to one brand’s modular system, or can I mix boxes? Mixing is fine as long as the expectation is realistic — a Milwaukee box and a DeWalt box will happily sit next to each other, they just won’t lock together or stack securely. If stacking and cleat-locking is the main appeal, stick to one brand; if it’s really about individual boxes with good seals, brand consistency matters far less.

Is a secure van vault worth it for a surveyor who isn’t carrying power tools? It depends what’s actually in the van. A thermal imaging camera or a decent laser scanner is genuinely attractive to an opportunist thief and worth protecting properly; a bag of damp meters and tape measures is a much lower-value target and a locked modular box is usually proportionate. Buy the security level that matches the resale value of what’s inside, not a blanket assumption either way.

What’s the best way to keep sample bags and report paperwork separate from instruments? A cheap clear stacking crate, kept entirely separate from the tool boxes, solves this better than trying to fit paperwork into whatever space is left in an instrument box. It also stops a damp sample bag or a leaking mortar sample from ending up next to something electronic.

Should tool boxes be bolted down or left loose in the back of a van? Loose boxes shift under braking and cornering more than most people expect, which is exactly what a modular stacking system is designed to resist — interlocked boxes behave far more like one solid unit. For a van that’s kept long-term, fitted racking like the Sealey system above removes the question entirely by giving everything a fixed position.

An organised van saves time on every single job, not just the ones where it’s obvious — the same principle that makes a tool bag worth choosing properly extends to what it sits inside for the rest of the week.
