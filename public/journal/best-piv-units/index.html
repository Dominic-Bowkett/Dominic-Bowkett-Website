The best
PIV units
for whole-house condensation

Black mould creeping across a north-facing bedroom wall, condensation running down the inside of the windows every morning, a musty smell out of a wardrobe backed onto an external wall — a good share of the calls I get start with one of these. A positive input ventilation (PIV) unit is often the right answer and sometimes entirely the wrong one, depending on the house it’s going into. Here’s how the mechanism actually works, the houses it won’t fix, and the seven units I’d look at — from a £270 basic loft box to a heated, digitally controlled system.

Most condensation is simple physics rather than a building defect: daily living generates a surprising amount of moisture — cooking, showering, drying washing, breathing overnight — and it meets a cold surface somewhere in the house and gives itself up as water. Draught-proofing, secondary glazing and loft insulation make a house cheaper to heat, but they also close off the background ventilation older houses relied on to shift that moisture out. A PIV unit puts some of that ventilation back.

The principle dates to the early 1970s and hasn’t changed much: a loft-mounted unit draws in outside air, filters it, and on heated models tempers it slightly, then a low-wattage fan pushes it down a short duct to a diffuser in the landing ceiling. That gently pressurises the house — not enough to feel like a draught, properly installed — and the drier air displaces moisture-laden air out through the gaps, trickle vents and extractor fans a house already has. It's dilution, not extraction, which is why it suits whole-house condensation rather than a single damp patch.

That also marks its limits. It needs a loft to draw from, so it doesn’t suit a flat or a house with none. It needs somewhere for the pressurised air to escape, so a very airtight, recently retrofitted house is better served by mechanical ventilation with heat recovery (MVHR) instead. And it only addresses airborne moisture — rising and penetrating damp are different problems, and a PIV unit does nothing for either. More on all three below. First, the seven units.

How I judge a PIV unit

Heated versus unheated. A heater tempers incoming loft air before it reaches the diffuser, which matters most over winter; an unheated unit is cheaper to buy and run, and is the sensible choice in a smaller or better-insulated loft where the temperature drop is less noticeable.

Airflow that can be turned down, not just up. A unit fixed at one rate sized for a large house will over-ventilate a small cottage; an adjustable range lets the flow be matched to the actual floor area.

Filter grade and what replacing it costs. A basic G4 filter suits general condensation control; an upgrade to F7 or ePM2.5 catches finer particulates and is worth it for a hay-fever or asthma household, but budget the replacement interval — typically two to five years, at roughly £20–£30 a filter.

A genuinely quiet diffuser, not just a quiet fan. The part a household actually notices is the ceiling diffuser on the landing. A manufacturer that publishes a clear dB(A) figure at the lowest setting is telling you something useful; one that doesn’t is itself informative.

A loft worth ventilating from. If the loft is under-insulated, damp, or barely accessible, that's the job to sort first — a PIV unit draws its air from that space and can't fix a problem that lives there.

Warranty and certification. A BBA certificate is the paperwork trail that satisfies building control if the installation is ever queried; a longer warranty (five to seven years here) is a reasonable proxy for confidence in the motor and electronics.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

No loft, no PIV. The unit draws its supply air from the loft void, so a flat or a house without one isn't a candidate for any of the units above. Wall-mounted PIV units exist for that situation, but they're a different product category and aren't covered here.

A cold or poorly insulated loft is the job to fix first. An unheated unit pulling air from a loft near outside temperature all winter is where "PIV makes the house draughty" complaints come from. Either choose a heated model, or sort the loft's own insulation first.

An airtight, retrofitted house wants MVHR, not PIV. PIV works by pressurising the house slightly and relying on existing leakage paths — gaps, trickle vents, extractor fans — to let that air back out. Once a house has been through a proper PAS 2035 airtightness retrofit, those paths are deliberately closed, which is when balanced mechanical ventilation with heat recovery becomes the right whole-house answer instead. What MVHR installation costs covers that comparison.

Rising and penetrating damp aren't condensation, and no PIV unit touches either. A failed damp-proof course, or water getting in through the fabric itself, needs its own remedy, not ventilation. If the pattern on the wall doesn't fit classic condensation — worse in cold weather, disappearing in summer — get it looked at before spending on a fan. Rising damp treatment costs and damp-proofing costs cover the genuine fixes.

Building regulations compliance is a paperwork question, not a blanket rule. PIV isn't a named compliant strategy for new-build work under Approved Document F, but it's widely accepted as a remedial strategy in existing houses, generally evidenced by a manufacturer's BBA certificate rather than treated as notifiable building work. Ask whoever fits it how compliance is being demonstrated.

What the marketing pushes that matters less than it sounds. A wireless remote or app is a convenience feature, not a performance one. A stated heater temperature boost is a headline figure, not a guarantee at every setting. None of it substitutes for the airflow, filter grade and dB(A) figure actually fitting the house.

Questions I get asked

Will a PIV unit make my house colder? Running costs are genuinely low, typically £6–£20 a year in electricity, so the direct cost isn't the issue. The real trade-off is comfort: an unheated unit in a cold loft can feel like a draught in winter, which is where a heated model or a warmer loft makes the difference. Once the excess moisture is gone, most households report the house feels less clammy overall.

Do I need Building Regulations approval to fit one? There's no single blanket rule requiring notification, but Approved Document F expects ventilation compliance to be demonstrated somehow — which is what a manufacturer's BBA certificate is for. Ask the installer to confirm the paperwork trail rather than assuming either way.

Should I get a PIV unit or MVHR? PIV suits an existing house with the usual amount of natural leakage for the pressurised air to escape through. MVHR only works properly once a house has been made deliberately airtight, typically as part of a wider retrofit. If you're not sure which applies, that's exactly what a proper retrofit assessment answers before money is spent on either system.

How often does the filter need changing, and what does that cost? A basic G4 filter typically needs replacing every two to three years; an upgraded F7 or ePM2.5 filter can last up to five, depending on the manufacturer. Replacement filters generally run £20–£30 each — worth factoring in alongside the unit price.

I don’t install or commission PIV units myself, but if condensation is driving the search, a PAS 2035 retrofit assessment is where I’d start — working out whether the fabric or the ventilation is the actual cause. Once a unit’s in, a ventilation testing visit shows what it's delivering at the diffuser, alongside the installer’s own paperwork. If it's rising or penetrating damp rather than condensation, get that diagnosed instead — see rising damp treatment costs and damp-proofing costs.
