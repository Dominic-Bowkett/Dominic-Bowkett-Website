The best
work gloves
for survey work

A fair chunk of a survey happens bare-handed — a laser measure, a tablet, a finger run along a skirting board for a soft patch. But there is always a stretch where that stops being sensible: pulling back loft insulation undisturbed since the seventies, feeling along a rusted soil pipe in a crawl space, or working a manhole cover that has not been lifted in years. Here is what is worth keeping in the van for that stretch, from a box of disposable nitrile to a pair rated for cut and impact protection.

Most of what a surveyor’s hands do during a visit rewards bare skin — a laser measure, a phone camera, the fiddly latch on a loft hatch. Gloves earn their keep for the other half: pulling back loft insulation undisturbed for decades, feeling along a corroded pipe in a crawl space, or gripping a manhole cover rusted into its frame. What is in a pair matters less than whether it goes on and off without a fight, because a glove wrestled off to answer a phone ends up left in the door pocket instead.

The other reason a decent pair earns its place has less to do with cuts than what is on the other side of the material. Loft insulation sheds glass fibre that sits in skin for days; damp and mould-affected timber is not worth handling bare-fingered before a coffee break; and the debris in an unmodernised crawl space — old nails, glass, whatever has dropped through a floor void over the decades — is exactly what a decent palm coating is built to shrug off.

How I judge a work glove

Dexterity that survives contact with a phone or laser measure. Half a survey’s tools have a touchscreen or a small button, and a glove that has to come off for every reading gets left off entirely. Touchscreen-compatible fingertips are worth having; a bulky palm that turns a laser measure into a two-handed job is not.

Grip that holds wet and dirty, not just in a dry product photo. A palm that grips a dry drill handle is a different test to a rusted manhole cover after rain. Latex, nitrile and PU coatings behave differently once wet — worth checking which the listing specifies.

Protection matched to a real hazard — not a marketing cut rating. Cut resistance matters for a handful of jobs: clearing broken glass in a derelict crawl space, handling a lath-and-plaster ceiling coming down, reaching past an old nail in a loft hatch edge. For the rest of a visit, a high cut rating just adds bulk and cost for a risk not there.

Breathable enough to still be on by the third hour. A fully rubber-dipped glove that felt fine trying on in a shop turns into a swamp after ninety minutes of loft crawling. A knitted or fabric back that lets air through is the difference between a glove worn all day and one abandoned after twenty minutes.

Cheap enough that losing one in a loft does not matter. Gloves get left behind — on a joist, in a skip, in whichever van was parked next to the site. Anything much over a tenner a pair starts to feel like a loss worth avoiding, defeating the point of kit meant to be grabbed without thinking.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Cut ratings matter for a specific hazard, not every glove in the van. EN388 and ANSI ratings are worth checking for jobs that actually involve something sharp — broken glass, exposed nails, a stripped-out room. A high cut rating as a default for every pair pays for bulk and cost most of a visit does not need.

Touchscreen compatibility is genuinely useful; padded “anti-vibration” extras rarely are. A glove that lets a phone or laser measure respond through the fingertip saves stopping to strip it off. Gel inserts and anti-vibration padding are aimed at people running power tools all day, not a surveyor’s occasional SDS bit.

Fit matters more than the spec sheet. A glove one size too big loses most of its dexterity advantage, and one too small tears at the seams within a few uses. Most picks above are sold across a full size range — worth checking against a hand measurement.

Disposable and reusable gloves solve different problems. A rigger or coated grip glove protects hands from a task; disposable nitrile keeps one job’s contamination away from the next. Wearing a reusable glove for a mould sample defeats the point of both.

A stocked van beats one do-everything pair. No single glove here covers grip, cut resistance, warmth and dexterity at once, and finding one that does usually means compromising on all four. Two or three pairs — a grip glove, something disposable, something for winter — cost less than one expensive do-everything glove and cover more of what a visit needs.

Questions I get asked

Do surveyors actually need cut-resistant gloves?

For most residential surveys, no — the bulk of a visit is inspection and measurement rather than handling debris. Cut resistance earns its place for specific situations: a part-demolished property, a loft with old exposed nails, or clearing glass and rubble in a derelict crawl space. Keeping one cut-resistant pair in the van for those jobs, rather than wearing one as a default, covers the risk without adding bulk to the rest of the visit.

What does an EN388 or ANSI cut rating actually mean?

Both are lab tests rating how much force a blade needs to cut through the material, translated into a scale — EN388 uses letters A to F, ANSI uses A1 to A9, and the two do not convert directly. Higher is not automatically better for survey work: a high rating usually means a thicker, less dextrous glove, the wrong trade-off for tools or a touchscreen.

Are disposable nitrile gloves worth carrying alongside proper work gloves?

Yes, for hygiene rather than protection. Mould, contamination, or a soil pipe that has backed up are better handled with something thrown away afterwards than a reusable glove that then travels to the next job. A box in the van costs little and covers a genuinely different need to a grip or cut-resistant pair.

How often should work gloves actually be replaced?

Coated palms wear through at the fingertips and thumb well before the rest of the glove looks tired, and that is the point to replace them — a worn patch stops gripping properly and lets debris through to the skin. A pair used regularly needs replacing every few months; a spare kept in the van will outlast that considerably.

Gloves are one of the cheapest lines in a survey kit list, and one of the easiest to get wrong by buying a single pair to do every job. Getting the practical details right on a building survey or retrofit assessment matters more than what is on your hands doing it — but the right pair, or two, makes the physical side of the job easier.
