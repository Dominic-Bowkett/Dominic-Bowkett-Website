Most of a survey happens indoors, and then there is the bit that does not. The roof slopes you cannot see from the ground, the rear garden where the downpipe discharges onto the footing, the side return full of wet leaves, the crawl space with a damp floor. On a dry day that is a pleasant walk round. On a wet one, a waterproof jacket protects the top half and leaves your legs to soak up every drip, and wet trousers in a cold loft or a cold van are a miserable way to write up a report.

That is the case for overtrousers. They are a thin waterproof layer that pulls on over what you are already wearing, lives in the door pocket of the van, and comes out for the wet bit and goes away again. They are not a replacement for sensible work trousers, and they are not a substitute for a decent waterproof jacket. They fill the gap between the two.

The market splits in two. At one end are cheap, PVC-coated or lightweight packaway overtrousers that cost the price of a pub lunch and are meant to be thrown in a bag. At the other are proper breathable shells from outdoor brands, which cost five times as much and feel noticeably better to wear for hours. Six picks follow across both ends, drawn from manufacturers’ descriptions and published owner feedback.

How I judge overtrousers

Full-length leg zips or press-stud hems. The single most practical feature. If you cannot get them on over safety boots without taking the boots off, you will end up not wearing them. Look for a lower-leg zip or a wide hem with press studs, and check the listing for how far up the zip runs.

Waterproof, not just showerproof. Look for taped or welded seams and a stated hydrostatic head or an EN 343 rating on the workwear options. A cheaper coated pair will keep you dry for an hour of drizzle. A breathable shell is the better choice if you are in them for a whole morning, because a non-breathable pair turns into a sauna on a warm wet day.

Packability. They live in the van, so they have to fold small and survive being stuffed under a seat. Packaway designs with a stuff sack are far less likely to be left at home than a bulky pair that needs hanging up.

Durability against abrasion. Roof valleys, ladder rungs, joist ends and brick walls will all go through a thin layer. Do not expect a lightweight shell to survive crawling across a rough floor on your knees. For that, the answer is knee pads and a tougher trouser, not a bigger budget.

Fit over bulk. A cut that allows room for work trousers and a thermal layer underneath, with an elasticated waist, matters more than a fashion silhouette. Check that the crotch does not pull when you climb a ladder or step up into a hatch.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Start with when you will wear them. If the answer is a handful of wet roof and garden inspections a year, a cheap packaway pair is the right answer and anything dearer is paying for a comfort you will rarely use. If you are outdoors most days from October to March, the case for a breathable shell is much stronger, because the difference between a pair that vents and one that does not is the difference between a dry morning and a damp one from the inside.

Second, ask whether you need them at all. Many surveys never need more than a waterproof jacket. A roof inspection from the ground with binoculars or a drone does not need a wet-weather trouser; neither does a short walk round the garden. Overtrousers earn their place when you are going to be standing or kneeling in wet places, which on my kind of job means a damp crawl space, a wet side return or a long wait for access.

What gets pushed at you, and does not matter much, starts with the hydrostatic head figure on a cheap pair. A very high number on a thin, coated fabric tells you it is waterproof when new, not how it copes with abrasion or how it feels after four hours. Fashion colours and branded zips are the same. A hi-vis finish is only worth paying for if you work near traffic; otherwise black or navy hides the dirt better.

The sensible stack for a wet day is a good pair of boots — safety boots for site work or wellington boots for the wet crawl space — with overtrousers outside the boot top so the water runs down and off, not into them. Tucking trousers into boots feels dry and fills the boot like a funnel.

Finally, wash them properly. Overtrousers pick up mud, loft dust and grease, and a dirty face fabric stops shedding water well. Follow the care label, rinse them off, and dry them away from direct heat, which can damage taped seams.

Questions I get asked

Are overtrousers worth buying, or should I just buy waterproof work trousers? Waterproof work trousers are heavier, hotter and dearer, and you wear them whether it rains or not. Overtrousers cost less, live in the van and go on only when needed. If you work outdoors every day in all weather, waterproof work trousers can make sense. For a mixed indoor and outdoor job like a survey, overtrousers are the better value.

Can I put overtrousers on over boots? Only if they have a long leg zip or a wide hem with studs. Many cheap pairs have neither, and you will find out at the worst possible moment. Check the listing for lower-leg zips, and try them on over the boots you actually wear.

How waterproof is waterproof? Look for taped seams and a stated standard or hydrostatic head figure. On workwear, EN 343 is the standard that matters; a Class 3 rating for water penetration is the highest. On outdoor shells, a figure of 10,000mm or above is a sensible minimum for regular UK rain. A pair with untaped seams will let water through at the stitch holes however good the fabric is.

Do overtrousers make you sweat? The cheap coated ones do, because they do not breathe. Breathable shells vent far better but are not magic, and a long climb in warm rain will make anyone damp. The answer is side zips, a thin base layer and taking them off when you come inside. Fit matters too, because a tight pair traps more heat than a roomy one.

Staying dry is the easy half of a wet-weather survey; the harder half is working out where the water is getting in.
