The best
CO2 monitors
for finding out if a room actually breathes

Carbon dioxide is the most honest ventilation number there is — it only ever measures one thing, and it can't be argued with the way a damp reading sometimes can. The catch is that a lot of what's sold as a “CO2 monitor” for £15 isn't measuring CO2 at all. Most of the condensation calls I get to start the same way: someone describing a bedroom that’s wet on the inside of the glass by morning, or a room that never quite loses its stuffiness no matter how often the door’s left open in the day. The moisture meter and the thermal camera tell me what’s already happened to the plaster and the paint. What they don’t tell me is why — whether the room’s simply too cold for the moisture load, or whether nobody’s actually getting fresh air in and stale air out of it overnight. That second question has an answer that doesn’t involve any guesswork at all, and it isn’t a damp reading: it’s the carbon dioxide level.

Exhaled breath runs somewhere around 40,000 parts per million of CO2, against roughly 420ppm in outdoor air. Shut a bedroom door on two sleeping adults for eight hours and the CO2 in that room climbs at a rate that depends on exactly one thing — how much fresh air is getting in, through a trickle vent, an extractor, a gap under the door, or nothing at all. It isn’t a proxy for damp, dust or allergens; it measures ventilation rate, directly, and it does it more honestly than almost anything else that comes out of the van. A chart that sits at 400–800ppm all night means the room breathes. One that climbs past 1,500ppm and stays there means it doesn’t, and that’s very often the missing half of a condensation diagnosis rather than the mould itself being the real problem.

The complication with this category is that a lot of what Amazon lists as a “CO2 monitor” for £15–£20 isn’t measuring CO2 at all — it’s a cheap VOC sensor extrapolating an estimated “eCO2” figure, which is worse than no reading if a real ventilation decision hangs on it. The seven below all measure real carbon dioxide: most with a manufacturer-stated NDIR (non-dispersive infrared) sensor, the only technology that actually detects CO2 directly, and the one exception here still ships a dedicated CO2 sensor with its own published accuracy rather than a VOC-derived guess. They span a £30 plug-in traffic light to a £190 portable with its own app.

How I judge a CO2 monitor

A genuine NDIR sensor, not an estimate. This is the one spec that matters more than any other. Cheap sensors infer an “eCO2” number from a VOC reading, which drifts and bears no fixed relationship to a room's real ventilation rate. NDIR shines infrared light through the air and measures how much of it CO2 itself absorbs — it's measuring the gas, not guessing at it from a different one.

A number you can read without your phone. The whole point of a bedroom test is what happens overnight, unattended. A monitor with its own display — ideally one readable in the dark without a glaring backlight — answers the question on the spot; an app-only monitor means checking a screen before the number means anything.

Battery or mains, depending on the job. A battery unit moves between rooms over a few nights, suiting a specific bedroom or an intermittent complaint. A mains or USB unit suits living permanently in one spot — a kitchen, a living room, wherever the household spends its evenings.

Logging, not just a live number. A single reading tells you the room right now, which might just be the moment someone opened a window. A history — on the device, an app, or an SD card — shows whether the level climbs all night or briefly spikes and recovers, and that shape matters more than any single figure.

A sane alarm, not a constant one. A threshold that flags a room past roughly 1,000–1,500ppm is useful. A unit that beeps every time the number ticks up gets muted within a week and stops being read at all.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

“eCO2” is not CO2. Plenty of cheap air-quality gadgets, including some sold explicitly as CO2 monitors, use a VOC sensor and an algorithm to estimate a CO2-equivalent figure. It drifts, it isn't calibrated against real carbon dioxide, and it can read a room as fine when it genuinely isn't. Anything that doesn't state NDIR, or a dedicated CO2 sensor with its own published accuracy, should be treated as decoration rather than a measurement.

Chasing lab-grade accuracy is pointless here. Every NDIR sensor on this list lands around ±50ppm, or a few percent of the reading. That's precise enough to tell 600ppm from 1,400ppm without doubt, which is the only distinction that actually changes a ventilation decision. A cheaper unit with a slightly wider tolerance isn't meaningfully less useful for that.

A colour touchscreen is a convenience, not a better measurement. The Qingping's screen is pleasant to look at, but underneath it's the same class of NDIR sensor as the plainer units here. Pay for a nicer display because it suits where it'll sit, not because it implies more accurate data.

This is not a smoke, heat or CO alarm. Carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide are different gases entirely, and nothing here meets EN 50291 or satisfies a landlord's legal alarm duties. A CO2 monitor is a ventilation diagnostic; a carbon monoxide alarm is a life-safety device, and a property needs both for entirely different reasons.

Logging beats a single number for anything you'll need to explain to someone else. A one-off reading of 1,800ppm is easy to dismiss as a bad moment. A graph showing a bedroom above 1,500ppm for six hours every night is much harder to argue with, whether the person on the other end is a tenant, a landlord, or a builder who fitted a trickle vent that never gets opened.

Questions I get asked

What CO2 level counts as bad in a bedroom? Below 800ppm is good ventilation. 800–1,000ppm is acceptable for most of the night. Above 1,000–1,500ppm for sustained periods is where school ventilation guidance (Building Bulletin 101) and workplace standards start flagging a room as under-ventilated, and it lines up with the groggy, stuffy feeling people report on waking. It isn't a health hazard at typical indoor levels, but it's a reliable sign the air isn't being replaced fast enough — usually also the condition mould and condensation prefer.

Will a CO2 monitor fix my condensation problem? No — it only diagnoses the ventilation half of it. If the overnight reading is consistently high, the fix is more air movement: a trickle vent that actually opens, an extractor on a humidistat, or a ventilation assessment to work out whether the property needs something more than a single room's fan, right up to what MVHR costs if the fabric's tight enough to justify it.

Do I need to test every room, or just the bedroom? Bedrooms overnight are the priority, because that's where a room is sealed up with people for the longest single stretch, and the CO2 climb is most consistent and easiest to read. A living room in the evening with several people and the telly on is the next most useful check; box rooms and hallways rarely show anything a bedroom hasn't already.

How do I tell a real CO2 monitor from a cheap one before buying? Look for the word NDIR, or “non-dispersive infrared,” explicitly in the listing or spec sheet. If a product only says “eCO2” or “equivalent CO2,” or doesn't mention the sensor type at all despite a suspiciously low price, it's very likely the VOC-estimate kind rather than a genuine measurement.

A CO2 monitor only ever answers the ventilation half of a damp or condensation question — if the overnight readings come back high, a ventilation assessment is the logical next step, and what MVHR costs is worth reading alongside it if a single fan clearly won't be enough.
