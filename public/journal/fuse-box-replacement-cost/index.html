Fuse box
replacement
cost in
2026

A new fuse box (consumer unit) typically costs £450–£750 in 2026, and £700–£1,200 in the South East. The board is the small part; the testing and what it finds decide the final bill.

Published 7 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Your electrician says the fuse box needs replacing, or an EICR has flagged it, or you are buying a house with a board that looks like it came out of a 1970s schoolroom. In 2026 a new consumer unit (the modern name for a fuse box) typically costs £450–£750 across most of the UK and £700–£1,200 in London and the South East, including the board, testing, the certificate and building control notification.

That is the swap on a tidy installation. If the electrician finds poor earthing, damaged cable or a circuit that fails its tests, the figure can climb past £1,500, and that is where most disputes about this job start. The other question is whether you need one at all. Plenty of people are told they do when their existing board is perfectly safe, and plenty are not told when theirs is not.

I'm a building surveyor, not an electrician. I don't fit consumer units or issue the certificate, a registered electrician does that, so I have no stake in which board you buy. What follows is the 2026 pricing and my read on when replacement is a safety job and when it is a sales pitch.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 7 October 2026 against Fantastic Services, Book a Builder, BPH Electrics and Voltflow, all published in 2026, with Less.co.uk as a lower outlier. They agree closely on the headline range. London and the South East run noticeably above northern rates (Fantastic puts labour 15–30% higher), so Kent, Sussex and Surrey sit toward the top of each band.

A worked example: a three-bed semi in Sussex with a straightforward older fuse board would typically cost £850–£1,100 for an RCBO board with surge protection, plus £100–£400 if the earthing and bonding need bringing up to standard. Lower online figures, such as Less.co.uk's £350–£600, look like the simplest like-for-like swap in a cheaper region, and I'd treat them as a floor, not a norm.

What drives the price

The type of board. This is the biggest lever. One guide puts RCBOs at £20–£35 per circuit against £5–£10 for an ordinary breaker, which adds roughly £120–£300 across a typical house. The labour is the same either way.

Number of circuits. More circuits means a bigger enclosure and a longer test. Each added circuit, such as an EV charger or cooker, adds £50–£200 or more.

What the testing finds. A standard job takes 4–6 hours. The electrician tests every circuit before and after, and anything that fails has to be fixed or the board cannot be certified. This is where a £600 quote becomes £1,300.

Earthing and bonding. Modern boards need the installation earthed and bonded with green-and-yellow cable to the gas and water pipes. Older houses often don't have it, and bringing it up to standard typically adds £100–£400.

Access and location. A board under the stairs is quick; one in a cellar or boxed in behind fitted furniture takes longer. Moving the board costs from around £400 extra.

Registration. The work is notifiable under Part P. A registered electrician self-certifies it. If you go with an unregistered one, building control fees of £200–£400 are added.

Do you actually need a new one?

This is where a surveyor's eye earns more than any number above. A consumer unit isn't like a boiler that wears out on a schedule. Whether it needs replacing depends on its condition and what protection it provides, not simply its age.

Replace it, in my view, if you have: old rewirable fuses (the porcelain-and-wire type); a board with no RCD protection on circuits that need it; scorching, a burning smell or loose connections; water damage; or no spare capacity for a circuit you are adding. The same applies if an EICR puts a C1 or C2 against the board itself.

You probably don't need to replace a plastic board installed before 2016 that has RCD protection, is in good condition and shows no sign of heat damage. The rule that consumer units in homes must have non-combustible (metal) enclosures, Regulation 421.1.201 from Amendment 3 to the 17th Edition, applied to new work from 1 January 2016, and it is not retrospective. An existing plastic unit can generally stay, including through alterations and additions, unless it shows thermal damage. Some trade sites imply otherwise to sell a replacement, and I wouldn't take their word for it.

Nor is a lack of RCD protection on every circuit automatically an urgent fault. IET guidance says non-compliance with the current edition of BS 7671 does not by itself warrant a defect code, and a socket with no RCD that is unlikely to feed outdoor equipment should usually be coded C3, an improvement recommended, rather than C2. How it is coded depends on the circuit and the inspector's judgement, so ask which code and why before paying for a new board on the strength of one line in a report.

The newest edition, BS 7671:2018+A3:2024, published on 31 July 2024, mainly added definitions for bidirectional and unidirectional protective devices, relevant where solar or battery storage is connected. It doesn't make an existing board non-compliant. If you are adding panels, the board is worth discussing at the same time; see my solar panels cost guide.

Landlords should treat the codes more strictly. In England, if an EICR records a C1, C2 or FI, the landlord must have the remedial or investigative work done by a qualified person within 28 days, or sooner if the report says so, and the local authority can impose a financial penalty of up to £30,000. If a board is the cause, the replacement is a deadline, not an option. My EICR cost guide explains the codes, and if the whole installation is tired, the house rewiring cost guide shows where a board swap stops being enough.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • RCBOs or dual RCD, and why. Ask for the board type in writing. A quote that doesn't say may be the cheapest split-load board available.
  • Metal enclosure. Any new domestic board should be non-combustible. A plastic board being quoted is a red flag.
  • Testing and the certificate. The quote should include testing every circuit, an Electrical Installation Certificate and Part P notification. A price that excludes the certificate isn't a price.
  • Earthing and bonding. Ask whether they have been checked and what happens if they are inadequate. A fixed price for the board with an open-ended "remedial work" line is how £600 becomes £1,300.
  • Registration. Check the electrician's registration (NICEIC, NAPIT or another competent person scheme) on the scheme's own website.
  • Making good. Replastering and decorating around a larger board is usually not included.
  • Surge protection. An SPD adds £50–£120. Ask whether it is in the price or an optional extra, rather than finding out on the day.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to replace a fuse box? In 2026 a new consumer unit typically costs £450–£750 across most of the UK and £700–£1,200 in London and the South East, including the board, testing, the Electrical Installation Certificate and Part P notification. Remedial work is extra.

Is a dual RCD board or an RCBO board better? An RCBO board protects each circuit separately, so a fault trips one circuit rather than half the house. It costs roughly £120–£300 more than a dual RCD (split-load) board. A dual RCD board is compliant and cheaper; for most households I would pay the difference for RCBOs.

Do I have to replace a plastic consumer unit? No. The rule requiring non-combustible (metal) enclosures in homes applies to new work from 1 January 2016 and is not retrospective. An existing plastic board in good condition, with RCD protection, can stay unless it shows heat damage or other defects.

Can I replace a fuse box myself? Replacing a consumer unit is notifiable work under Part P of the Building Regulations in England. It should be done by an electrician registered with a competent person scheme, who certifies it. If you use an unregistered electrician you need to notify building control and pay a fee, typically £200–£400.
