A surveyor’s van does odd hours. It sits on a drive for forty minutes while I go round a house, then outside a flat for an hour, then it does a long run in the dark in November. It also spends days parked while I write reports. Every one of those patterns is hard on a battery and a tyre, and when either gives up it is at the worst moment: an appointment at nine, a vendor at the door, a client who does not want to be told you will be late.

Two small kit items deal with most of it. A lithium jump starter is a battery pack with clamps that starts your engine without a second car and without a pair of leads. A tyre inflator tops up a soft tyre in a car park, which matters more than it sounds, because a van carrying ladders, a drone case and a rucksack of kit runs a different pressure from an empty one. They are not survey tools. They are what stops a bad morning becoming a cancelled day.

None of this replaces breakdown cover, a spare wheel or a proper work light for a roadside job in the dark. If you carry a warning triangle and hi-vis in the van, add these two to the same shelf.

How I judge a jump starter and an inflator

Engine size and fuel. A jump starter has to match the engine. Diesels need more current than petrols of the same size, and a cold morning needs more than a warm one. Read the maker’s engine sizes on the listing, not the headline number, and buy the next size up if you are unsure.

Clamp safety. Reverse-polarity protection is what lets you get the clamps wrong in a dark lay-by without a spark or a damaged ECU. It is the single feature I would not do without.

How it is charged and stored. A lithium pack loses charge in a drawer and does not like extreme heat. A van in July can pass the temperature the maker states, so check the storage range and keep it out of direct sun. Top it up every few months, whether or not you have used it.

Power source. A plug-in inflator uses the van’s 12V socket and never needs charging, but it needs the socket. A cordless one goes anywhere but runs out. Pick the one that matches how you work, not the one with the biggest number.

Accuracy and auto-stop. A digital gauge with a preset that stops the pump at your pressure is much better than a dial you have to watch. Neither is a laboratory instrument, so check the result against a decent gauge now and then.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Peak amps are a marketing number. Some jump packs advertise a peak current far above what they deliver on the clamps. Treat the starting current and the engine sizes the maker lists as the useful figures, and be sceptical of a small, cheap unit that claims the same output as a large one.

Power banks with a jump function are a compromise. A pack that also charges a laptop is handy, but the jump side is usually the weaker half. If a flat battery is the job, buy a jump starter. If you also want a power bank, buy that separately.

Check the tyre pressure at the door. The correct pressure is on a sticker in the door shut or in the handbook, and a loaded van needs the higher figure. Check cold, before you drive, and use a gauge you trust. A tyre that is 8psi down wears the shoulders and uses more fuel.

Know when to stop. If a battery is flat because of a fault, a jump start is a lift to a garage, not a repair. If a tyre is losing air quickly, an inflator buys you a few miles, not a journey. Neither is a reason to drive a damaged tyre to the next appointment.

Do not store a jump pack somewhere hot and sealed. Keep it in the van’s cabin rather than a roof box, and away from loose metal tools in the same bag. It is a lithium cell with a lot of energy in it. The tool backpack that carries your kit is a sensible home if the pack is not left in the sun.

Questions I get asked

What size jump starter do I need for a van? It depends on the engine, not the van. A small petrol engine is fine with 500 amps, and a 2-litre diesel van is better served by 1,000 amps or more. The listing gives the maker’s engine sizes. If you are between two sizes, buy the larger.

Is a jump starter better than jump leads? For one person on their own, yes. You do not need another car, and the clamps with polarity protection are safer than a pair of leads connected in the dark. Jump leads are cheaper and never go flat, so some people carry both.

How often should I charge a lithium jump starter? The usual advice is every three to six months, and after every use. Check the maker’s manual for your unit, because the interval depends on the cell and how it is stored.

Are cheap 12V tyre inflators any good? For topping up, yes. They are slow, noisy and not meant for a continuous run, but they get a soft tyre back to a safe pressure. For a punctured tyre you want a repair kit or a spare, and a call to breakdown cover.
