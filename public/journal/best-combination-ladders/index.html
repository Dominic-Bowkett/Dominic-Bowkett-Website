I already wrote up the telescopic ladder that comes into every house I survey and the step ladder that handles quick indoor jobs. A combination ladder sits between the two: a self-employed trade, a first-home landlord or anyone without a garage full of access kit doesn’t want a third ladder just for a stairwell with no square landing to stand a normal ladder on. One ladder that steps, extends and adjusts to a staircase covers most of what a household or small job actually needs.

The honest trade-off is versatility against purpose-built rigidity. A combination ladder has more hinges and locking pins than a dedicated telescopic or step ladder, because it folds into several shapes rather than one. Every joint has to lock correctly before you climb, and every one adds a little flex a single-purpose ladder doesn’t have. That isn’t a reason to avoid the category — it’s a reason to be clear-eyed about what “5 in 1” is actually buying you.

It’s also a category with a genuine oddity worth knowing before you shop: two ladders here are, as far as the published specifications go, the same physical product under two brand names at two prices. First, the one I’d buy.

How I judge a combination ladder

Genuine configurations, not a marketing count. “3 in 1” on a straightforward combi ladder usually means stepladder, extension and stairwell — three real, different shapes. By the time a listing claims 5 or 6, check what has actually been added: a stairwell platform or a freestanding extension mode is a real extra capability; the same stepladder counted once as “stepladder” and once as “A-frame” is the same mode twice.

EN131 Professional certification and the 150kg benchmark. As with the telescopic and step ladder guides, this is the baseline separating trade-rated kit from occasional-use DIY equipment: 150kg rated, tested for repeated cycling rather than the odd weekend job. A ladder stating only a lower kg figure and no EN131 class — there is one further down this list — is telling you plainly what tier it belongs to.

The stairwell mode specifically. The actual reason to buy a combination ladder rather than two dedicated ones: an adjustable leg or staggered-rung mechanism that lets one stile sit lower on a step below while the other stands on the landing above, levelling the ladder on a staircase. Check it is a genuine sliding adjustment rather than just “can be leant against the wall of a stairwell,” which any ladder can technically do, badly.

Weight and joint count, honestly. Every extra mode is another hinge, another locking pin, another few hundred grams. A 5-in-1 ladder with a platform section will always flex more than a dedicated 3-way, which will in turn flex more than a purpose-built telescopic ladder of the same reach. That is the price of versatility, worth weighing against how often you’d actually use the extra modes.

Same ladder, different badge, different price. Manufacturers who own more than one ladder brand sometimes sell an identical design under both names at different prices. Werner owns Abru in the UK, and two of the picks below share published working heights, weight and product photography down to the pixel. Knowing this before you shop can save real money.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The ‘X in 1’ number is marketing, not a spec. Read what each configuration actually is before comparing counts. A genuine freestanding extension mode or a stairwell platform adds real capability; a stepladder mode and an “A-frame” mode that are the same shape described twice does not. The comparison table above lists what each ladder can actually do, not the number printed on the box.

The stairwell mode is the actual reason to buy this category. If you never need to stand a ladder on a staircase, a dedicated telescopic ladder for reach and a dedicated step ladder for quick indoor jobs will each be lighter, more rigid and cheaper than a combination ladder trying to do both. Buy the combination ladder specifically because you need the stairwell capability, not as a default “does everything” purchase.

More hinges is a genuine trade-off, not just caution for its own sake. Every extra configuration is another locking pin that has to fully engage before you climb. Check every lock on every section before use, every time, regardless of how many times you’ve set the ladder up before — the failure mode is a section that looks locked but isn’t, not a dramatic structural fault.

A stated kg rating with no test standard behind it is not the same thing as EN131. The BPS ladder above states 95kg and nothing else. That isn’t dishonest — it’s exactly the information you need, provided you read it rather than assume every “3 way combination ladder” listing means the same 150kg professional-grade product.

Questions I get asked

Is a combination ladder as good as buying a separate telescopic ladder and step ladder?

For most jobs, no — each dedicated ladder will be lighter and more rigid for its specific task than a combination ladder trying to cover both. The combination ladder earns its place for one specific reason: the stairwell mode, which neither a telescopic ladder nor a standard step ladder can do safely. If you never work on a staircase, the telescopic ladder and step ladder guides will likely serve you better and cheaper.

What do the ‘3 in 1’, ‘4 in 1’ and ‘5 in 1’ numbers actually mean?

In principle, the number of genuinely different shapes the ladder configures into: stepladder, extension and stairwell is the standard three. A fourth mode usually adds a freestanding extension configuration for jobs with nothing to lean against; a fifth typically adds a working platform. Check what the extra number actually adds rather than assuming more is simply better — some sellers count minor variations of the same mode separately to inflate the figure.

Are combination ladders safe to use on stairs?

Yes, when the stairwell mode is used as designed: the adjustable leg mechanism levels the ladder across the step, both feet are fully engaged and locked, and stability is checked before climbing exactly as with any other configuration. The Work at Height Regulations 2005 apply as they do to any ladder — suitable equipment, properly maintained, checked before use. What makes stairwell use specifically risky is skipping that check because the ladder “looks” set up correctly; with several locking points, that assumption is exactly what to avoid.

Why do two ladders in this guide have identical specifications?

Because they are, as far as the published data goes, the same physical ladder. Werner owns the Abru brand in the UK, both are manufactured by WernerCo, and the Werner 7101318 and Abru 2101318 above publish identical working heights and share the same product photography — the same factory tooling sold under two brand names at two price points. Worth a quick specification check before paying the premium for the better-known badge.
