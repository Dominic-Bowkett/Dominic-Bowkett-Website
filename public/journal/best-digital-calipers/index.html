The best
digital calipers
for survey work

A tape measure or laser gets a room to the nearest few millimetres, which is fine for a floor plan and useless for telling a 44mm stud from a 47mm one, or putting a number on a crack that’s either widening or isn’t. A caliper closes that gap — jaws onto a timber section, a cavity tie, a glazing unit’s spacer bar, or a mortar joint, and a reading to a hundredth of a millimetre that a tape simply can’t give. Here’s what actually matters when buying one for site work rather than a machine shop, and seven worth carrying.

A caliper doesn’t come out of the bag on every job. When it does, it’s usually because a tape measure’s nearest-millimetre reading isn’t good enough to answer the actual question — is this stud timber 44mm or the older 47mm, is that cavity wall tie’s protrusion within tolerance, how thick is the spacer bar in a sealed glazing unit, and exactly how wide is a crack that a client’s asking whether to worry about. None of that needs machinist-grade tooling; it needs a caliper that gives a confident reading to a tenth of a millimetre or better and survives living in a tool bag rather than a drawer.

Where this overlaps with existing kit is worth being clear about. A crack monitor tracks movement over weeks or months with a fixed tell-tale or glass gauge left in place; a caliper gives a single precise reading on the day, useful for that same crack before a monitor goes up, or for a hundred other dimensions a monitor was never meant to measure. A tape measure or laser measure still does the room dimensions; the caliper is for the handful of measurements each survey throws up where a millimetre either way changes the answer.

How I judge a digital caliper

Accuracy that matters, not resolution that doesn’t. Every caliper in this guide reads to 0.01mm, but the accuracy behind that reading — usually ±0.02–0.03mm on a decent unit — is already far tighter than any survey description needs. The number worth checking is whether the manufacturer states an accuracy figure at all, not chasing a smaller one.

Stainless steel jaws over painted or plastic ones. A caliper living loose in a tool bag gets dropped, sat on and left in a damp van. Hardened stainless steel shrugs that off; a plastic-bodied caliper marketed on price alone usually doesn’t survive a season of site use.

A display readable in a dim loft, not just a bright workshop. Large LCD figures matter more on a survey than they do on a bench, where the tool's usually read under a fixed light rather than a head torch beam in a crawl space.

Zero-set at any jaw position. Being able to zero the display with the jaws already closed on a reference point turns the caliper into a comparative gauge — useful for checking how much a crack has opened relative to a fixed mark, not just an absolute dimension.

A battery type worth carrying a spare of. SR44, LR44 and CR2032 cover most of the calipers here and are all cheap and common, but a flat battery mid-survey is still a flat battery. Worth keeping a spare in the same pocket as the caliper rather than assuming it’ll last.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Sub-0.01mm resolution is a spec-sheet number, not a survey requirement. Every caliper here reads to 0.01mm, and every one of them is already far more precise than any survey description needs to be. The number worth comparing is the stated accuracy — usually ±0.02–0.03mm — not chasing a smaller resolution figure that won’t change what gets written in a report.

Calibration certificates matter for a minority of jobs, not most of them. A UKAS or manufacturer’s calibration certificate is worth having if a measurement might end up disputed — a boundary line, a party wall matter, or anything heading toward expert witness territory. For a routine condition survey, it’s an unnecessary cost.

Digital is faster to read, but a dead battery is a dead caliper. Every unit in this guide is digital rather than a plain vernier or dial gauge — faster to read at a glance, but useless the moment the battery dies mid-survey. Worth carrying a spare in the same pocket as the caliper itself.

Fractions and inch displays are largely irrelevant for UK survey work. Several calipers here advertise fraction conversion aimed at a US or hobbyist market. It doesn’t hurt to have it, but it's no reason to pick one caliper over another when every measurement in a UK report is in millimetres.

A 150mm range covers almost everything a survey needs. Longer-range calipers exist, but jaw width and depth beyond 150mm rarely come up on site — timber sections, cavity ties, glazing spacers and crack widths are all comfortably within range. A bigger caliper just means more to carry for capability that goes unused.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a caliper if I already carry a tape measure and a laser measure?

For room dimensions and floor plans, no — a tape or laser measure remains the right tool. A caliper earns its place for the smaller number of measurements where a millimetre either way changes the answer: identifying a timber section size, checking a glazing unit’s spacer bar width for an approximate age or U-value estimate, or putting a precise figure on a crack width rather than an estimate. It’s a specialist tool for a specific job, not a tape replacement.

What’s the difference between a digital caliper and a crack monitor?

A crack monitor is fixed in place across a crack and read repeatedly over weeks or months to track whether it’s actively moving. A caliper gives one precise reading on the day, useful before deciding whether a crack needs monitoring at all, or for measuring anything else that isn’t a crack. The two do different jobs and often get used together on the same defect.

How accurate does a measurement need to be for a survey report?

Most survey descriptions work to the nearest millimetre, sometimes less precisely than that. Sub-millimetre accuracy matters less for the number itself than for the confidence it gives — distinguishing two similar timber sizes, or placing a crack reading clearly within one BRE damage category rather than borderline between two. Any caliper in this guide comfortably delivers that; the differences between them are mostly about durability and price, not accuracy.

Will a budget caliper actually be accurate enough to trust?

Generally yes, at this specific measurement range. Several budget calipers here quote the same ±0.02mm accuracy as calipers costing several times more — the difference tends to be build quality, display size and how long the caliper survives being dropped, not whether the reading itself can be trusted. Where it's worth spending more is if a measurement might ever need a calibration certificate behind it, which none of the budget options offer.

A caliper reading doesn’t replace judgement, but a precise number behind a defect — a crack width, a timber section, a joint gap — carries more weight in a building survey than an estimate does.
