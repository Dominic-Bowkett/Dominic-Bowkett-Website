The best
ladder accessories
for site access

A ladder gets you up. Whether it stays put is a separate problem, and this guide covers four different accessories that solve four different parts of it — a stand-off bracket protects the guttering the top is resting against, a leveller or stabiliser sorts out the ground the feet are standing on, and a roof rack clamp keeps the whole thing secured to the van between jobs. None of them do each other’s job. Here is what each one actually fixes, and six worth buying.

Every external elevation I look at starts with getting a ladder somewhere it doesn’t really want to be: against a UPVC fascia with no proper stay point, on a sloped block-paved drive, propped over a flower bed that turns out to be soft clay under a thin skin of bark chip. By that point the ladder itself is rarely the weak link — it’s whatever the top two feet are leaning against, or whatever the bottom four feet are standing on, that decides how the next twenty minutes go.

None of the accessories in this guide do the same job as each other, which is worth being clear about before buying any of them. A stand-off bracket keeps the stiles off guttering and downpipes rather than resting the ladder’s full weight on a section of plastic that was never designed to take it. A leveller or a stabiliser tray solves the opposite end — the feet, on ground that isn’t flat, isn’t hard, or both. A roof rack clamp has nothing to do with using the ladder at all: it’s what stops a nine-metre extension ladder becoming a hazard on the A22, or simply not being there when you get to the job. Buy the one that matches the actual problem in front of you, not the one with the most confident name on the listing.

None of this is exotic engineering — a bracket, a set of rubber pads, a locking clamp — which is exactly why the manufacturer’s own spec sheet tells you most of what you need before buying. The verdicts below are built from those specifications and from published buyer feedback, not from carrying each one up a ladder myself.

How I judge a ladder accessory

Which failure mode it actually fixes. A ladder can fail at the top — slipping sideways, crushing a gutter, sliding down smooth render — or at the bottom, where a foot skids on a hard surface or sinks into soft ground. A stand-off bracket only ever addresses the top; a leveller or stabiliser only ever addresses the bottom. Buying one because it’s cheaper than the other, when the actual problem is at the opposite end, fixes nothing.

A stated figure, not just ‘heavy duty’. A genuine load or footprint number matters more than the adjective printed on the box. The Sealey stabiliser above states the actual footprint increase in cm²; some of the stand-off brackets further down state a warranty length rather than a working load, which is different information and worth reading as such rather than assuming it means the same thing.

Fit checked before ordering, not after. ‘Universal, fits most ladders’ is printed on almost everything in this category. Stile spacing, tube diameter and rung profile vary enough between a budget DIY extension ladder and a trade-grade EN131 unit that a universal claim is a starting point to check against your own ladder’s dimensions, not a guarantee.

What it’s actually protecting. A stand-off bracket is as much about the building as the ladder — keeping weight off a plastic gutter or a downpipe joint that will crack under it. A roof rack clamp is about the ladder and the van, not the job at all: security and safe transport, a concern nowhere else in a typical tool or PPE kit.

Whether the mechanism adjusts, or just pads. A rubber mat with nodules compresses slightly on hard ground and adds grip, but does nothing about a genuine slope; a stabiliser tray or a hinged leg actually changes the geometry underneath the ladder. Know which one you’re buying before assuming either solves a sloped drive.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The base is the more common failure, but the stand-off is the more common accessory. HSE’s own ladder guidance consistently points at the base — a foot slipping or sinking — as the more frequent cause of a fall, yet stand-off brackets are the accessory most readily sold alongside a new ladder. Buy for the ground underfoot as often as the wall in front of you, not just whichever accessory happens to be on the shelf next to the ladder.

‘Fits most ladders’ is a starting point, not a guarantee. Check stile spacing and rung profile against your own ladder before ordering any bracket or clamp in this category. A universal claim on a listing photograph rarely comes with dimensions attached, and the failure mode is finding out on site rather than before it arrives.

A mat compresses; a stabiliser tray adjusts the geometry. The BPS and HITEGEAR kits both spread load and add grip, but neither one changes the ladder’s actual angle on a real slope the way a rigid, pegged-down tray does. If the ground genuinely isn’t level, rather than just soft or loose, a mat alone is the wrong tool for it.

A clamp without a lock is a strap with extra steps. The theft-prevention case for a roof rack clamp only holds if it actually locks. Check whether padlocks are included, as with the TB Davies pair above, or sold separately, before assuming a clamp alone keeps a ladder on the van overnight.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a ladder stand-off if I’m not working near guttering?

Probably not for every job. A stand-off bracket earns its place specifically where the top of the ladder would otherwise rest on plastic guttering, a downpipe, or fragile render — a solid fascia board or a plain brick corner doesn’t need one. Fit it for the jobs where the alternative is resting the ladder’s full weight on something that will crack, and leave it off the rest.

What’s the difference between a leveller and a stabiliser tray like the Sealey?

A mat-based leveller such as the BPS or HITEGEAR kits above spreads load and adds grip under each foot individually. A stabiliser tray like the Sealey LAD001 goes further, physically widening the ladder’s footprint into a single rigid base with its own tie-down point. Both address the same failure mode — the base moving — but the tray is the more thorough, and more expensive, answer to it.

Are universal roof rack ladder clamps actually secure against theft?

Only as secure as the lock fitted to them. A clamp with no padlock, or with a cheap keyed washer instead of one, mainly stops the ladder shifting in transit rather than stopping anyone taking it. Buy a pair with genuine padlocks included, as with the TB Davies clamps above, if security rather than just safe transport is the point.

Can I use a stand-off bracket and a leveller at the same time?

Yes, and on an external elevation with a fragile gutter above and soft ground below, that’s the correct setup rather than a belt-and-braces one. They solve opposite ends of the same ladder, and neither substitutes for the other.
