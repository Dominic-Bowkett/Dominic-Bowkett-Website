Limits first, because with electricity the limits are the point. I am a surveyor, not an electrician. On an EPC or a building survey I look at the consumer unit, note its type and apparent age, check the supply is present — and where anything looks wrong, the report says “electrician” and means it. Nothing here is an invitation to open a consumer unit or work on a circuit. This is a guide to the small family of instruments that let you make basic electrical checks safely — is this socket wired correctly, is there voltage here, is this battery flat — and to the instruments that only look like they do that job.

The most important idea comes from the trade: safe isolation. When an electrician needs a circuit dead, they don’t trust the switch, the label in the board, or a glowing pen. They prove their tester on a known live source (that’s what a proving unit is for), test the circuit with a two-pole voltage tester, then prove the tester again. Prove, test, prove. Every serious instrument in this guide serves that discipline, and HSE guidance note GS38 — the document specifying finger guards, insulated leads and barely-there probe tips — is the standard the good ones are built to.

And the neon screwdriver — the pocket screwdriver with the bulb in the handle that every toolbox in Britain seems to contain — works by passing current from the live part through your body to earth. It has no meaningful insulation category, it stays dark when the bulb fails or the contact is poor, and a dark bulb reads as “safe”. GS38 warns against them in terms. It belongs in the bin.

How I judge a voltage tester

Two poles, always. A two-pole tester measures between two points — live and neutral, live and earth — the way voltage actually exists. Single-pole devices (neon screwdrivers, and to a lesser degree non-contact pens) infer voltage from capacitive leakage, sometimes through you. The trade proves dead with two poles because a two-pole reading is a measurement; everything else is a hint.

GS38 features. Finger barriers on the probes, fully insulated leads, and exposed metal tips of 4mm or less (2mm is better). These details exist because the failure mode isn’t “wrong reading” — it’s a slipped probe bridging two terminals in a live board. If a tester’s marketing doesn’t mention GS38, its designers weren’t thinking about UK electricians.

Works when the batteries don’t. The best two-pole testers indicate voltage on LEDs powered by the circuit under test, so a flat battery can’t silently turn “live” into “dead”. Some — the Martindale VI13800 is the type specimen — contain no batteries and no fuse at all, which means nothing inside can fail towards a false “safe”. For a device whose whole job is telling you it’s safe to touch something, that logic matters more than any feature.

An honest CAT rating. Overvoltage categories describe where on the supply an instrument can safely face a transient spike: CAT II is appliances and socket outlets, CAT III is the fixed installation and distribution boards, CAT IV is the origin — the cutout, the meter tails. The voltage after the CAT matters too: CAT III 600V beats CAT II 1000V near a board. On premium brands the rating is independently certified; on £20 internet specials it is, at best, an aspiration printed on the case.

Provable. Whatever you buy must be checked against a known live source before and after the reading you care about. That can be a proving unit, and for anything beyond casual use it should be — which is why one is in this list.

This is the electrical check that genuinely is within a surveyor’s (or a landlord’s, or a house-hunter’s) remit: plug it into a 13A socket and three LEDs plus an audible tone report whether live, neutral and earth are where they should be — correct, reversed polarity, or a missing conductor. What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Know where checking ends. Worth restating without the product talk. Legitimate territory for a surveyor, landlord or capable homeowner: is the supply present, does the socket tester show correct wiring, is the meter battery flat, does the pen suggest this cable is live so I’ll keep away from it. Electrician-and-EICR territory: anything involving opening the consumer unit, testing circuits, earth loop impedance, RCD performance, or deciding an installation is safe. An EICR — Electrical Installation Condition Report — is a few hundred pounds from a registered electrician, it’s already mandatory for private rentals in England, and no quantity of Amazon test gear substitutes for it. My reports flag electrical concerns; they never clear them.

CAT ratings, decoded once more. The category is about transient spikes, not steady volts: mains circuits carry brief multi-kilovolt transients from switching and lightning, and the closer to the supply origin, the fatter the spike. CAT II covers plug-in appliance level, CAT III fixed wiring and distribution boards, CAT IV the supply side — cutout, tails, meter. An instrument facing a transient beyond its category can flash over internally, and with your hand on it. For anything used near a consumer unit, CAT III 600V from a certified brand is the sensible floor — and certification (a genuine UKCA/CE backed by TUV, UL or similar marks) is what separates a rating from a sticker.

What the marketing pushes that you can skip. For basic checks: True RMS (matters for distorted waveforms in industrial settings, not for “is this live”), 20,000 counts (precision beyond any question you’re asking), Bluetooth apps and data logging. The unglamorous things — lead quality, finger barriers, fused or current-limited design, a self-test, a case that survives the van — are where the money should go. A £50 two-pole tester with GS38 leads is safer and more useful than a £50 multimeter with a spec sheet the length of this page.

One tester does not make you an electrician. The trade joke about the most dangerous thing on site being a labourer with a multimeter exists for a reason. The instruments here answer narrow questions safely. The moment the question becomes “why is it wired like this?” or “can I move this?”, the answer is a registered electrician, and in England any notifiable work falls under Part P of the Building Regulations besides.

Questions I get asked

Can I use a multimeter to check if a wire is live? A quality multimeter set to AC volts can read voltage, but electricians don’t prove dead with one, and the reasons apply double to amateurs: a multimeter left on ohms or amps and touched to a live circuit can fail explosively; a wrong range, a flat battery or a broken lead can all read “0V” on a live conductor; and budget meters lack certified input protection. A two-pole voltage tester has one job, fails safe, and costs less than the multimeter’s replacement fuses. Use the multimeter for batteries and continuity; use a two-pole tester for “live or dead”.

What voltage tester do electricians actually use? A GS38-compliant two-pole voltage indicator or tester — the Fluke T-series, Kewtech, Martindale and Di-LOG units you see on every site — proved against a proving unit before and after each test. That’s the safe-isolation procedure the industry codified after too many people trusted a switch or a screwdriver bulb. Non-contact pens are carried too, but for triage, never for proving dead.

Are non-contact voltage testers reliable? They’re reliable at what they do — sensing the AC field around an unscreened live conductor — and unreliable as a safety decision, because absence of a beep is not absence of voltage. Screened cable, metal capping, depth, even probe orientation can silence one over a live cable, and conversely induced voltage makes them chirp on dead conductors that run alongside live ones. Buy one with a self-test (that’s the case for the Fluke over the bargain bin), use it to decide where to be careful, and never let it tell you something is safe to touch.

Do I need a proving unit? If a tester’s reading will ever be the reason you touch a conductor, yes. The logic is unforgiving: a tester that shows nothing is either reading a dead circuit or is itself broken, and you cannot tell which without a known live source. A proving unit is that source in your pocket — no hunting for a live socket that may itself be on the circuit you just isolated. For plug-in socket checks and battery testing you can live without one; for anything resembling isolation, it’s the cheapest life insurance in the toolbag.
