A third-floor flat with no lift turns a straightforward inspection into a fitness test before the front door is even open. The instruments that make up a normal kit — a tripod, a telescopic ladder or ladder bag, a tool bag, a moisture meter case, a laptop for notes — add up to more than one person can carry in a single trip, and a block of flats rarely has anywhere convenient to leave the second load while the first one goes up. Two or three trips up four flights, twice over, before the survey itself has even started, is a genuine cost in time and in a back that has to do the same thing again tomorrow.

A sack truck turns that into one trip instead of three, provided it can actually manage stairs rather than merely tolerate a kerb. That distinction matters more than most of the spec sheet: a dedicated stair-climbing cart uses a cluster of small wheels arranged in a triangle or star, so the load rolls step to step instead of being dragged or lifted down a flight by hand. An ordinary two-wheeled sack truck, however heavy-duty the branding, still has to be tilted, carried and lowered one step at a time — fine on a driveway and into a boot, a liability on a communal staircase with a loaded tripod bag balanced on top.

Six compared below — three genuine stair climbers, and three standard folding sack trucks for the survey work that never sees a staircase at all, a house with drive access, or simply loading the van at the end of the day. First, the one I’d buy.

How I judge a sack truck

Genuine stair-climbing wheels, not just heavy-duty branding. A triangular or star-shaped wheel cluster rolls a load step to step under its own geometry; an ordinary two-wheeled truck with bigger tyres and a “heavy duty” label still has to be walked down a flight by hand, one step at a time. The two categories get marketed side by side, and only one of them actually solves the flats-without-lifts problem.

A folded size that earns its place in the van. A stair truck that only comes out once a month for a specific job needs to fold flat enough to live behind the seats or under the racking without displacing the ladder and tool boxes that are used every day.

Load capacity with headroom, not against the limit. A tripod bag, a tool bag and a ladder stacked together rarely exceeds 30–40kg, so a truck rated to 150–200kg is more stable at that load than one rated to 70–90kg and already close to its ceiling — capacity buys margin, not an excuse to load it to the limit.

Solid tyres over pneumatic, for something that sits idle between jobs. A sack truck used weekly rather than daily can sit in a cold van for a fortnight between outings, and a slow puncture found on the one morning it’s needed defeats the purpose entirely. Puncture-proof solid rubber costs nothing in ride quality that matters here.

A handle and grip that work one-handed on a step edge. The other hand is usually on a door, a stair rail, or a torch, so a handle that needs both hands to steer defeats half the point of a stair-climbing cart.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Rated capacity is headroom, not a target. A tripod bag, a tool bag and a folded ladder stacked on one truck rarely comes close to 40kg, so the difference between a 90kg-rated truck and a 200kg-rated one is stability and frame stiffness at that real-world load, not whether it can technically take more weight than it will ever see.

Stair-climbing wheels only earn their keep if stairs are actually the job. For a practice built around houses with drive access, the extra bulk, weight and price of a dedicated stair climber solves a problem that doesn’t exist day to day — a plain folding truck that fits neatly alongside the van storage boxes and the telescopic ladder is the better buy for that pattern of work.

Solid tyres beat pneumatic for anything stored between jobs. A pneumatic wheel rides slightly softer over broken paving, but a slow puncture discovered on the one morning the truck is needed is a worse outcome than a firmer ride every other day — every truck in this comparison uses solid or puncture-proof wheels for exactly that reason.

Included bungee cords are a genuine convenience, not a gimmick. Securing a tripod bag on top of a stacked tool bag properly, rather than trusting it to balance, is the difference between one trip and a dropped instrument case halfway up a flight — worth checking for on any truck being bought specifically for stairs.

Questions I get asked

Do I actually need a stair-climbing sack truck, or will a standard one do?

It depends entirely on how often flats without lifts come up. For a practice that’s mostly houses with driveway access, a standard folding truck like the Draper or Loadit above covers everything that’s actually needed, and the extra weight and price of stair-climbing wheels solves a problem that isn’t there. Where flats and maisonettes are a regular part of the diary, the time saved on even one job a fortnight justifies the difference.

What’s the safe way to get a loaded sack truck down a flight of stairs?

Slowly, and under control rather than gravity — the truck should be doing the work of holding the load, not being allowed to run ahead of it. Keep the heaviest item lowest and best secured, take stairs one at a time rather than trying to save time by rolling over two steps at once, and back down a steep flight rather than leading with the load where the staircase and the truck’s design allow it.

How much should I actually load onto one for a single trip?

Comfortably under the rated capacity rather than up to it, particularly on stairs. A tripod, a tool bag and a ladder bag together rarely exceed 30–40kg, which is well within what every truck in this comparison is rated for on flat ground; the stair-climbing rating, usually roughly half the flat-ground figure, is the number worth watching if the load is going down a flight rather than across a car park.

Solid or pneumatic tyres for something that mostly sits in the van?

Solid or puncture-proof rubber, without much debate. Pneumatic tyres ride a little smoother over rough ground but need occasional attention to stay inflated, and a tool that’s only used a few times a month is exactly the kind of thing that finds a slow puncture at the worst possible moment. Every truck recommended here uses solid or puncture-proof wheels for that reason.
