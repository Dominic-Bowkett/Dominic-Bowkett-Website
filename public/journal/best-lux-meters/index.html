The best
lux meters
for site & workplace lighting checks

A lux meter is a small instrument that answers a question a lot of survey work touches on sideways: is there actually enough light in this room, at this workstation, on this stair? Office fit-outs get measured against BS EN 12464-1’s task-lighting figures, HMO habitable rooms get judged on natural light adequacy, and a homeowner worried about a proposed extension wants to know what a neighbour’s daylight loss might mean before it gets to a formal assessment. Here is what earns a place in the kit for that job, from a compact pocket meter to the trade instrument with a calibration certificate in the box, and what the spec sheet is actually worth paying for.

Most of what I measure on a survey has a hard number attached to it — a moisture reading, a pressure differential, a crack width. Light is different. Nobody’s eye is a reliable instrument for it: a room can feel bright enough on a sunny afternoon and read well under the figure a workplace assessment needs, or feel gloomy under artificial light that’s perfectly adequate on paper. A lux meter takes the guesswork out of that, and it comes up in three quite different jobs.

The clearest is a workplace lighting check against BS EN 12464-1, the standard that sets task-area illuminance for offices, workshops and factories — 500 lux for general office work, up to 750–1,000 lux for technical drawing or fine assembly, down to 300 lux for general factory space. The second is a landlord compliance question with no lux figure actually attached to it: HMO habitable-room standards are built around glazed area, not light readings, but a meter still earns its keep making the case in writing when a room’s daylight is borderline. The third is the one I get asked about most — a homeowner or neighbour worried about daylight loss from a proposed extension or loft conversion, wanting a sense-check before it turns into a formal BS EN 17037 daylight assessment run by a specialist consultant.

None of those jobs need a laboratory-grade photometer. What they need is a meter that reads accurately off-axis, doesn’t get its own reading shadowed by the hand holding it, and holds a stable figure long enough to write down. That’s the shortlist below.

How I judge a lux meter

Cosine correction, not just a headline range. Light rarely hits a sensor straight-on, and a sensor without cosine correction over-reads or under-reads light arriving at an angle — near a window, under an angled fitting, on a sloped rooflight. A manufacturer stating the sensor is cosine and colour corrected is telling you the reading holds up off-axis, not just facing the source directly.

A sensor that isn’t shadowed by the meter itself. BS EN 12464-1 readings are taken at the working plane — roughly desk height, not the floor — and a bulky meter body held close to that plane can shadow its own sensor. A remote sensor on a cable, or a genuinely slim head, avoids the reading being quietly wrong because of how it was taken.

Range matched to the job, not a single big number. Every reading in this guide’s three job contexts sits under 1,000 lux — HSE’s HSG38 guidance puts rough construction work at a 20 lux minimum and a 50 lux aim, precise work at 100 lux minimum and 200 lux aim, and BS EN 12464-1’s office and workshop figures top out around 1,000 lux for fine assembly. A 400,000 lux ceiling only matters for a direct-sun outdoor comparison, not an indoor task check.

Min/Max or Hold, for a reading that won’t sit still. A reading taken under a flickering fluorescent tube, or outdoors as cloud moves across the sun, needs holding or averaging rather than read off a jumping display. Peak and relative modes do the same job for a reading that needs to catch a momentary high or a change between two points.

A stated accuracy figure or calibration certificate, for a reading that has to stand up in a report. A workplace lighting complaint or an HMO licensing dispute is worth backing with a traceable figure rather than a phone photo of a display — the difference between a meter stating ±3 lux against a factory calibration certificate and one stating only a wide accuracy band.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A 400,000 lux ceiling is an outdoor-daylight spec, not an indoor one. Every job in this guide — office task lighting, HMO daylight adequacy, a rough site-lighting check — reads well under 1,000 lux. The wide-range units earn that headroom for a direct-sun outdoor comparison, not for anything measured indoors.

Bluetooth and app export is convenience, not compliance. A dated, exported reading is tidy for a report, but a written figure with a photo of the display has done the job for years and remains acceptable for most of the checks this guide covers.

A calibration certificate matters when a reading has to stand up in a dispute, not for a sanity check. The Testo 540’s factory certificate is worth the premium when a lighting complaint or licensing disagreement needs a traceable figure behind it. For a quick check of whether a room feels darker than it should, any meter here is adequate.

Lux and colour rendering are different measurements. A lux meter reads illuminance — how much light is falling on a surface — not colour temperature or CRI. A room can read a perfectly adequate lux figure under lighting that still looks flat or unflattering.

A lux reading isn’t a daylight assessment. A formal daylight or sunlight impact assessment for a planning application is run under BS EN 17037 by a specialist consultant, using climate-based modelling or a daylight-factor calculation, not a spot reading. What a meter gives a homeowner or surveyor on site is a comparative sanity check, not a substitute for that assessment.

Questions I get asked

What lux level does BS EN 12464-1 actually require?

It depends on the task, not just the room. The standard steps through a fixed scale of illuminance values — 20, 30, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 300, 500, 750, 1000 lux and upward — and assigns a figure to the activity: 500 lux for general office work, 300 lux for general factory and workshop space, 750–1,000 lux for technical drawing or fine assembly. Reading the wrong task figure for the room is the most common way this gets misapplied.

Is there a minimum lux level for a bedroom or living room in an HMO?

Not as a standalone figure in most councils’ HMO standards — the yardstick is usually a glazed area equivalent to at least a tenth of the room’s floor area, with a direct view out, and a fallback to adequate artificial lighting where natural light isn’t practicable. A lux reading doesn’t replace that test, but it’s useful evidence where a room’s daylight is genuinely borderline.

Where should a lux reading actually be taken?

At the working plane — roughly desk or worktop height, not the floor — and away from the surveyor’s own shadow or the meter body blocking the sensor. That’s the practical case for a remote-sensor meter like the Extech LT300 over a fixed-head one: the reading comes from where the work happens, not from wherever the meter body sits.

Does a lux meter reading count as a proper daylight assessment for a planning objection?

No. A formal daylight or sunlight assessment for a planning application is run under BS EN 17037, which replaced BS 8206-2 in 2019, using daylight-factor calculations or climate-based modelling by a specialist consultant. A spot lux reading is a useful comparative check — is this room clearly darker than it used to be, or than a similar room next door — not evidence that stands in for that assessment.
