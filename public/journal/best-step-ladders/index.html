Not every job needs the telescopic ladder out of the boot. Checking a smoke alarm on a hallway ceiling, reading a consumer unit rating, glancing into a shallow airing cupboard, or getting a phone camera square-on to a ceiling crack — a good chunk of what happens indoors on a survey calls for something you can open in a doorway, stand on for thirty seconds, and fold flat again. That is a step ladder’s job, and it is a genuinely different tool with different failure modes to a telescopic ladder, which is why it gets its own guide rather than a footnote on that one.

The use cases split into three. Platform steps with a proper standing area and tool tray are what I’d reach for on anything that takes more than a few seconds — a loft hatch in a bungalow with a lower ceiling, photographing a roof void, or working at a fascia from indoors. Swingback steps, climbable from either side, earn their keep on a stairwell or landing where a one-sided ladder cannot be positioned squarely. And fibreglass matters the moment the job is near a live consumer unit or meter cupboard, where aluminium stiles are the wrong material full stop.

The certification picture on step ladders is less dramatic than on telescopic ladders, but the same principle holds: EN131 splits into Professional and Non-professional classes, and a fair few of the folding steel stools sold as “step ladders” on Amazon are actually certified to EN14183, the lighter domestic-stool standard, not EN131 at all. Neither is dishonest — a £24 folding stool was never meant to carry a surveyor and a laptop bag eight times a day — but the two categories get marketed side by side as if they were interchangeable, and they are not. How I judge a step ladder

EN131 Professional, if it is going to work. Class 1 (Professional) is rated to 150kg and tested for repeated daily use; Class 3 (Non-professional/Domestic) and the EN14183 step-stool standard are rated for occasional home use. Both are legitimate categories — they are just answering different questions. If the steps are going into the boot for daily paid work, Professional is the baseline; if it is a stool for the odd smoke-alarm battery, EN14183 is honestly fine and considerably cheaper.

Platform height matched to the actual job. The marketing photographs favour the tallest model in the range. In practice, a standard domestic ceiling wants a platform around 0.8–1m; anything taller is for high ceilings, stairwells or genuine roof-void work, and just adds bulk you carry through a hallway for no reason.

Weight and footprint for a boot that is already full. The step ladder shares car space with a telescopic ladder, a tool bag, a damp meter case and a laptop. Anything under 8kg that folds genuinely flat is worth a premium over a bulkier unit that is £20 cheaper.

Aluminium or fibreglass, deliberately. Aluminium is lighter and cheaper and fine for the overwhelming majority of survey work. Fibreglass is heavier and pricier and exists for one reason: it does not conduct electricity. Buy it if consumer units and meter cupboards are a regular part of the job, not as a default.

A platform, not just a top tread. Standing on the top tread of ordinary steps is the manufacturer’s own instructions telling you not to. A proper platform — a flat standing area one or two rungs down from the very top, sized to take a full boot — is the difference between steps you can work from for a minute and steps you can only balance on.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The certification tells you what the ladder is actually for, not how good it looks. An EN131 Professional platform unit and an EN14183 domestic stool can look almost identical in a listing photo — folding aluminium or steel steps with a couple of treads — and cost wildly different amounts for a reason that has nothing to do with finish. One is built and tested for a tradesperson standing on it repeatedly, five days a week; the other is built for a homeowner reaching the top shelf twice a year. Read the certification, not the star rating, before deciding which one you actually need.

Maximum height is oversold here too. As with telescopic ladders, the marketing always shows the tallest model in the range fully open. Indoors, a platform around 0.8–1m clears almost every domestic ceiling for hatch and alarm work; buying taller “just in case” mostly buys you something heavier to carry through a hallway.

A handrail is a genuine safety feature, not a marketing add-on. On anything with a platform above head height, something to hold onto while you climb the last step or turn to reach a hatch reduces the single biggest risk with step ladders: overreaching sideways instead of moving the ladder. It is not a legal requirement, but on the taller platform units it is worth paying for.

Fibreglass is a specific-purpose material, not an upgrade. It is heavier, pricier, and buys you exactly one thing: non-conductivity near live electrics. If consumer units and meter cupboards are not part of your week, aluminium is the right choice and fibreglass is just dead weight in the boot.

Things the listings push that you can ignore: “3-in-1” or “multi-position” combination ladders marketed as step ladders — they are rarely as stable in step-ladder mode as a purpose-built platform unit, because the hinge that lets them reconfigure is also a point of flex. Also skip anything that leads with paint colour or an “XL” suffix on an otherwise unremarkable domestic stool — check the certification and the platform height first, and let those decide it.

Inspect what you use for work, and write it down. The Work at Height Regulations 2005 apply to step ladders exactly as they do to any other access equipment: the kit has to be suitable, maintained, and checked before use. A monthly five-minute routine — feet, hinges, locking arms, platform surface — and a note that you did it is the whole of the paperwork this actually requires.

Questions I get asked

What height step ladder do I need for indoor work?

Less than most listings push. A standard UK ceiling is 2.3–2.4m, and a platform around 0.8–1m on a 4-tread unit reaches a loft hatch, a smoke alarm or a high shelf with the standing platform at a comfortable working height and your head well clear of the ceiling. Go taller — 1.2–1.7m platforms — only for genuinely high ceilings, stairwells, or roof-void work where you need to stand for longer. Buying the tallest model “to be safe” mostly buys you a ladder that is awkward to store and slower to set up for jobs that never needed the extra height.

Aluminium or fibreglass step ladder — which should I buy?

Aluminium for almost everything: it is lighter, cheaper, and there is no safety downside for general indoor work away from electrics. Fibreglass earns its considerably higher price and weight in one specific situation — working at or near a live consumer unit, meter cupboard, or exposed wiring, where a non-conductive stile is the whole point. If that is not a regular part of your work, buy aluminium and put the difference towards something else.

What is EN131 Professional, and how is it different from EN14183?

EN131 is the European ladder standard, split since 2018 into Professional and Non-professional classes; Professional is rated to 150kg and tested for repeated, frequent use, which is what trade work does to a ladder day after day. EN14183 is a separate, lighter standard specifically for domestic step stools — short folding steps meant for occasional home use rather than daily trade work. Both are legitimate certifications for what they cover; the mistake is assuming a cheap EN14183 stool is a budget version of an EN131 Professional platform ladder. It isn’t tested to the same regime, and it isn’t meant to be used the same way.

Can step ladders be used for professional or trade work?

Yes, provided they are EN131 Professional class and treated as work equipment under the Work at Height Regulations 2005 — suitable for the task, maintained, and checked before use. In practice that means buying Professional-certified platform steps for daily use (every model in the comparison table except the two EN14183 stools), doing a quick pre-use check, and keeping a simple inspection record. The EN14183 stools have their place — a quick smoke-alarm check does not need trade-rated platform steps — but they should not be the ladder on a formal risk assessment for repeated professional use.

The step ladder handles the quick indoor jobs; the telescopic ladder handles the loft hatches that need real reach. Between the two, most of a domestic EPC or building survey gets covered without touching scaffolding.
