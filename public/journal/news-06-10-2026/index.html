Surveyor’s notes
6 October 2026:
database fee

A landlord database that costs far more than promised, a party conference pledge to scrap the Future Homes Standard, a fire safety sentence and a landlord survey. Here is what each changes on the ground in the South East.

Published 6 October 2026 Reading time · 6 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Today is mostly about landlords being asked to pay, comply and prove things, with one political pledge about new homes thrown in. The common thread is paperwork: who holds the certificates, who has to upload them and what it costs when they are missing.

The landlord database is £65 a property, and it wants your certificates

As LandlordZONE reports, the NRLA says the annual fee for the private rented sector database is almost seven times the original estimate of £28.58 a property, which was to be paid once every three years. The article puts the minimum total cost to landlords at £327 million a year and quotes the NRLA’s Ben Beadle calling it “a costly mess”. The NRLA is a lobbying body, so read the tone with that in mind, but the £65 figure matches Winkworth’s guide to the database. That guide says registration starts on 15 December 2026 in the West Midlands and rolls out region by region, with civil penalties of up to £7,000 for a breach and up to £40,000 for false information or letting without an entry.

Winkworth lists the documents landlords should have ready: gas safety record, electrical installation condition report, EPC, deposit protection details and any licences. The LandlordZONE piece says gas and electrical certificates have to be uploaded manually, which is the part the NRLA objects to. I have not read the draft regulations myself, so check the government’s own text before acting on any detail here.

My read is that the fee is the smaller issue. The database makes the EPC a compliance document in a way it has never quite been for small landlords. An expired EPC, or one from 2012 that nobody has looked at since, now sits in a register a council can check. Landlords with older stock should find the certificate, check its date and see what band it shows, because that is the number the energy rules will be measured against.

The Conservatives say they would scrap the Future Homes Standard

Inside Housing reports, and Housing Today covers, that at the Conservative conference in Birmingham on 5 October the party set out plans to replace Section 106 and the Community Infrastructure Levy with a single levy, scrap the Future Homes Standard, and abolish Natural England and the Environment Agency. The party claims the changes would cut the cost of a new home by up to £50,000. Both outlets attribute the announcement to Shadow Chancellor Andrew Griffith, with Inside Housing also quoting the shadow housing secretary, Katie Lam. The two reports do not agree on when the Standard takes effect, so I will not give a date.

This is an opposition pledge, not policy, and the £50,000 figure is the party’s own claim. I have not seen the working behind it. It is also worth saying that the standard exists largely to stop developers building houses that need retrofitting within a decade. Every home built without good insulation and low-carbon heating is one that someone, eventually, pays to upgrade. As a retrofit assessor I see that bill from the other side.

For a small developer or self-builder in the South East, the sensible advice is not to change your design on the strength of a conference speech. Build to the regulations that apply when your application goes in, and if your scheme is marginal on cost, ask whether insulation and airtightness are really where the money goes. In my experience they are not usually the biggest line.

A £246,000 fire safety bill is a reminder of what landlords owe

According to LandlordZONE, a landlord has received a suspended jail term, and his company a fine, over fire safety failings at two Lancashire properties, Belgrave Chapel in Darwen and Victoria Hall in Bacup. The article says the offences were under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 and that inspectors found obstructed escape routes, damaged fire doors, missing fire-resisting glazing and inadequate detection and alarms. It reports a 12-month sentence suspended for 12 months, a £200,000 company fine and costs of £37,293. I could not find the fire service’s own release, so I am relying on LandlordZONE for the details.

Note the timeline: the inspections were in 2021 and sentencing came in 2026. Fire safety enforcement is slow, but it does arrive. Most of the defects listed are ordinary maintenance failures, not exotic ones. Doors that do not close, blocked escape routes and alarms that do not work are what I see when I walk through tired rented buildings.

If you let a house in multiple occupation or a converted building, have a written fire risk assessment, test the alarms, and check the doors actually shut. It costs far less than a prosecution.

Bigger portfolios, and supply figures you should not trust blindly

LandlordZONE reports Fleet Mortgages’ Buy-to-Let Rental Barometer, which says the average portfolio among its applicants rose to 18 properties in the third quarter, from 12 a year earlier, and that 66% of applications came from landlords with four or more properties. That is Fleet’s own applicant base, not the whole market. A second LandlordZONE piece, on the rental supply figures, sets official stock growth against agent reports of falling availability, and concludes this is “a reshuffle rather than a total rout”.

That squares with what I would expect. Small landlords with one or two older houses are the ones facing the hardest decisions on energy upgrades, the database and the new tenancy rules, and bigger operators can spread the cost. My bet is that the stock they sell is where most retrofit work ends up.

What I’d actually do

  1. Landlords: gather your gas record, EICR, EPC and deposit paperwork in one folder now, before the database opens in your region.
  2. Landlords with old EPCs: check the date and band on your certificate and get it refreshed if it is stale or the property has been improved.
  3. Developers and self-builders: design to the rules in force, not to a pledge from opposition.
  4. HMO and converted-building owners: check your fire risk assessment is written, current and acted on.
  5. Buyers of ex-rental homes: get a proper building survey. Tired stock is where the hidden costs are.
