Retrofit for
architects: PAS 2035
and moisture

Most retrofit failures aren’t caused by too little insulation. They’re caused by moisture and air going where nobody planned for them to go. Here’s how PAS 2035 works from the designer’s side: when an architect has to be the Retrofit Designer, what the assessment should hand you, and the moisture and ventilation decisions that make or break the job.

Published 4 November 2026 Reading time · 9 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Clause numbers are from PAS 2035:2023, which came into effect on 30 March 2025. A review is under way, so check for a newer edition before relying on a specific clause.

When an architect must be the Retrofit Designer

PAS 2035 has six roles: Retrofit Advisor, Assessor, Designer, Installer (working to PAS 2030), Evaluator, and the Retrofit Coordinator who holds it all together from assessment to evaluation. One person can hold more than one role if they’re qualified for each and any conflict of interest is declared.

Under Annex A, clause A.3.7, the Retrofit Designer must come from a short list of professionals where a project has three or more interacting fabric measures, or any fabric insulation or replacement windows on a high-rise or system-built dwelling:

  • an architect registered with the ARB
  • a chartered architectural technologist
  • a professional member of the CIOB
  • a chartered building surveyor

“Interacting” means the measures meet at junctions: wall and roof, wall and floor, wall and windows. For traditionally built homes, where the measures could affect the fabric or its significance, the designer also needs the Level 3 Award in Energy Efficiency for Older and Traditional Buildings or recognised conservation accreditation. The coordinator must check the designer’s qualifications in every case.

The design only starts after the assessment, the improvement options evaluation and the medium-term improvement plan, which the 2023 edition makes a requirement for every dwelling. The plan records the order measures go in over time, so a good design today mustn’t block the next stage.

What the assessment should give you

Clause 7.3.2 asks for far more than an energy rating: heritage, construction and condition; the location and severity of defects, condensation and mould; the existing ventilation, with any fan testing; the main elements with their U-values and moisture properties; a measured survey of the heat-loss envelope; exposure to wind and rain; services; occupants; and risks such as flooding and overheating.

Two details matter most to a designer. The survey should show where windows and doors sit in the wall depth, because that governs the thermal bridge around them once the wall is insulated. And defects come in two lists: those that must be repaired before retrofit, and those that are only recommended. The first list is your starting point.

Most designers don’t realise this: before designing, the Retrofit Designer must confirm the assessment is compliant and holds enough to design from. If it doesn’t, you report what’s missing to the coordinator, who gets the assessor to collect it. So if the wall build-up is a guess, don’t design around the gap. Ask for it.

Be careful with the numbers, too. PAS 2035 itself notes that energy models such as SAP and PHPP mostly don’t deal with moisture, and that RdSAP’s default U-values may not suit traditional buildings. A rating tells you about energy. It tells you nothing about whether a wall will stay dry.

What to ask your assessor for

  • Wall build-ups measured or opened up where needed, not assumed
  • Window and door positions in the wall depth
  • Photographs of every elevation, the roof and the junctions
  • Defects split into must-repair and recommended
  • Measured extract fan rates and background ventilator sizes
  • Moisture readings, with where any mould is

On one of our assessments, a block of flats with a mansard roof and parapet gutters, a drone survey showed plant growth in the gutter, staining below it, and the condition of the slates, the lead and the vent terminals through the roof. That’s the difference between a guess and a detail: gutters that overflow need fixing before any wall is insulated, and every roof penetration is a junction the insulation and air barrier have to meet.

Moisture: where retrofit goes wrong

PAS 2035 describes fabric first in five stages: repair and dry out the fabric; quick wins such as controls and lighting; insulation and airtightness with adequate ventilation; efficient heating; then renewables. Insulation is never the first step.

BS 5250:2021 widened the old condensation standard into the management of moisture in all its forms, and clause 8.2.10 of PAS 2035 says the design shall provide for managing moisture in the construction in line with it. Before detailing, think about the sources: driving rain and defects from outside, ground moisture from below, cooking, washing and breathing from inside, and moisture trapped within the construction itself.

PAS 2035 (clause 4.3.3, note 1) says a Glaser-type check might not be enough as the basis for a moisture risk assessment for certain walls, giving solid walls with internal wall insulation in exposed locations as the example. That’s the classic failure: a wall that used to be warmed and dried from inside becomes colder and wetter once it’s insulated internally, especially where driving rain hits it.

So match the existing wall’s moisture strategy. Where a wall relies on a dynamic moisture balance, vapour-permeable and capillary-active materials suit it best, and the air barrier needn’t be a plastic membrane: a wet finish such as lime plaster can be both airtight and vapour-permeable.

Five questions for any wall before you insulate it

  1. Is it in good repair, and is it dry?
  2. How exposed is it to driving rain?
  3. What is it made of, and how does it manage moisture now?
  4. Once insulated, which way can it dry, and fast enough?
  5. What does it meet at every edge: floor, ceiling, windows, party wall, roof?

Junctions and thermal bridges

Where the design includes fabric measures, it must detail every corner, junction and edge of the affected elements, including junctions with elements that aren’t being improved. The insulated envelope stays continuous in three dimensions, the air barrier stays intact, and thermal bridging is minimised. A non-standard detail has to show, by calculation to BRE IP1/06, a temperature factor (fRsi) of at least 0.75.

In a detached cottage we assessed, the mould sat on the window reveals and around the frames, the coldest surfaces in the rooms, with surface moisture readings well into the wet range. Insulate the wall but not the reveal, and that strip becomes colder relative to everything around it, so mould concentrates there even more.

Staged work brings its own risks. Annex D’s measures interaction matrix sorts pairs of measures into green (no interaction), yellow (physical junctions that need details), orange (other interactions, such as insulation and heating) and red (incompatible). For traditional buildings, add an assessment of significance to BS 7913, and favour reversible measures.

Build tight, ventilate right

Clause 8.2.11 treats airtightness as part of any insulation, window or door measure, because those measures seal up the leakage that UK homes have relied on for ventilation. If the package is Category B or C under Approved Document F, or the assessment found condensation or mould, the designer must confirm whether the existing ventilation is adequate and, if not, specify an upgrade to Annex C.

  • Higher-impact packages: continuous mechanical extract from every wet room with background ventilators elsewhere, or whole-dwelling heat recovery.
  • Lighter packages: correctly sized intermittent extract fans in all wet rooms, plus background ventilators in every room.
  • With continuous extract, background ventilators of at least 4,000 mm², and none in wet rooms or with heat recovery.
  • If testing shows air permeability below 5 m³/(h·m²) at 50 Pa, intermittent fans are no longer enough.
  • Purge ventilation in every habitable room, door undercuts or transfer grilles, automatic controls, rigid ducts, and commissioning.

Measure it, don’t assume it

In the same cottage, the three bathroom extract fans measured with an anemometer and flow hood gave:

FanMeasuredIntermittent minimumResult
Bathroom 12.5 l/s15 l/sFail
Bathroom 26.6 l/s15 l/sFail
Bathroom 39.2 l/s15 l/sFail

Every fan was running, and every fan failed. Door undercuts were 6–8 mm against roughly 10 mm needed, and few windows had trickle vents. The owners were planning new windows, which fitted first would have removed most of the accidental ventilation the house relied on. So the ventilation had to be fixed first, or at the same time at the latest.

The rest of the air-and-heat picture: size any new heating on the improved heat loss (clause 8.2.19), plan and test the air barrier, and design out overheating with shading, secure night ventilation and lower internal gains before reaching for cooling. TrustMark points designers to CIBSE TM59 and Approved Document O.

A designer’s checklist

  • Check the assessment is good enough before you design, and ask for what’s missing.
  • Repairs and drying out come first.
  • Detail every corner, junction and edge.
  • Match the existing wall’s moisture strategy, and go beyond Glaser where solid walls, internal insulation and exposure meet.
  • Ventilate to the new airtightness, and measure what’s there rather than assuming it works.
  • Write it into the medium-term improvement plan, so the next stage doesn’t undo this one.
