External wall
insulation
cost in
2026

External wall insulation typically costs £10,000–£18,000 for an ordinary 3-bed semi in 2026 — around £100–£180 per square metre — and it's the right fix for solid-wall homes with no cavity to fill. The bigger question this year, after a well-documented run of botched installs under government schemes, is who's fitting it and to what standard.

Published 5 Aug 2026 Reading time · 9 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

External wall insulation (EWI) is the standard answer for a solid-wall property — broadly, anything built before the 1930s and much housing into that decade, where there's no cavity for the cheaper cavity-fill route. Fitted properly in 2026, the external wall insulation cost for an ordinary 3-bed semi runs about £10,000–£18,000, working out at roughly £100–£180 per square metre depending on the insulation board and render system specified. A small terrace or flat comes in from around £8,000, and a large detached or period property with a lot of wall area and detailing can run £15,000–£25,000 or more.

But the number on the quote is only half the story in 2026. Solid wall insulation fitted under the government's ECO4 and Great British Insulation Scheme grants since 2022 has been the subject of a genuinely serious quality problem: tens of thousands of homes audited, a majority found not to meet the required standard, and installers suspended from doing further work. None of that means EWI is a bad idea on the right house — it means who fits it, and how well, matters at least as much as what it costs.

I diagnose buildings for a living and don't install render systems myself, so I've no reason to talk EWI up or down. What follows is 2026 pricing cross-checked against current UK trade cost guides, what actually drives the number, the grants and 0% VAT still running, and — because it's directly relevant right now — what's gone wrong with solid wall insulation under the government schemes and what to watch for so it doesn't happen on your house.

As with most trades in this series, London and the South East — Dom’s patch included — trend above the national figures quoted here.

What drives the price

Wall area, storeys and detailing. You're covering the whole external face rather than filling a gap, so wall area, height and the number of window and door openings — each needing insulated reveals and new cills — move the number directly. A plain two-storey box costs less per m² than a house with bays, dormers or a lot of articulation.

Insulation board. EPS (expanded polystyrene) is the cheapest and most common board. Mineral wool costs more but is fire-resistant and vapour-open, and phenolic boards give the best insulating value per millimetre for a similar price to mineral wool. On an older solid-wall property, a vapour-open board matters more than the small cost difference suggests — more on that below.

Render finish. Monocouche render is the cheapest standard finish. Silicone and acrylic through-colour renders cost more but need repainting less often and resist algae staining better, which matters on north-facing or sheltered elevations.

Scaffolding and access. EWI needs scaffolding round the whole building, not just one elevation, so terraces and semis with shared boundaries, tight side access or a neighbour's property in the way often add cost that doesn't show up until the site visit.

Region. Installers in the South East, Surrey and London carry higher overheads, and that shows up in the quote the same way it does for any trade.

Do you actually need it — and do you need permission?

The first useful question isn't the price, it's whether your walls are the kind EWI is actually for. If your house was built after about 1930 it very likely has a cavity, and cavity wall insulation does the equivalent job for a fraction of the cost — EWI is the route for solid-wall construction with nothing to fill: Victorian and Edwardian terraces, most 1900s–1930s stock, and any property where a look in the loft or a core sample confirms a single-skin wall, roughly 220mm of brick plus render, rather than two skins with a gap. It's a five-minute check for a surveyor, and a genuinely wasted £10,000+ quote if you get it wrong.

Most houses can add EWI under permitted development, since it isn't usually treated as a material change to the building's use, but there are real exceptions. Conservation areas, listed buildings, and properties in an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty or National Park generally do need planning permission, because EWI necessarily changes the external finish and thickens the wall by 100mm or more at every opening. On a terrace or semi it's also worth checking the boundary line with a neighbour before insulation is built up against it — a few centimetres either side of a shared wall can matter more than it sounds, and can need their agreement.

Grants and VAT in 2026

Two routes still apply as of August 2026, and it's worth being precise about which is actually running. ECO4 — the Energy Company Obligation — was extended by nine months and now runs to 31 December 2026. If you receive a qualifying means-tested benefit (Universal Credit, Pension Credit, income-based JSA or ESA among others) and your home has an EPC rating of D or below, external wall insulation can be substantially or fully funded, delivered through an energy supplier or an accredited installer as part of a whole-house PAS 2035 assessment rather than a single-measure quote. The Great British Insulation Scheme (GBIS), which offered a route without a full benefits test, closed to new applications on 31 March 2026 and has not been extended — don't chase it.

Outside a grant, the work itself still carries 0% VAT when the insulation material and its installation are supplied together by the same VAT-registered contractor, a relief due to step up to 5% from April 2027. That's a real saving built into every quote you get this year rather than next — worth confirming it's actually reflected in the price, not just assumed.

The render-failure risk: what's gone wrong with solid wall insulation

This is the section worth reading properly before anyone's on your doorstep with a clipboard. Since 2025, government and industry audits have found what regulators themselves have described as a systemic failure in solid wall insulation fitted under ECO4 and GBIS since 2022: around 60,000 households had the work done, a majority of installations checked did not meet the required standard, and the National Audit Office found the great bulk of one large audited sample at risk of damp and mould if left unaddressed. Ofgem has since written to every one of those 60,000 households, 39 installers have been suspended from fitting new solid wall insulation, and the industry standard (PAS 2035/2030) was tightened from 30 March 2025 as a direct result.

None of that is a reason to rule EWI out — on a suitable solid-wall house, done to the standard, it's still one of the more effective single measures available. It is a strong argument for treating the render system, not just the insulation board underneath, as the part worth scrutinising. The failures reported cluster around a specific set of causes: boards fixed with joints that aren't staggered in a brick-bond pattern, which lets the whole system move at every joint line; base coat and reinforcing mesh applied too thin or not properly embedded, which cracks and lets water in; and — the one that matters most on an old solid-brick house — a render system that isn't vapour-open enough for a wall built to breathe. A standard cement-based render on a solid Victorian wall can trap moisture that used to evaporate outward, pushing it instead toward the inside face — a slower, less visible version of exactly the damp problem EWI is meant to prevent.

If your own house already has solid wall insulation fitted under a grant scheme since 2022 and you're seeing damp patches, staining or a musty smell that wasn't there before, Ofgem's remediation route is free to you and installer guarantees typically run up to £20,000 — worth chasing before paying anyone privately to investigate. A damp meter reading is a reasonable first check of your own before you call anyone in.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • A vapour-open, breathable system — wood fibre, mineral wool or a lime-based render — specified for a solid-wall period property, not a generic polystyrene-and-cement system priced for a 1960s cavity house.
  • Board joints staggered in a brick-bond pattern on the written spec, not just promised verbally on site.
  • PAS 2035 retrofit coordinator sign-off if the work is grant-funded — a condition of ECO4 funding, not an optional extra.
  • A written spec naming the exact render system, brand, thickness and coat build-up, not just “insulation and render.”
  • Ventilation addressed alongside the insulation — trickle vents, extractor fans — since tightening up a wall without addressing ventilation is how condensation problems start indoors.
  • A manufacturer-backed guarantee (typically 10–25 years depending on system), not just an installer's own promise that doesn't survive them going out of business.

Questions I get asked

How much does external wall insulation cost? In 2026, budget roughly £8,000–£14,000 for a flat or small terrace, £10,000–£18,000 for an ordinary 3-bed semi, and £15,000–£25,000 or more for a large detached or period property. Most quotes work out at around £100–£180 per square metre, depending on the insulation board and render system specified.

Is my house suitable for external wall insulation? EWI is built for solid-wall properties — most housing from Victorian and Edwardian terraces through to around the 1930s — that have no cavity to fill. If your house was built later than about 1930 it very likely has a cavity, in which case cavity wall insulation does the same job for a fraction of the cost. A quick check of wall thickness, or a look in the loft at how the brickwork is built up, tells a surveyor which you've got in a few minutes.

Can I get external wall insulation for free in 2026? Under ECO4, extended to run to 31 December 2026, it can be substantially or fully funded if you receive a qualifying benefit and your home has an EPC rating of D or below, delivered through an energy supplier or accredited installer as part of a whole-house PAS 2035 assessment. The Great British Insulation Scheme, which offered a route without a full benefits test, closed to new applications on 31 March 2026 and hasn't been extended.

Is external wall insulation safe after the recent problems with solid wall insulation? Government and industry audits since 2025 found that a majority of solid wall insulation fitted under ECO4 and GBIS since 2022 — around 60,000 households — didn't meet the required standard, with poor detailing and unsuitable render systems raising the risk of trapped moisture, damp and mould. That's a strong argument for scrutinising the render system and installer accreditation closely, not a reason to rule EWI out on a suitable house done properly. If insulation already fitted under a grant scheme is causing problems, Ofgem's remediation route is free and worth chasing before paying anyone privately to investigate.
