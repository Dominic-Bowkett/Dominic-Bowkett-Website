A survey produces a steady trickle of physical things that need identifying later: a bagged scrape of loft insulation going off for a fibre check, a core sample cut from a solid wall, a set of moisture readings taken at four points round the same bay window, three near-identical loft hatch photographs that need to stay in the right order. None of it means anything back at the desk unless it was labelled clearly before it left the property, and a permanent marker on a sample bag rubs off in a pocket long before anyone opens it.

Retrofit work adds a second, more formal use. PAS 2035 and most competent-person schemes expect a durable record somewhere accessible — inside the meter cupboard, on the loft hatch, at the consumer unit — noting what was installed and when, so whoever opens that hatch in ten years isn’t guessing whether the joists above have already been dealt with. A luggage tag in biro does not survive that timescale; a proper label does.

The category splits the same way most kit on this site does: cheap direct-thermal printers whose labels fade within a year, sat against laminated-tape machines built to survive a loft, a van and daylight for a decade. Seven compared below, from a keychain-sized phone printer to an industrial patch-panel machine, on what actually matters for labelling samples, evidence and site fixtures rather than office filing. How I judge a label printer

Laminated tape over direct thermal, for anything that leaves the site. A direct-thermal label — the kind printed without a ribbon or laminate — fades within months of daylight and rubs off if it gets wet or brushes against something. That is fine for a temporary box label, useless on a sample bag that might sit in a lab’s in-tray for a fortnight or a meter cupboard label meant to last the life of the installation.

Hot keys for the labels actually repeated on a survey. Cable wrap, cable flag, sample reference, room ID — the same handful of formats get printed over and over, and a printer with a dedicated key for each saves retyping the layout from scratch every time, which matters more on site than a long list of clip-art symbols.

A label width range that stretches from a sample tag to a cable ID. A 6mm label wraps a probe or a small sample bag; a 24mm label is what most consumer-unit and cable-ID jobs actually need. A printer limited to one narrow width ends up doing only half the job it was bought for.

Usable one-handed, in a loft, with the other hand on a torch. A backlit screen, a keyboard laid out for gloved fingers and a body that survives being dropped matter more on a genuine site printer than anything on the spec sheet about app polish.

Battery type that matches how the printer is actually used. A printer that lives in the van and gets picked up once a week is better served by replaceable AA or AAA batteries than a rechargeable pack that is flat by the time it is needed; one used daily is the reverse.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Laminated tape matters more than tape colour or symbol count. A label that has to survive being on a sample bag, a loft hatch or a meter cupboard for years needs Brother’s TZe-style laminated tape or DYMO’s industrial vinyl, not a direct-thermal print that fades within months regardless of how many clip-art symbols the printer’s library claims to hold.

Hot keys save more time on site than a bigger font library. The handful of label formats used on a repeat basis — cable wrap, sample reference, room ID — are worth a dedicated key each; a longer list of fonts and symbols nobody uses on a survey is not.

WiFi and app connectivity are conveniences, not requirements. A solo surveyor gets no practical benefit from a printer that can be shared across a network; a physical keyboard and a Bluetooth pairing to one phone cover everything a one-person site visit actually needs.

Barcode printing is only worth paying for if something is actually scanning it. The Rhino 5200’s barcode capability is genuinely useful for a practice tracking equipment by scan, and dead weight for one that isn’t — it is worth checking which category a given firm falls into before paying the premium for it.

Questions I get asked

What’s the best way to label a sample being sent off for testing?

A laminated label, not a marker pen direct on the bag. Ink rubs off inside a sealed sample bag long before it reaches a lab, and a loose paper tag can separate from the sample it was meant to identify. A small laminated label printed with a reference number that matches the site notes, stuck to the outside of the bag, survives handling and transit far better than either.

Do meter cupboard and loft hatch labels actually need to be this durable?

For retrofit work, generally yes. PAS 2035 and most competent-person schemes expect installation records to remain legible for the life of the measure, which for loft or cavity insulation can mean decades. A direct-thermal label printed on a budget printer can fade to blank within a year or two in a sunlit meter cupboard; laminated tape is built to survive that timescale.

Can labels be printed from a phone on site, without carrying a printer with a keyboard?

Yes, on any of the Bluetooth-enabled printers here, including the Niimbot and both Brother E-series picks — the label is typed and previewed in an app and sent to the printer over Bluetooth. The trade-off is that the printer becomes useless without the phone, which is why the picks with a physical keyboard as well as Bluetooth are the safer choice for anyone who doesn’t want a single point of failure.

Is DYMO or Brother tape better for site use?

Both make a laminated option that performs well — Brother’s TZe range and DYMO’s industrial vinyl for the Rhino series — and the practical difference is availability and format rather than durability: TZe cassettes fit a wider range of Brother printers and are stocked more widely, while DYMO’s Rhino tape is built specifically around the cable and patch-panel hot-key templates the 5200 uses.
