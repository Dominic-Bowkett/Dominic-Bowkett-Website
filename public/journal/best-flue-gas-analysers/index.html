The best
flue gas analysers
for gas engineers

A stone-cold boiler, a gas safety certificate that’s lapsed, a faint smell in a room with no obvious source — these are the flags a survey raises, not a diagnosis. Closing that loop is a Gas Safe registered engineer’s job, and the instrument that produces the actual reading is a flue gas analyser: probe into the flue, and the display gives O2, CO, a calculated CO2 and the combustion ratio that separates a clean burn from an appliance that needs attention now. Here is what earns a place in the van for that job, from a compact single-purpose unit to the multi-fuel kit that also covers oil work, and what the spec sheet is worth paying extra for.

I’m a building surveyor, not a gas engineer, and I want to be upfront about that before anything else in this guide: I don’t carry a flue gas analyser or run a combustion test myself. What I do see, on a routine survey or a HomeBuyer Survey, is the appliance itself and the paperwork sitting behind it — a boiler that’s stone cold when it should be ticking over, a gas safety certificate with a date that’s quietly slipped past twelve months, a flue terminal positioned somewhere it plainly shouldn’t be. Those are things I flag and write up. Turning a flag into a pass or fail is a Gas Safe registered engineer’s work, and the tool that does it is the one this guide is about.

A flue gas analyser clips a probe into the flue and reads what’s actually coming out of the appliance: oxygen, carbon monoxide directly, carbon dioxide calculated from the two, flue temperature, and the combustion ratio that tells an engineer whether the appliance is burning cleanly or starved of air. That reading is what turns “seems fine” into a number on a certificate — the same certificate a landlord needs for a gas safety check, and the same document I go looking for the date on when I’m writing up a survey.

How I judge a flue gas analyser

BS 7967 / EN 50379-3 compliance, not just a price that looks about right. BS 7967 is the British Standard covering the use of a portable combustion analyser to assess CO and combustion performance on domestic gas appliances, and it assumes an analyser tested to EN 50379-3 behind it. A stated compliance line on the listing is worth more than a plausible-looking spec sheet with no standard named against it.

Sensor life, and what replacing it actually costs. The electrochemical O2 and CO sensors in these units have a working life, typically quoted around five years on the mid- and premium-tier units here, before they need replacing rather than just recalibrating — worth checking the replacement cost before buying on the headline unit price alone.

Fuel scope matched to the actual diary. A gas-only analyser is cheaper and lighter, but oil-fired appliances, kerosene and wood-pellet systems need a probe and calibration gas table rated for that fuel — an OFTEC engineer with oil boilers on the round needs a genuinely multi-fuel unit, not a gas-only one with an optional accessory that was never bought.

A printer or app export for the paper trail. A printed or emailed combustion reading alongside the certificate is increasingly what a landlord or letting agent expects to see, not just a verbal “it’s fine.” Some kits build an IR printer in; on others it’s a phone photo of the display, dated and saved against the job.

Calibration cost and lockout behaviour. Most of these analysers are serviced annually against a UKAS-traceable reference gas, and several will refuse a valid reading once that calibration date has lapsed. Factor the yearly service cost into the real cost of ownership, not just the price on the box.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

NO/NOx measurement is rarely needed on routine domestic servicing. It earns its keep on commercial catering appliances and incident investigation, where nitrogen oxide levels are genuinely part of the assessment — not on an ordinary annual combi or regular boiler safety check, which most engineers will run hundreds of times before NO measurement changes the outcome.

A printer is convenience, not compliance. A dated, saved phone photo of the display is an accepted record for most gas safety documentation. A built-in printer saves a step and looks tidier in a folder; it isn’t the only route to a valid one, and it’s not worth the price jump between kits if the paperwork process already works without it.

A high CO measurement ceiling matters for investigation, not routine testing. Readings on a normally functioning appliance sit well below the top of any analyser’s range here; a 10,000ppm-plus ceiling is there for a badly malfunctioning appliance or a post-incident investigation, not for the numbers a routine service actually produces.

Fuel-probe breadth is only worth paying for if the diary actually spans it. The 327-1’s gas, oil, kerosene, LPG and pellet compatibility is genuinely useful for an engineer who also does OFTEC oil work — buying that breadth “just in case” on a gas-only round pays for capability that may never get used.

Electrical CAT ratings don’t apply here. That’s the relevant safety marking on a clamp meter or multimeter working near live wiring; a flue gas analyser’s relevant standards are BS 7967 and EN 50379, and a stated compliance line on the listing is what’s worth checking here instead.

Questions I get asked

What does a flue gas analyser actually measure?

Directly: oxygen (O2) and carbon monoxide (CO), via an electrochemical sensor, plus flue temperature and often draught or differential pressure through a separate probe. From those direct readings the unit calculates CO2, the CO/CO2 combustion ratio, and combustion efficiency — the figures that actually appear on a gas safety certificate.

Does a Gas Safe engineer have to use a BS 7967-compliant analyser?

BS 7967 is the British Standard that frames how a portable combustion analyser should be used to assess CO and combustion performance on domestic gas appliances, and it assumes an analyser tested to EN 50379-3 behind it — effectively the industry baseline a registered engineer’s kit is expected to meet, even where it isn’t spelled out as a standalone legal requirement for every job. Every analyser in this guide states EN 50379 compliance on its own listing.

How often does a flue gas analyser need calibrating?

Commonly annually, checked against a UKAS-traceable reference gas by the manufacturer or an approved service centre — several of the units here will refuse to give a valid reading once that calibration date has lapsed, and a Gas Safe inspection can ask to see the calibration certificate. Build the yearly service cost into the real cost of owning one, not just the purchase price.

Can the same analyser test an oil-fired boiler as well as a gas one?

Only if it’s fitted with the right probe and fuel setting — a gas-only analyser reads nothing useful pointed at an oil-fired flue. The Testo 327-1 above states natural gas, oil, kerosene, LPG and wood-pellet compatibility outright, and the Testo 310 and 300+ both support an oil probe; check the exact kit contents before assuming multi-fuel capability comes as standard on any given listing.
