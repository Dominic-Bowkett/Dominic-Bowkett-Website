A block of masonry paint over a hairline crack, a render finish that’s clearly newer than the wall behind it, a mortar joint packed with hard cement pointing where the wall was built with lime — a fair share of the harder questions on a survey can’t be answered by looking at the surface. They need a small, controlled opening into the wall itself: a section of render chased back to check for wall ties, a mortar bed chipped out to look for a DPC that isn’t there, a path cleared to a lintel or flue the drawings alone won’t confirm. A bolster chisel, worked with a club hammer, is what gets you that opening without turning a five-minute look into an afternoon of making good.

It earns its place for a narrower job than the name suggests. Most bolster work on an inspection isn’t demolition — it’s a controlled cut along a line you’ve already chosen: an existing mortar joint, the edge of a crack, the perimeter of a small test opening. A properly forged, hardened edge splits cleanly along that line; a soft or badly ground import glances off, crumbles the masonry wider than intended, or simply won’t bite without three times the effort.

The list below runs from a genuine trade-forged, guarded bolster through to an unguarded traditional pattern for finer work, priced from a few pounds to proper trade money. It sits naturally alongside a crowbar in the same section of the bag — one gets a floor or skirting up, the other gets you into the wall behind it.

How I judge a bolster chisel

Forged, not cast or stamped. Forging works steel under pressure into a dense, consistent piece; a cheaper stamped or cast blade can carry soft spots invisible until the edge rolls or chips on the first proper strike. A listing naming “drop-forged” or “forged and tempered” is telling you something real; one that just says “steel” usually isn’t.

Blade width matched to the job. A 100mm (4in) blade is the general-purpose size. A narrower 50–75mm blade suits a single mortar joint or a delicate opening where a wide blade would take out more wall than you need to see.

A guard that actually stays put. Most survey-related bolster work is a one-handed swing in an awkward position — low in a crawl space, reaching into a loft, braced against a ladder. A moulded guard bonded to the shaft protects the supporting hand from a mis-hit; a loose sliding disc is worth less than it looks in the photo.

An octagonal or hexagonal shaft, not round. A flat-faced shaft sits still on a sill or board rather than rolling away, and grips better through a gloved hand than a round bar does.

Edge geometry over headline steel grade. A chrome vanadium or carbon-steel label matters less than the bevel itself: a properly ground, moderately acute edge bites and splits along a line, while a blunt or over-steep grind bounces off the masonry rather than cutting it.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

“Forged” and “tempered” are claims worth checking, not assuming. A forged, heat-treated edge holds its bevel and resists chipping under repeated strikes; a soft or poorly hardened one rolls or mushrooms after surprisingly little use. If a listing doesn’t name the process, price the tool as the budget option, whatever the steel grade printed on the packaging.

Chrome vanadium versus plain carbon steel is a smaller factor than the grind. Both make serviceable bolsters. What determines a clean cut is the bevel angle and how well it’s finished — a blunt edge on premium steel still glances off masonry rather than biting.

Blade width chosen for the job, not the biggest one in the shop. A 100mm bolster is the sensible general-purpose size; a narrower 75mm blade gives finer control for a single mortar joint.

Permission and reinstatement, not the tool itself. What actually protects a report and a client relationship is asking before opening up anything structural or decorative, and being clear who makes good the opening afterwards. A line in the report — “small opening made with the owner’s consent to confirm wall construction” — covers far more than any spend on the bolster that made it.

What the marketing pushes that you can skip. Bright handle paint, “unbreakable” warranty claims and chrome plating on a blade that will be chipped within a week are all decoration. None of it changes whether the edge bites cleanly or rolls first time you lean into it.

Questions I get asked

What’s the difference between a bolster chisel and a cold chisel? A bolster has a wide, flat blade suited to splitting or facing brick and cutting a clean line through masonry or render. A cold chisel is typically narrower, intended more for general metalwork and demolition. Manufacturers use the terms loosely, so buy on blade shape and width rather than the name alone.

What size bolster chisel do I need for opening up masonry? A 100mm (4in) blade covers most general opening-up work — facing a brick, clearing render, chipping out a mortar bed. For a single mortar joint or a smaller opening, a narrower 50–75mm blade gives more control and disturbs less of the surrounding wall.

Do I need a guarded bolster chisel? For occasional use in an awkward, one-handed position — low in a void, braced on a ladder — yes. A bricklayer using one daily may prefer the unguarded traditional pattern for the feedback it gives, but that comes with practiced technique most surveyors won’t have built up. A guarded bolster like the Roughneck or OX Tools picks above is the sensible default.

Will a bolster chisel damage brickwork or render? Any opening into masonry leaves a mark, but a controlled cut along an existing mortar joint or crack edge does far less damage than random hammering into a solid face. Where the opening will be visible afterwards, agree with the owner beforehand whether you’re making good yourself or flagging it in the report.

A bolster rarely gets its own line in a report, but the small opening it makes often turns “wall construction could not be confirmed” into an actual answer about ties, cavities or a missing DPC.
