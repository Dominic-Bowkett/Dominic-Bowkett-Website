Black silicone round a bath is one of the commonest things I photograph in a house. It turns up in a HomeBuyer report as a line in the bathroom section, and in a homeowner’s email as “I have cleaned it three times and it is back”. People assume the tube they bought was a dud. Usually it was not. The silicone was put on over a film of soap and old sealant, in a room with no extractor, and it had stopped being clean within a few months.

An anti-mould sealant is worth buying, but only for what it is: a silicone with a fungicide additive that makes the surface harder for mould to colonise. It is not a damp treatment. It will not rescue a bathroom where the walls run with condensation every morning, and the additive does not last for ever. The decision that matters most is what you do before the bead goes on, and what the room does afterwards.

Below is how I would choose a bathroom sealant, six that are worth looking at, and the preparation that makes any of them last. If the black line is already there and you want to clean it first, the mould remover guide comes before this one in the sequence.

How I judge an anti-mould bathroom sealant

Sanitary silicone, not general-purpose. A bathroom sealant should be a silicone that is labelled for sanitary use and says it contains a fungicide. Acrylic decorators’ caulk is for filling gaps before painting. It shrinks, splits when a bath moves under load and holds water against the joint. Use it round a bath or a shower tray and you will be back within a year.

A named additive, where you can find one. Everbuild names Mould Shield, and Dowsil describes its 785 as bacteria-resistant and mould-resistant. A vague “mould-resistant” on the front of the tube is weaker evidence. The additive slowly leaches out in a wet room, so it is a delay, not a permanent shield.

Cure time against how soon you need the shower. Quoted figures run from half an hour water-ready to a full day to cure. Quick is convenient, but a quick-skinning bead gives you less time to tool a neat joint. If you can leave a bathroom unused overnight, a slower cure and better tooling is the better trade.

Colour and finish. White ages yellow and grey fastest. Clear shows trapped dirt. Match the colour to the suite, and remember that a new bright white line next to a grubby old grout line will look worse than the old joint did.

Gun or no gun. A cartridge needs a sealant gun, which costs a few pounds. A squeeze tube is for small repairs. For a full bath perimeter, buy the cartridge.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Preparation matters more than the brand. Cut out all of the old silicone with a blade and a remover, scrape the residue off, and clean the joint with a spirit-based cleaner or white spirit so that no soap scum, oil or silicone smear is left. The joint must be completely dry. Mould that is already in the old bead has to come out, because new silicone will not stick to it and will carry the spores with it. A line of masking tape each side gives a straight edge. Run a bead, tool it once with a finger or a profile tool dipped in a drop of washing-up liquid in water, and peel the tape while the silicone is wet.

Fill the bath before you seal it. A bath moves under the weight of water and a bather. If you seal an empty bath the bead is stretched when the bath is next filled, and tears at the tile edge. Trade fitters fill the bath, seal it while it is full, and let the water out after the cure. It is a simple step that explains most of the splits I see.

Mind the gap. Silicone is for small movement joints. A gap much wider than about five millimetres needs a backing strip or a different fix, such as a flexible trim, and a bath that has dropped away from the wall has usually moved for a reason worth checking.

Ventilation decides how long it lasts. A fungicide additive is fighting a steamy room every day. If the bathroom has no working extractor fan, or the fan is too weak or runs only while the light is on, the bead will go black again whatever you bought. A hygrometer tells you what the humidity actually does after a shower, and a damp meter will tell you if the wall behind the tiles is wet, which is a different problem.

When a new bead is the wrong answer. If the black growth sits on the wall above the tile line, on a ceiling or in a corner of an external wall, the sealant is not the cause. Water that gets behind failed silicone can rot a bath panel or stain the ceiling below, and in that case the repair is bigger than a tube of silicone. The damp survey cost guide explains what a proper diagnosis involves, and the damp proof paint guide explains why painting over it is not a fix.

What the packaging pushes that does not matter. “Lifetime guarantee” normally covers cracking and shrinking of the cured product, not mould in a bathroom with poor ventilation. “Kills bacteria” is a surface claim. Neither tells you how long the bead will stay clean in your bathroom. Choose on the sanitary rating, the additive, the colour and whether it comes with a gun.

Questions I get asked

Why does black mould keep growing on my shower silicone? Silicone does not feed mould, but the film of soap, skin and limescale that builds up on it does. In a room that stays humid after a shower, that film is enough. Better ventilation, a squeegee or a wipe after use, and a regular clean with a mould spray are what keep a bead white.

How often should bathroom sealant be replaced? There is no fixed interval. A well-prepared bead of good sanitary silicone in a ventilated room can last many years. When the edge lifts, the bead goes brittle, or black staining runs through the thickness rather than sitting on the surface, it is time to cut it out and redo it.

Can I put new silicone over old? No. New silicone does not bond to cured silicone, and any mould in the old bead stays underneath. Cut it out and clean the joint back to the substrate.

Is anti-mould silicone safe to use around a bath I use every day? Once cured, it is a solid rubber, and the manufacturers sell it for exactly this use. Wear gloves while you work, ventilate the room while it cures and follow the label, because the uncured product gives off a vinegar smell.

If the black line keeps coming back however carefully you reseal, the problem is probably the room, not the tube. A ventilation assessment or a building survey will tell you whether it is the air, the fabric or a leak, and getting in touch is the quickest way to arrange one.
