Septic tanks come up in a surprising share of rural survey jobs. A buyer near Hartfield or Forest Row sees a lid in the lawn, asks what it is, and the vendor says it “just works”. Often it does. Sometimes it is a tank from the 1960s discharging into the ditch at the bottom of the garden, which since 2020 is not allowed. The search term people use is “septic tank treatment”, so that is what this guide is about, but the first honest thing to say is what a treatment can and cannot do.

A working septic tank grows its own bacteria from the household waste going into it, and nothing in the government’s rules requires you to add anything. Bacterial sachets and tablets are a maintenance supplement, sold mainly to help with fats and greases and with odours. They will not repair a cracked tank, unclog a saturated drainage field or replace emptying. If the system is failing, the answer is an inspection and probably a drainage contractor, not another packet.

What follows is a features overview of six products: four additives, plus the toilet paper and the cleaner, which do as much good. If you are buying or selling a property with a septic tank, the septic tank survey cost guide covers what an inspection involves.

How I judge a septic tank treatment

What is it claiming to do? Digesting fat, oil and grease and reducing smells are plausible claims for a bacterial product. Unblocking a failed soakaway or “doubling the time between empties” are not claims I would rely on. Be cautious of anything promising to remove the need to empty the tank.

Is it a regular dose? A monthly sachet or tablet is designed to be part of a routine. One large dose of a powerful chemical is the opposite of that, and some strong chemicals harm the bacteria the tank already has.

Does it say what it is, and for what? Look for plain wording and a stated purpose. A listing full of adjectives and no description of what is in the pack is a poor sign.

Is the dose count clear? Pack sizes vary and so does the price per month. Compare the cost of twelve doses, not the headline price of the pack.

Does it suit your system? A septic tank with a drainage field is what these are sold for. A sealed cesspit has no outflow and no biological treatment, so none of this applies. A small sewage treatment plant has its own servicing regime, and the maker’s instructions come first.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Emptying matters more than any additive. The government’s general binding rules say sludge must be removed before it reaches the tank’s maximum capacity, and that you should do this at least once a year or in line with the manufacturer’s instructions, using a registered waste carrier. No packet changes that. If the tank has not been emptied in years, empty it before you spend a penny on tablets. For the pipework side, drainage survey cost sets out what a CCTV survey looks like.

The 2020 rule. A septic tank must not discharge effluent to a watercourse such as a ditch or stream. If yours did, the options were to connect to the public sewer, replace the tank with a small sewage treatment plant, or install a drainage field so the discharge goes to ground. This is the most expensive thing a buyer can find out about on a rural purchase, and no additive touches it. When you sell, you are also expected to tell the buyer in writing about the system, where it is and what has been done to it.

Look at what goes down the drain. Fat poured away after cooking, wet wipes (including the ones labelled flushable), nappies, paint and strong disinfectants all shorten the life of the system. Keep them out. If a slow drain is the problem rather than a smell, a set of drain rods or the options in drain unblockers and snakes deal with the pipe, with the caveat that harsh chemicals do harm in a septic system.

Know the signs of trouble. Persistent smells outside, gurgling, slow flushing, soggy ground or unusually lush grass over the drainage field, and sewage backing up in the lowest drain are all signs that need a professional. Wear work gloves and a respirator mask if you have to handle anything dirty, and never enter a tank or chamber: the gases are dangerous.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. Miracle powders that promise to end emptying for good, and yeast or rotten-meat folklore. Anything that says it makes desludging unnecessary should be treated as a red flag, given what the rules require.

Questions I get asked

Do I need septic tank treatment? No rule requires you to add anything to a septic tank. A properly used and regularly emptied system grows its own bacteria. A monthly additive is optional upkeep, mainly of interest if a lot of fat goes in or you have a recurring odour.

How often should a septic tank be emptied? At least once a year or in line with the manufacturer’s instructions, according to the government’s guidance. Keep the emptying receipts, because a buyer’s solicitor will ask for them.

Can I still discharge a septic tank into a ditch? Not since the 2020 deadline. A septic tank discharging to a watercourse had to be replaced with a treatment plant, connected to the mains sewer or fitted with a drainage field. If you are buying such a property, who pays for the upgrade should be agreed as part of the sale.

Will bleach kill my septic tank? Heavy or repeated use of bleach and strong disinfectants can upset the bacteria in the tank. Occasional household use in small amounts is a different matter, but I would not rely on additives to repair the damage afterwards. Use less, and choose septic-safe products where you can.
