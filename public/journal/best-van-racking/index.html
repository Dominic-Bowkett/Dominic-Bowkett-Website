A bare van floor is an invitation for everything to end up in a heap by the second roundabout — the borescope wedged under a stepladder, a torn box of consumables, whatever rolled forward under braking now blocking the side door. Racking solves that, but only the version that’s actually bolted to something structural and sized for what a surveyor carries, not a stock van full of copper pipe. Most survey work doesn’t need much kit, but it needs the same handful of things every single day — a damp meter, a laser measure, a torch, a tablet, sample bags, and whatever’s been picked up for that afternoon’s job. Left loose, all of it migrates to the bulkhead within a week and stays there, which is exactly the kind of thing that turns a five-minute pull-up outside a job into a ten-minute rummage. Racking fixes that by giving everything a shelf it lives on, not a corner it happens to end up in.

It also matters for reasons that have nothing to do with tidiness. An unsecured tool box or a loose stack of crates becomes a genuine hazard under hard braking, which is why the racking worth buying is bolted through into the van’s floor or wall structure, not wedged in and left to find its own level. Before any brand comes into it, the two real decisions are the material — steel, aluminium or plywood — and whether the fit is universal or cut for one van.

How I judge van racking

Fixing method and crash safety before anything else. A racking system is only as good as what it’s bolted into — look for fixings that go through into structural floor or wall points, not self-tappers into thin door skin, and prefer a manufacturer that states its unit has been crash-tested rather than just described as heavy-duty.

Material is a genuine trade-off, not a quality ranking. Steel is the toughest and usually the cheapest per shelf, aluminium keeps weight down without giving up much rigidity, and plywood is the cheapest to buy outright and the easiest to modify on site — but it needs to stay dry, and it eventually delaminates if the van leaks.

Universal fit against vehicle-specific fit. A universal bracket kit adapts to several van models and moves with the business if the van changes; a kit cut for one exact model — a Transit Custom SWB offside, say — uses the space far more tightly but is tied to that van for as long as it’s owned.

Weight against the van’s payload plate. A loaded steel racking run can weigh 40–80kg before a single tool goes on it, which is worth weighing against what’s already eaten into payload from ply lining, a bulkhead and a roof rack on a 3.5t panel van.

Self-build against delivered assembled. Flat-pack kits are cheaper and easier to trim to fit an odd corner; assembled units cost more but save a weekend and arrive square.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A crash-test claim is worth more than a thick gauge of steel on its own. Genuine load-restraint testing, or a named standard like ECE/R17, is a real signal buried among a lot of spec-sheet padding — the honest question is always whether the fixings go into something structural, not how heavy the unit feels in the showroom.

A high compartment count is a marketing number, not a capacity figure. Sixteen small compartments sound generous until the thing that needs storing is a laser level case that needs one deep space, not four narrow ones — count what actually needs to fit before counting the compartments printed on the box.

Powder-coat colour and branding don’t affect how well racking works. Buy on fixing method and material, not because a unit happens to colour-match the van’s livery.

“Designed for your exact van” is a real fit benefit, not just an upsell — but only if the van’s being kept. A vehicle-specific kit uses the space more tightly than a universal one; it just doesn’t transfer to a replacement van of a different make or size, which a universal system does.

Full-height racking down both sides looks impressive but eats headroom and payload before anything’s loaded. For a surveyor’s modest kit list rather than a plumber’s stock van, a single half-height run is usually enough, with the rest of the floor left free for ladders and boxes.

Questions I get asked

Does fitting racking affect a van’s payload or its warranty? It can affect payload — a loaded steel system can weigh 40–80kg before anything’s stored on it, worth weighing against the van’s payload plate, especially if it’s already carrying ply lining and a roof rack. Warranty terms vary by manufacturer and dealer, and a leased van usually needs the leasing company’s sign-off before anything’s permanently bolted in — check both before drilling.

Should I buy a universal system or one cut for my exact van? It comes down to how long the van’s being kept. A vehicle-specific kit fits tighter and uses the space better, but it’s tied to that model; a universal bracket kit uses the space slightly less efficiently but moves to the next van, which matters more for a business that changes vehicles every few years than one that keeps the same van for a decade.

Is plywood or steel racking better for a working van? Steel and aluminium hold their shape for years and shrug off being knocked about; plywood is cheaper to buy and far easier to modify on site with a jigsaw, but it needs to stay dry and will eventually delaminate if the van develops a leak.

Can racking be fitted without permanently drilling into the van? Most bolt-through systems do need holes in the floor or wall, though a few smaller units use existing factory fixing points. For a leased or soon-to-be-sold van, check the lease terms first, or budget time to fill and seal the holes before handover.
