woodworm treatments,

“Woodworm” is one of the most reliable ways to raise a client’s pulse during a survey, and one of the findings I most often have to talk people down from. Flip back a loft hatch on a house built before about 1980 and there is a fair chance you will find small round holes in the rafters somewhere. That is not, on its own, a problem. It is frequently just a record of an insect that was active decades ago, in timber that has been bone dry ever since, and has nothing whatsoever to do with the structure today.

The insect responsible in the vast majority of UK cases is the common furniture beetle, Anobium punctatum. Its larvae live inside the timber for two to five years, tunnelling as they feed, before an adult beetle emerges through a neat, round exit hole — usually between April and September — leaving behind a scatter of fine bore dust known as frass. Whether that cycle is still running in your rafters, or finished forty years ago, is the entire question. It is also, conveniently, one you can usually answer for free with a torch and a bit of patience, which is most of what this guide is really about before it gets to the tins.

For a small number of active holes in timber you can reach, a tin of the right treatment brushed or sprayed on is genuinely often all that is needed — not a call-out, not a contract, not a twenty-year guarantee. Below is how I judge the treatments, seven worth buying, and where I think the line sits between a weekend job and one to hand to a professional.

How I judge a woodworm treatment

A registered active ingredient, not just marketing. A genuine wood preservative biocide carries an HSE approval number on the tin (older permethrin-based products typically show one, along with a PCS number). That is what separates a regulated insecticide from a vaguely-worded “wood tonic” that does very little. If the label doesn’t say what it kills and how, be suspicious.

Format matched to the job. A brush-on or spray liquid is fine for accessible surface timber — rafters, joists, floorboards you can get a brush to. It does not meaningfully penetrate large-section timber or protect the buried end of a joist built into a damp wall. That job belongs to an injectable gel, pumped into drilled holes so the active ingredient sits inside the timber rather than on it.

Whether it also treats decay. Damp and woodworm travel together more often than not — the beetle’s larvae do best in timber that has been damp or partially decayed by fungus. A dual-purpose product that also protects against wet and dry rot is doing a more honest job than one that only claims the insect.

Low odour and safe indoors. Most of this gets used in occupied houses — a loft over a bedroom, exposed joists in a converted room. Solvent-heavy older formulations need ventilation and time before anyone sleeps nearby; newer low-odour and water-based products are far more forgiving.

Coverage that matches the actual job. A 500ml spray for three exit holes in a windowsill; a 5L tin for a whole roof void. Buying five litres to treat one joist end is money and shelf-life wasted — these products don’t keep indefinitely once opened.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Active vs historic is the whole diagnosis. Fresh exit holes are pale and sharp-edged, often with loose, cream-coloured frass sitting in or below them — run a finger under a suspect area and if fine dust comes away readily, that’s recent. Old holes are dark, sometimes filled with old paint or varnish from a previous decoration, with no frass and no fresh tunnelling visible if you gently probe with a bradawl. Flight season for common furniture beetle runs roughly April to September, so genuinely fresh holes and dust found outside that window are worth a second look before you assume the worst. Most of the loft rafters I’m asked about are the second kind: a record of something that happened, not something happening.

Moisture is the actual driver, not the beetle. Common furniture beetle overwhelmingly favours timber that has been damp, or has had some degree of fungal decay soften the wood — it is far less interested in dry, well-ventilated structural timber even where old flight holes show it was once present. This is why a woodworm finding on a survey is so often really a damp or ventilation finding wearing a different name: a blocked air brick starving a suspended floor void, a leaking valley gutter dampening a rafter end, condensation in an unventilated loft. Treat the beetle and ignore the moisture and, if conditions stay damp, you haven’t actually fixed anything. Check the timber with a damp meter before treating, and again a season later to confirm it’s drying out.

What a tin actually fixes, and when to call someone in. A small number of active holes in timber you can see and reach is a DIY job, and often a genuinely cheap one — a single tin at somewhere around £25–£30 covers most domestic cases, which is why what a woodworm finding costs to put right so often lands at the low end of the range rather than the alarming end. Get a proper look from a building survey instead where the damage is extensive, where timber sections look structurally reduced, where you can’t reach or see the affected areas, or where the beetle isn’t common furniture beetle at all — house longhorn beetle (see below) and death watch beetle in large old hardwoods both warrant a specialist rather than a tin from the shed.

What salesmen push that you can skip. An insurance-backed 20 or 30-year guarantee sounds reassuring, but for a straightforward, accessible domestic case it is largely paying for paperwork you don’t need — useful when a mortgage lender specifically asks for one, otherwise not. The same goes for pressure to spray an entire roof structure “to be safe” when only one corner shows any sign of activity: treating dry, uninfested timber protects against nothing and mostly protects the contractor’s invoice.

Questions I get asked

How do I know if woodworm is still active? Look for pale, sharp-edged exit holes with loose cream frass nearby or falling out when you tap the timber, ideally spotted during the April–September flight season. Old holes are dark, often painted or varnished over, with no dust and no give when you probe gently with a bradawl. When in doubt, mark a few holes with a pencil and check again in a few months — new dust or new holes nearby confirms activity; nothing changing points to history.

Do I need to treat every exit hole I find? No. If the timber is dry, well-ventilated and the holes are clearly old, treatment adds little — the beetle needs damp or decayed wood to thrive and dry structural timber is naturally poor habitat. Treatment earns its keep where you can see or reasonably suspect current activity, or as a precaution on timber you know has been damp until recently.

Is woodworm treatment safe to use indoors? The low-odour and water-based products on this list are designed for exactly that, but ventilate the space while it’s wet, keep pets and children out until it’s dry, and follow the label’s stated re-entry time. Older solvent-heavy formulations need more caution and more airflow — if a loft is over a bedroom, treat it when the room won’t be slept in that night.

What is house longhorn beetle, and why does it matter more? It’s a much more destructive species than common furniture beetle, capable of seriously weakening structural softwood, and in the UK it is essentially confined to parts of north-west Surrey and neighbouring areas of Hampshire and Berkshire — which is why Building Regulations specifically flag roof timbers in that zone for treatment. Its exit holes are larger and more oval than common furniture beetle’s neat round ones. If you’re in that area, or the holes look unusually large, that’s a case for a specialist survey rather than a tin from this list.
