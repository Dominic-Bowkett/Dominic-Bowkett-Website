Underpinning
cost in
2026

Underpinning a single wall typically costs £6,000–£25,000 in 2026, and a full property £20,000–£50,000+. Most subsidence never gets there — it’s the insurer’s engineer, not a contractor’s quote, that actually decides whether you need it.

Published 3 Aug 2026 Reading time · 9 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Underpinning a single wall — the most common domestic job, usually one elevation of a semi or terrace — typically costs £6,000–£25,000 in 2026, with industry cost guides putting the average nearer £13,500. A full property, where every external wall needs it, runs £20,000–£50,000 or more. Those are the numbers that get quoted back at frightened homeowners within a day of the first crack appearing.

They’re also, for most people, the wrong number to be looking at first. Something like 70–80% of subsidence cases never get underpinned at all — they’re resolved by dealing with whatever’s actually causing the ground to move: a thirsty tree, a leaking drain, clay that shrinks and swells with the seasons. Which situation you’re in isn’t decided by a builder’s quote. It’s decided by a monitoring period, usually run by your insurer’s own engineer over several months, and getting that sequence right — insurer first, contractor a long way second — matters more to the eventual bill than which underpinning method anyone recommends.

I diagnose movement for a living. I don’t underpin houses or fell trees, so I’ve no reason to talk you into the expensive answer before the cheap one has been ruled out. What follows is 2026 pricing for underpinning and the wider subsidence repair bill, what actually drives the cost, and the insurance route that decides how much of this you ever need to spend.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 3 August 2026 against MyJobQuote’s and Home Repair Guide’s 2026 cost guides, cross-referenced against each other rather than taken from one source alone. As with most trades in this series, London and the South East — Dom’s patch included — trend meaningfully above the national figures quoted here.

So as a rough guide: if the insurer’s engineer confirms the cause is a tree or a cracked drain, most homeowners spend somewhere in the low thousands and never underpin anything. If underpinning genuinely is the answer, a single wall on an ordinary semi lands around the £13,500 average; a full-property job on a more seriously affected house runs into five figures, occasionally six.

What drives the price

Method. Traditional mass concrete underpinning runs roughly £1,200–£1,800 per metre of wall, the cheapest and most common approach on ordinary domestic foundations. Beam-and-base and mini-piled methods, needed where the ground is poor or access is tight, run £2,000–£3,000 per metre. Resin injection, which stabilises the soil under the foundation rather than replacing it, is quicker and less disruptive again, though it doesn’t suit every ground condition.

Wall length and dig depth. You’re paying for metres of wall underpinned to a specified depth, not for the size of the house above it. A short run underpinned deep into clay can cost more than a longer run on shallower, more stable ground.

Site investigation and design. A structural engineer’s report, needed before any competent underpinning proposal, typically costs £300–£1,500, sometimes charged as a fixed fee, sometimes at £75–£125 an hour. Building Regulations approval, which underpinning always needs, adds £750 or more on top.

Access and party walls. Excavating around and under a shared wall usually triggers the Party Wall Act, and a party wall agreement can cost up to £1,000 per neighbour on top of the building work itself. Where a run of scaffolding or a skip is needed, that’s usually priced separately again.

Region. London and the South East trend above the national figures quoted here, in line with most trades in this series — one 2026 guide put the London gap at an extra £1,000–£10,000 on the total job.

Before you call a contractor: the insurance route

The single most consequential decision on a suspected subsidence job happens before any quote is requested: who you call first. Most standard buildings insurance policies cover subsidence, but almost every one of them applies a separate, higher excess specifically for a subsidence claim — typically around £1,000, regardless of your normal policy excess, rising to £2,500–£5,000 if the property already has a subsidence history. Calling your insurer first, before a builder has been anywhere near the house, is what keeps that excess as the ceiling on your cost rather than the starting point for a separately-funded repair.

Once you report it, the insurer typically appoints a loss adjuster and a structural or geotechnical engineer, who install tell-tale gauges across any cracks and monitor them, usually over six to twelve months, sometimes longer where clay soils and nearby trees make the seasonal picture harder to read. It’s a frustrating wait if the crack looks alarming from day one, but the monitoring period is what tells the engineer whether the ground is still moving or has already settled — and that answer is what determines whether you need underpinning at all, or whether tree removal, a drain repair, or crack stitching and a clean bill of health is the actual outcome. Only around 20–30% of monitored cases end up underpinned.

The trap worth naming: a contractor who turns up before the cause is established and proposes underpinning on the spot is skipping a step your insurer will want followed, and jumping ahead with unauthorised work risks paying for it yourself rather than through the claim. If you’re ever uncertain whether the insurer’s own assessment is being rushed, or the property isn’t covered at all, an independent structural engineer’s opinion is worth commissioning separately before anything is dug up.

What it means when you come to sell

This is usually the second panic, once the first has passed. Untreated or undeclared subsidence is a genuinely difficult problem to sell through: many lenders won’t mortgage it, which pushes a sale toward cash buyers only and can cut the achievable price by 20–25% or more. Professionally resolved subsidence is a much smaller issue — a house that’s been underpinned, monitored and signed off, ideally with a Certificate of Structural Adequacy in the file, is generally mortgageable again and typically only knocks around 5% off value against a comparable unaffected house, though it can still take longer to find a buyer. Specialist subsidence-history buildings insurance is usually available going forward, at a higher premium and excess than a standard policy, and having that quote ready is worth more to a buyer’s solicitor than any amount of reassurance from the seller.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • Underpinning proposed before the cause is confirmed or before an insurer’s monitoring period has run its course.
  • No structural engineer’s design or calculations behind the specific method being quoted — "underpinning" isn’t a spec on its own.
  • No Building Regulations application included, which underpinning always needs.
  • Party wall costs and access left vague, rather than itemised separately from the underpinning itself.
  • An insurance-backed guarantee, typically 10 years, that survives the contractor going out of business.
  • A single method quoted with no site investigation — ground conditions, not preference, should decide mass concrete versus piling versus resin.

Questions I get asked

How much does underpinning cost? In 2026, a single wall typically costs £6,000–£25,000, with an industry average around £13,500 for an ordinary 3-bed semi. A full-property job runs £20,000–£50,000 or more. Resin injection, a lighter alternative, is usually £4,000–£17,000. Method, wall length, dig depth and access drive most of the difference, and London and the South East trend above these national figures.

Do I definitely need underpinning if my house has subsidence? Not necessarily, and most of the time no. Roughly 20–30% of monitored subsidence cases actually end up underpinned. The rest are resolved by removing the cause — commonly a thirsty tree, a leaking or cracked drain, or clay shrinkage that settles once the trigger is dealt with — sometimes with crack stitching once an engineer confirms the ground has stopped moving.

Will my insurance cover subsidence repair, and what’s the excess? Most standard buildings policies cover subsidence, but nearly all apply a separate, higher excess for it, typically around £1,000 regardless of your normal excess, rising to £2,500–£5,000 with a known history. Report it to your insurer before commissioning any repair work — calling a contractor first can complicate the claim.

Can I sell a house that’s been underpinned? Yes, and the paperwork matters more than the fact of it. Professionally resolved subsidence, documented with a Certificate of Structural Adequacy and a completed monitoring period, is generally mortgageable and typically costs around 5% against comparable unaffected homes. Untreated or undocumented subsidence is a harder sell — often restricted to cash buyers, with value cut by 20–25% or more.
