Green roofs are one of the first things I notice when I pull up outside a house. A scatter of moss along the north slope of a tile roof is common, harmless in the short term, and the sort of thing nobody thinks about until a buyer, a mortgage valuer or a gutter full of green sludge brings it up. Left alone for years on a shaded slope, it holds moisture against the tiles, lifts edges, and drops into the gutters.

The instinct is to hire a pressure washer for the weekend. Please don’t. A jet of water at that pressure strips the surface from concrete tiles, forces water up under laps, and can drive moss and grit into the gutters and the drains below. Roofing trade advice, including from manufacturers such as Marley, is generally against it. The gentler routes are a brush or scraper from a safe position, a biocide that you spray on and leave, and a metal strip at the ridge to stop it coming back. Those are what this guide covers.

One thing first. Moss is usually a symptom of shade, damp and a roof that is not drying out, not a fault in itself. If the roof is old, the tiles are porous or the battens are suspect, treating the green does nothing for the cause. Look from the ground with a pair of binoculars before anyone goes up, and if the roof is nearing the end of its life, the new roof cost guide sets out what replacement really involves.

How I judge a roof moss killer

Is it made for roofs? A path cleaner and a roof biocide are not the same thing on the label. Look for tiles, slates or roofs named in the instructions, and follow the dilution. If the label does not name your surface, I would not guess.

Does it need scrubbing or rinsing? On a roof, the best treatments are the ones that do not need either. Anything that depends on someone walking on the tiles with a brush adds risk and breakage. Leave-on products are safer and kinder to the covering.

Where does the run-off go? Biocides do not vanish. The wash goes down the roof, through the gutters and into the downpipe or soakaway. Check the label for guidance on plants, ponds and watercourses, and keep treated water off planting and out of surface water drains where you can.

How long does the effect last? Treatment kills what is there. It does not stop spores landing next year. That is the job of a zinc or copper strip, and of fixing whatever keeps the roof damp: overhanging trees, blocked gutters, a north slope that never sees sun.

Can you apply it safely? Most roof treatments can be applied from the ground with a long-reach sprayer or from a safe platform. If it needs someone on a pitched roof, that is a job for a roofer with proper edge protection, not a weekend on a ladder.

05

Zinc roof strip for moss prevention

Best long-term prevention

Zinc strips are the old trick for stopping moss coming back. A strip is fixed under the ridge or the top course of tiles, and as rain runs over it small amounts of zinc wash down the slope and make the surface less hospitable to moss. It does nothing for moss that is already established, and it only protects the slope below it. Treat it as the second step after a clean, not as a cleaning product. Fixing it means working at ridge height, which is a roofer’s job unless you have proper access.

For

  • Works continuously with each rainfall
  • Cheap compared with repeated treatments
  • Quiet and invisible once fitted

Against

  • Does not kill existing moss
  • Only protects the slope below it, and needs fixing at ridge level

Copper roof strip for moss prevention

Copper does the same job as zinc and is generally reckoned to last longer, though it is dearer and takes time to weather. What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Do not pressure-wash a roof. It is the most common mistake I see, and the one that costs most. High-pressure water erodes the face of concrete tiles, removes protective coatings, forces water under laps and can open a leak where there was none. It also fills the gutters with debris. If a contractor offers a roof jet-wash as the whole answer, ask how they will protect the tiles and where the washings go.

Treat first, then prevent. A biocide kills the moss; a zinc or copper strip slows the return. They are different jobs and you usually want both. Spend the money in that order: clean, then strip.

Fix the cause where you can. Cut back overhanging branches, clear the gutters and see whether a shaded slope can be let into more light. A gutter vacuum and gutter guards help with the debris, and if the gutters are failing altogether the guttering replacement cost guide is the honest comparison.

Do not scrape dry moss off in a hurry. A scraper or brush pushed up under tile edges breaks the slip and can lift or crack old clay and slate. Let a biocide soften the growth first, and brush lightly, if at all, once it is dead. Work from the ground or a proper platform, never off a ladder leaning on the tiles, and see the roof ladders guide if access really is the problem.

Moss on a roof is not always a fault. A small amount of moss on a sound roof is cosmetic. Heavy growth, lifted tiles, a sagging ridge or damp in the loft is a different matter, and a roof inspection will tell you which you have. Do not use a sealant to chase a leak you have not traced; the roof sealants guide explains why.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. “Roof coatings” that paint over the moss problem, “one visit, 10-year guarantee” cleaning packages and anything sold on a doorstep. Coatings change the colour of the tile and can trap moisture. A guarantee is only as good as the company that wrote it.

Questions I get asked

What is the best moss killer for a roof? For most pitched tile or slate roofs, a spray-on, leave-on biocide that is named for roofs, followed by a zinc or copper strip at the ridge. Wet & Forget is the most widely recommended of its kind, and Roxil is a sound alternative where walls need doing too.

Can I pressure-wash moss off my roof? I would not. It can strip the face of concrete tiles, drive water under laps and clog the gutters, and roofing trade advice is generally against it. A treatment that you apply and leave is gentler and cheaper in the long run.

Do zinc and copper strips really stop moss? They help. Rain washes tiny amounts of metal down the slope, which makes it harder for moss to take hold below the strip. They do not clear moss that is already there and they only protect the roof beneath them, so treat first and fit the strip after.

How often should a roof be treated? It depends on shade, trees and how damp the site is. A shaded north slope may need attention every year or two, while a sunny roof may need nothing. If it needs treating every spring, it is the cause that needs fixing, not the label on the bottle.

If a roof is heavily mossed, you are buying a house with a green roof, or the moss has come with a ceiling stain, a building survey will look at the coverings, the ridge, the gutters and the loft and say whether it needs cleaning or replacing.
