Every building survey includes a roof I am not going to walk on. The question is how well I can read it from the ground, and the oldest answer is still one of the best: a decent pair of binoculars. From the pavement, the far side of the garden or an upstairs window across the street, 8x magnification turns a grey slope into legible detail — slipped and delaminating tiles, cracked ridge flaunching, nail sickness in a slate roof, lead flashings lifting at the abutment, moss damming a valley, a chimney stack leaning out of plumb. That is real evidence, and it goes straight into the report.

Drones changed the game for the difficult cases — I’ve written a separate guide to drones for roof inspections — but a drone needs airspace you’re allowed to fly in, weather that permits it, charged batteries, and in plenty of suburban streets a conversation with the neighbours. Binoculars need none of that. They work in drizzle, next to an airfield, under trees, in a conservation area, on a fifteen-minute EPC visit where a drone was never leaving the van.

The catch is that binoculars are sold on numbers that mislead. Magnification is the headline figure, and it is the one that matters least. What follows is how I’d actually choose a pair for survey work, and seven that the spec sheets and owner feedback support.

How I judge binoculars for roof work

Steadiness beats magnification. The counter-intuitive truth of hand-held optics: 8x shows you more than 10x, because you can hold it still. Every wobble of your hands is multiplied by the magnification, and at 10x and above the fine detail you paid for is smeared by tremor — exactly the detail you need to tell a hairline crack in a ridge tile from a shadow. 8x also gives a wider field of view, so tracking along fifteen metres of ridge line is a sweep rather than a hunt. The exception to the rule is image stabilisation, which is a genuine technology rather than a marketing line — more on that with the Canon below.

Objective size against bag weight. The second number (the 42 in 8x42) is the objective lens diameter in millimetres. Bigger means brighter — and heavier. A 42mm objective gives a 5mm+ exit pupil at 8x, which keeps a north-facing slope readable on a dull December morning; a 25mm compact gives you a dim little image the moment the light drops. But a 42mm pair weighs 500–700g, and everything in a survey bag is a negotiation. For me 8x42 is the right compromise; 8x30 or 8x32 is defensible if the bag is already full of damp meter, laser measure and borescope.

Close focus. Bird-watchers care about this and surveyors should too. On a terraced street you are often standing eight or ten metres from the eaves, not eighty. A pair that focuses down to 2–3m lets you read the mortar in a verge from the pavement below it, and doubles as a tool for examining high-level render, cills and hopper heads you can’t reach a ladder to.

Eye relief, if you wear glasses. Eye relief is how far the image floats behind the eyepiece. Under about 15mm, spectacle wearers lose the edge of the picture. Anything designed with 15–20mm and twist-up eyecups works with glasses on — worth checking the spec line before anything else if that’s you.

Field manners. Waterproofing and nitrogen purging (so the internals don’t fog when you walk out of a warm van into a cold morning), rubber armour that shrugs off the footwell, and a focus wheel you can turn in gloves. A lens that shipped with a stiff, precise diopter adjustment matters more than any coating acronym on the box.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Technique is worth more than glass. A £60 pair used well beats a £500 pair waved about. Work the roof systematically, the way you’d read a wall: start at the ridge and track its whole length (humps, gaps, missing mortar, slipped ridge tiles), then each slope in overlapping passes from ridge to eaves, then the details — hips, valleys, verges, abutment flashings, the chimney and its flaunching, and finally the rainwater goods. Brace your elbows on something: a car roof, a wall, a gatepost. Sitting with elbows on knees is worth roughly one full step of magnification.

Use the light. Roofs read best in low, raking light — early or late in the day, when every proud tile and dished slate throws a shadow. At midday under flat cloud a defective roof can look immaculate. If a roof matters and the light is wrong, look again from a different angle or come back; and always read a slope from at least two positions, because a slipped tile hides behind its neighbour from one direction and waves at you from another.

What the listings push that you can skip. “High-power” magnification is the big one — anything hand-held beyond 10x without stabilisation is a downgrade dressed as an upgrade, and the 30x60 and 80x zoom compacts flooding the listings are toys with fictional numbers printed on them. Zoom binoculars in general trade away brightness and sharpness for a feature you’ll never use. Ruby-red “anti-glare” coatings, gimmicky night-vision claims (a binocular gathers light, it doesn’t make it) and bundled phone adapters are all decoration. Spend the money on the glass, the coatings and the build.

Know where the tool ends. Binoculars tell you the far slope exists; they don’t show it to you. Anything you cannot see from the ground — hidden valleys, flat roofs behind parapets, the back of the chimney, box gutters — needs a drone, a pole camera or, where the evidence justifies it, scaffold and a closer look. Binoculars are the reconnaissance; they establish where the questions are. A survey report should be honest about which slopes were inspected, from where, and with what.

Questions I get asked

Are 8x or 10x binoculars better for surveying? 8x, for almost everyone. The extra reach of 10x is cancelled by amplified hand shake, a narrower field of view and a dimmer image — three penalties for one benefit you can usually get by taking four steps forward. The genuine 10x cases are buildings you physically cannot approach: set-back rural properties, towers, roofs read across a road. If fine detail at distance is routinely the job, the better answer is stabilised 10x — the Canon — or a drone.

Can you really inspect a roof with binoculars instead of a drone? For the slopes you can see, yes — surveyors did it for generations, and a ground-level binocular inspection is still the standard first pass on most house surveys. You can identify slipped, cracked and delaminating tiles, nail sickness, defective ridge and hip mortar, lifting flashings, blocked valleys and leaning stacks perfectly well from the pavement. What binoculars cannot do is see what ground level cannot see: hidden valleys, parapet gutters, flat roof coverings, rear slopes with no vantage point. An honest inspection uses both — binoculars first, drone or access equipment where the geometry demands it.

Are compact binoculars any good for roof inspections? They’re better than squinting, and a 25mm compact in the glovebox gets used more often than a 42mm pair left at home. But be clear-eyed about the physics: a small objective means a small exit pupil, which means a dim image in exactly the flat grey light most UK surveys happen in, and the 10x–12x magnifications compacts are sold with multiply hand shake. If a compact is the budget, choose 8x25 over 12x25 every time.

What do image-stabilised binoculars actually do? A gyro-sensed prism or lens group inside the binocular tilts continuously to cancel the tremor of your hands — the same optical stabilisation as a camera lens. The effect on fine detail is dramatic: steadying the image lets your eye integrate detail that shake otherwise smears away, which is why a stabilised 10x resolves more in the hand than a conventional 12x or 15x. For survey work it is the one genuine upgrade over a good 8x42 — at the cost of batteries, weather fragility and several hundred pounds.
