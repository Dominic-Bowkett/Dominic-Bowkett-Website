Every domestic EPC involves a loft hatch. The RdSAP methodology wants to know what’s up there — insulation depth, roof construction, any room-in-roof — and “the homeowner thinks it’s about 100mm” is not evidence. So a ladder comes into the house with me on every job, and because the job might be a Victorian terrace with a hatch above the stairwell or a bungalow with a hatch in an airing cupboard, it has to be a ladder that folds down small enough to carry through someone’s hallway without redecorating it. That is what a telescopic ladder is for.

A surveyor’s ladder gets extended and collapsed perhaps a thousand times a year, in other people’s homes, boots off and full body weight on it above a staircase. That use pattern makes me fussy in ways a weekend DIYer doesn’t need to be, and it is why this guide keeps returning to two things: the EN 131 Professional certification, and what the ladder actually weighs and measures when closed, because it lives in a car boot next to the rest of the kit.

The category has a real quality problem. At the top end you have properly engineered aluminium that locks positively and barely flexes. At the bottom you have units that look identical in the photos and are not — one 3.8m ladder sold through Amazon was the subject of a government product safety report because it bent excessively under load. I’ll come back to that. First, the ones I’d trust.

How I judge a telescopic ladder

EN 131 Professional certification, non-negotiable. EN 131 is the European ladder standard; since 2018 it has split into Professional and Non-Professional classes, and Part 6 covers telescopic ladders specifically. Professional means the ladder is rated for a 150kg load and has passed a harder durability regime, including repeated cycling — which is exactly what survey work does to a ladder. If I’m using it for paid work, my insurer, the Work at Height Regulations and common sense all point the same way: EN 131-6 Professional, from a manufacturer willing to put the certificate in writing.

Closed length and real weight. The spec sheet numbers matter because the ladder lives in the boot alongside a damp meter case, a borescope, a drone case and a laser. A 2.9m ladder that closes to 0.79m fits across any boot; a 3.8m unit that closes to 1.09m has to go on the back seat of some cars. Weight matters twice: once at 7.45am lifting it out, and again when you’re carrying it up a customer’s staircase in socks.

The closing mechanism. A telescopic ladder collapsing is several kilograms of aluminium sections dropping through each other. Early designs took fingertips off; the injury even has case law. Soft-close (Werner’s term) or a controlled-descent latch (Xtend+Climb’s Lightning Latch) lowers the sections gently. On a ladder you close a dozen times a day, this is the difference between a tool and a workplace incident.

Locking indicators you can see. The better ladders show a visible indicator — green when a rung is locked. When you’re setting up in a dim landing under a loft hatch, being able to glance down the stiles and confirm every section is locked is worth real money. A rung that hasn’t engaged fully is how telescopic ladders hurt people.

Flex under load. Stand on the fourth rung of a good ladder and it feels like a ladder. Stand on the fourth rung of a cheap one and it feels like a diving board. Some flex is inherent to the telescopic design — more sections, more joints — but the gap between the best and worst here is enormous, and you only discover it at height.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The certification is the whole game at the bottom of the market. In 2024 the Office for Product Safety and Standards published a product safety report on a WolfWise 3.8m telescopic ladder sold through Amazon: it presented a serious risk of injury because it bent excessively under load, and the listing was withdrawn. That ladder had thousands of glowing reviews. Reviews measure whether the ladder arrived on time and looked shiny; they do not measure what happens at cycle four hundred with 95kg of surveyor on rung nine. An EN 131-6 Professional certificate from a named test house does. When a listing says “EN131” in the title but the manufacturer won’t show the certificate, treat the claim as decoration.

Maximum height is oversold. The marketing photographs always show the ladder fully extended against a wall. In practice a telescopic ladder is at its worst fully extended — maximum flex, maximum consequence — and most of its life is spent at half height under a hatch. Buy the length your work actually needs, not the biggest number: for loft access that is 2.6–3.2m, not 3.8m. The reverse is also true: don’t buy short and stand on the top rungs. The top three rungs of any leaning ladder are not for standing on.

Weight claims deserve suspicion in both directions. A suspiciously light 3.8m ladder has thin walls; wall thickness is where budget units save money and where they fail. The professional 3.8m models cluster around 14–15.5kg for a reason. If a £90 ladder claims the same length at 11kg, the missing metal came from somewhere.

If you use it for work, inspect it and write that down. Telescopic ladders are working-at-height equipment, so the Work at Height Regulations 2005 apply, and HSE guidance expects pre-use checks plus a recorded detailed inspection at sensible intervals. A proper routine takes five minutes a month: extend fully, check every lock engages and every indicator shows, look for bent stiles, cracked feet and grit in the tubes (grit is what kills the locking mechanism), and log it. If a customer’s loft insulation has been raining grit into the tubes, a blast of air and a silicone-free dry lubricant — never oil, which holds the grit — keeps the action honest. A ladder that won’t pass that check gets retired, not “kept for light jobs”.

Things the listings push that you can ignore: carry bags (fine, but a bungee cord works), “16.5ft” imperial headline figures that quietly include your own reach, claimed ratings in pounds rather than the EN class, and colour choices. One thing pushed as an extra that is actually worth having: a stabiliser bar on anything 3.2m and over. Wider feet, less wobble, better manners on smooth floors.

Questions I get asked

Are telescopic ladders safe?

A certified one, used correctly, yes — surveyors and trades put their full weight on them every working day. The genuine risks are specific: uncertified budget units that flex or collapse (the WolfWise safety report above is the documented example), fingers caught in closing sections on ladders without soft close or rung-spacing protection, and sections that haven’t fully locked before someone climbs. Buy EN 131 certified — Professional class for work — check every lock before climbing, set the ladder at 75 degrees (one out for every four up), and keep the tubes clean so the locks keep working. Do that and a telescopic ladder is no more dangerous than any other leaning ladder.

What is EN 131 certification?

EN 131 is the European standard for portable ladders, and since 2018 it has two classes: Professional and Non-Professional. Both require a 150kg static load capacity, but the Professional class is tested for repeated, frequent use — harder durability and cycle testing — which is what distinguishes a ladder for daily trade work from one for occasional home use. Part 6 of the standard (EN 131-6) covers telescopic ladders specifically, adding tests for the locking mechanisms and the telescoping action. For anyone using a ladder at work, EN 131 Professional is effectively the baseline: it is what an insurer, a principal contractor or the HSE will expect to see on the kit list.

What length telescopic ladder do I need for a loft hatch?

Less than most people buy. A typical UK ceiling is 2.3–2.4m; with the ladder leant at the correct 75-degree angle against the hatch edge, a 2.6m ladder reaches comfortably and a 2.9m gives you margin for taller Victorian ceilings and stairwell hatches. A 3.8m ladder will do the job but you’ll only ever use two-thirds of it indoors, and you’ll carry the extra 5kg through the house every time. My rule: 2.6–2.9m if the ladder is only for loft access, 3.2m if you want one ladder for hatches plus occasional single-storey outside work, 3.8m only if eaves and gutter lines are a regular part of your week.

Can telescopic ladders be used professionally?

Yes, provided the ladder is EN 131 Professional class and you treat it as work equipment. The Work at Height Regulations don’t ban any ladder type; they require the equipment to be suitable, maintained and inspected, and ladder use to be justified for short-duration, low-risk tasks — which describes a loft-hatch inspection exactly. In practice that means buying Professional-certified kit (every ladder in the top six above qualifies except where noted), doing a pre-use check each job, keeping a simple inspection record, and replacing the ladder when it stops passing. What you cannot sensibly do is put a £90 Non-Professional unit on a professional risk assessment; if something goes wrong, that purchase decision will be the first thing examined.

The ladder gets me to the loft hatch; what happens above it — insulation depths, roof condition, ventilation, signs of damp — is the actual job.
