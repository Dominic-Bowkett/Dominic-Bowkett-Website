Every surveyor ends up with a jacket that lives in the van. It is not the waterproof, which comes out for rain, and it is not the good coat, which stays in the house. It is the middle layer: the one that goes on for a walk round the outside of a Victorian terrace on a grey morning, comes off in the warm kitchen, and gets thrown over the passenger seat while you write up the notes. For most people, that is a softshell or a fleece.

They are not the same thing, and the difference matters. A fleece is warmth: soft, light and cheap, but the wind goes straight through it. A softshell is a tougher, tighter-woven fabric that blocks wind and sheds light rain, at the cost of being a little less cosy. A cold loft needs the first and a gusty roof edge needs the second, and a good winter kit usually has both.

This guide sits in the middle of the winter stack. Underneath it goes a thermal base layer; over it, when the weather turns, a waterproof jacket. For the coldest days there are also heated gilets and hand warmers. Six picks follow, from a cheap fleece to a trade-priced softshell, drawn from manufacturers’ descriptions and published owner feedback.

How I judge a softshell or fleece

Wind resistance. This is the whole point of a softshell. If you can feel the wind through the fabric standing at a gable end, it is a fleece wearing a softshell’s label. Look for a tight outer face and a stated wind-resistant or windproof finish.

Showerproof, not waterproof. A water-repellent finish lets light rain bead and roll off for a while. It will not survive an hour on a wet roof, and any softshell sold as fully waterproof deserves suspicion. Treat it as a layer you can wear while it spits, and carry a proper shell for when it does not.

Cut and movement. You will be reaching up into a hatch, crouching in a void and climbing a ladder. A stretch fabric and sleeves that do not ride up make a bigger difference than a few more degrees of insulation. Check the length too: a jacket that rides above your belt when you reach is a jacket that lets the draught in.

Pockets. A zipped chest pocket for a phone and a pen, and hand pockets that sit clear of a tool bag or harness strap, are worth more than extra branding. Pockets that sit under a belt are lost behind a pouch.

Durability and washing. Loft hatches and joist ends are hard on fabric. Cheaper jackets pill and snag sooner, and a fleece will fluff with repeated washing. Anything that survives a 30°C machine wash and dries overnight is a practical choice for something that gets dirty weekly.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The first thing that matters is layering. One thick jacket is a worse answer than three thin layers, because a survey swings from cold to warm to cold and you need to strip and add in the time it takes to walk from the van to the front door. A base layer, a fleece or softshell, and a shell in the bag will beat a single heavy coat in nearly every house I walk into.

The second is that clothing is not tools, and it is fine to buy it cheaply. A fleece that costs the same as a sandwich lunch and does a decent job is better value than a premium jacket you are scared to take into a loft. Spend on the layer you wear most, and treat the rest as consumables.

What salesmen push, and what does not matter much, starts with “waterproof” softshells. A softshell that is truly waterproof has lost the stretch and breathability that made it a softshell in the first place. Next come fabric weights quoted in grams without a comparison, and fashion colours. If you work near moving vehicles, a hi-vis vest over the top is the cheap fix, and work gloves and work trousers complete the outfit.

The last point is washing and ageing. A loft puts fibreglass, dust and sometimes droppings into whatever you are wearing, so I would never hang a work jacket in the house after a bad loft. Keep it in the van, shake it out before you get back into the car, and wash it on its own.

Questions I get asked

Is a softshell warmer than a fleece? Not on its own. A fleece traps more warm air, and a softshell blocks the wind that strips that warm air away. In a still cold loft a fleece is warmer. On a windy roof edge or garden, a softshell often feels warmer because the wind cannot get in. That is why they work best as a pair.

Is a softshell waterproof? No. It is showerproof at best, using a water-repellent finish that beads light rain. For a wet roof or an afternoon in a garden in the rain, you want a proper waterproof jacket over the top, not a softshell asked to do a job it was not designed for.

What should I wear for a winter survey? A thermal base layer, a fleece or softshell, and a waterproof in the bag. Add gloves and a hat, and warm boots for the cold floors. You can strip back to the base layer in the warm rooms and add the shell on the roof or in the garden.

How do I wash a fleece or softshell? Follow the care label, but a 30°C wash, a low spin and a hang-dry is the safe default. Avoid fabric conditioner, which clogs the pile of a fleece and the water-repellent finish of a softshell, and keep it away from hook-and-loop fastenings, which pull at the fibres.
