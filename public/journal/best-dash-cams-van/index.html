The best
dash cams
for van and site work

The van doesn’t stay parked outside the office. It sits on a stranger’s drive for two hours while you’re in their loft, tucked down a farm track for an EPC, or nose to tail in traffic between three appointments before lunch. A dash cam earns its keep less for the driving itself and more for what happens when the van’s sitting unattended, or when somebody else’s account of a scrape down a narrow lane doesn’t match yours. Here’s what actually matters when buying one for a working vehicle, and seven worth having in the cab.

A fair chunk of any given week in this job is spent on the road rather than in a house. Between an early RdSAP appointment in one village, a full building survey across town, and an afternoon retrofit assessment somewhere else entirely, the van covers real miles on roads that were never built for anything wider than a horse and cart — single-track lanes with passing places, farm gateways, and the sort of on-street parking in a Victorian terrace where wing mirrors get folded in as a matter of habit. None of that is dramatic driving, but it’s exactly the kind of driving where a low-speed dispute over who clipped what is common, and where there’s rarely an independent witness standing around to back up either account.

Then there’s the van itself, parked and unattended for most of the working day. A loft survey or a full building survey can easily run two or three hours, and for the whole of that time the van sits on the client’s drive or the nearest bit of kerb with a laser measure, a thermal camera and a bag of other kit in the back — exactly the sort of load that makes a work van a more attractive target than the family hatchback next to it. A dash cam with a genuine parking mode doesn’t stop somebody trying the door handle, but it does mean there’s footage afterwards instead of a shrug.

The insurance angle matters just as much and gets talked about less. A low-speed knock in a supermarket car park, a give-way dispute on a blind bend, a cyclist who appears from nowhere on a rural lane — these are exactly the incidents where two accounts disagree and a claim drags on for months without footage to settle it quickly. What follows is what I’d actually look for when buying a dash cam for a vehicle that works for a living rather than one that mostly sits on a driveway, and seven worth having.

How I judge a dash cam for a working vehicle

Genuine hardwired parking mode, not just power from the cigarette lighter. A dash cam plugged into a 12V socket stops recording the moment the ignition goes off, which is precisely when a work van parked outside a client’s house is most exposed. A hardwire kit taps the fuse box for a trickle of power and includes a low-voltage cut-off so it can’t flatten the battery — without one, parking mode is a spec-sheet feature that never actually switches on.

Night vision that resolves a number plate, not just brightens the picture. Plenty of budget cameras produce a washed-out, grainy image after dark that looks fine on a phone screen and is useless zoomed in. Sony STARVIS and STARVIS 2 sensors are the current benchmark for genuinely usable low-light footage, and worth checking for by name rather than trusting resolution alone.

GPS logging, because it’s evidence beyond the crash itself. A GPS overlay proves speed and location at the moment of an incident, which matters to an insurer weighing up a disputed claim. It’s also a quiet, incidental record of exactly where and when the van was on a given day — handy if a site visit is ever queried months later.

Storage and loop recording that won’t quietly fail. A dash cam that’s recording almost constantly wears through microSD cards faster than most other uses, and a corrupted card is worthless exactly when it’s needed. Higher-end models use more efficient HEVC/H.265 compression to fit more footage per gigabyte and are kinder to the card in the long run; check the manufacturer’s recommended card spec rather than assuming any microSD will do.

Channel count matched to what’s actually at risk. A front-only camera covers the driving itself; front-and-rear adds cover for rear-end shunts and reversing disputes, both common in van work; a third, cabin-facing channel adds a record of who was actually in the vehicle and when, which matters more for a commercial vehicle than a private car.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Hardwiring isn’t optional if parking protection is the point. A dash cam running off a 12V socket switches off with the engine, which is exactly when a van parked outside an unfamiliar address is most at risk. A hardwire kit is typically £15–£25 on top of the camera and either a straightforward DIY job behind the fuse box or a half-hour fitting job at a garage — without it, parking mode is a line on the spec sheet that never actually does anything.

4K sounds like the obvious upgrade, but the sensor matters more than the resolution. A 4K image from a weak sensor in poor light is still a soft, grainy mess once you try to zoom in on a plate after dark. Sony STARVIS and STARVIS 2 sensors, named specifically on the spec sheet, are a better guide to genuine low-light usability than the megapixel count on its own.

Mirror-style dash cams look tidy but are a hassle when it matters. Replacing the rear-view mirror with a screen is popular for the extra rear visibility it gives while driving, but reviewing and exporting footage after an incident is usually slower and fiddlier than a standard dash cam’s companion app — not the trade-off worth making if the footage itself is the whole point of owning one.

Cloud and LTE features are a genuine business tool, not just a gimmick, if they’re actually used. Being able to check the van’s parked and undisturbed from inside a loft, rather than walking back out to look, has real value on a long survey. It’s only worth paying for if the ongoing data plan gets used, though — it’s an easy feature to pay extra for once and then ignore.

A cabin-facing third channel isn’t about not trusting yourself. It’s about having a record of who else was in the vehicle, and when, if that’s ever disputed — a genuinely useful addition for a commercial vehicle that occasionally carries a colleague or a client, and largely irrelevant if the van’s always driven solo.

Questions I get asked

Do I actually need a dash cam for a work van, or is that just for personal cars? If anything, a van used for daily site visits has more to gain than an ordinary car — more time on the road, more time parked unattended outside unfamiliar addresses with tools and equipment inside, and more exposure to disputed low-speed knocks on narrow lanes and in car parks. The case for one is stronger for a working vehicle, not weaker.

Will fitting a dash cam actually reduce my van insurance premium? Some UK insurers offer a discount for a fitted dash cam, typically in the region of 10–15 per cent, though it varies by insurer and isn’t universal — worth checking with your own provider before assuming it’s automatic. Even without a discount, footage that settles a disputed claim quickly, rather than dragging on for months, has its own value.

Do I need front and rear, or is a front-only camera enough? Front-only covers the most common scenario — an incident directly ahead — and is cheaper and simpler to fit. Front-and-rear adds cover for rear-end shunts and reversing disputes, both genuinely common around vans making multiple stops in a day, and is worth the extra cost for that reason alone.

What happens to the footage if the van’s broken into while I’m on site? With a hardwired parking mode active, motion or impact detection triggers a recording automatically, buffered to include the seconds before the trigger as well as after — footage that’s then available on the memory card, and on models with Wi-Fi or cloud connectivity, potentially viewable on a phone before you've even walked back to the van.

A dash cam is one small part of a wider picture of keeping a work van and everything in it secure and accounted for — if you’re logging site visits and photos digitally as well, it’s worth pairing with one of the rugged tablets that can survive being carried between the van and a damp loft all day.
