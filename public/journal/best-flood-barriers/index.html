Autumn is when the phone starts. In Kent and Sussex, with the Medway, the Rother, the Ouse and a lot of small streams that only become rivers in November, somebody always asks whether a flood barrier would have saved their ground floor. The answer is nearly always “partly”, and the part it saves depends on how the water gets in.

Water enters a house through doors and thresholds, through air bricks, up through the floor and walls, and back up the drains. A door guard deals with the first. An air brick cover deals with the second. Neither touches the rest. Most of the disappointment people have with flood products comes from buying one for a problem the house does not have, or not having one for the problem it does.

This guide covers the products people actually buy, what I look for, and the honest limits. If you want to know how likely your address is to flood in the first place, start with the flood risk assessment cost guide, and if you are buying in a flood zone, the house survey cost guide explains what the inspection will and will not tell you.

How I judge a flood barrier

What route of entry it covers. A product that protects a doorway does nothing for the air bricks. Walk the ground floor outside first and count the openings: doors, air bricks, vents, pipe penetrations, low windows. Buy against the list, not against the picture on the box.

How long it takes to deploy. Flood warnings can give you hours or minutes. Anything that needs two people and a spanner at 3am is a product you will not use. Cover plates and gelling pads win here.

Fit and seal. A barrier fails at its edges. Made-to-measure frames with a compression seal do better than a generic board in an uneven Victorian opening. If your door reveals are out of true, expect to need a fitted product.

Independent testing. Look for products tested under a recognised scheme such as the BSI Kitemark for flood protection products. Where a brand does not publish test data, treat claims as marketing.

Storage and reuse. A barrier you cannot find in the shed is no barrier. Flat, light, labelled and kept near the door beats a heavy kit in the loft.

The picks in detail

01

FloodSax

Best overall first buy

FloodSax are flat pads containing an absorbent polymer. Dry, they store in a drawer; wet, they swell into a sandless bag that holds water back. Manufacturer and council literature describe them as lightweight, quick to deploy and biodegradable, and local authorities in flood-prone areas point residents to them. They are a sensible first purchase because they cost little, need no fitting and can be moved to wherever the water turns up. They are a delay, not a seal, and how many you need depends on the depth you are expecting.

For

  • Stores flat and light, no sand to shift
  • Quick to deploy once wet
  • Council flood advice names them as a practical alternative to sandbags

Against

  • Single-use after soaking in most cases
  • Does not seal a doorway on its own

Floodgate Door Barrier

Best premium pick for a doorway

The Floodgate door barrier is a telescopic frame covered in neoprene that jacks across a door opening. Manufacturer information says the design dates from the 1990s, was developed with the University of Wales and is still made in Wales. It is the sort of product I would expect a specialist to recommend for a property that has flooded before. It is dear, it needs measuring to the opening, and the brickwork either side has to be sound enough to take the seal, which a surveyor can check for you.

For

  • Reusable, and lasts years if stored dry
  • Established UK manufacturer

Against

Manthorpe Airbrick Flood Defence

Best for suspended timber floors

Air bricks are a weak point that flood advice often overlooks, because they sit low in the wall and open straight to the void under the floor. The Manthorpe system is a replacement air brick with a cover that closes when floodwater threatens. The condition is that you actually close it, and open it again afterwards, because a permanently sealed void starves the floor timbers of air and invites rot. A cover on every ground-floor air brick is a small job and a sizeable reduction in water entering the building.

For

  • Closes off the route into the underfloor void
  • Cheap compared with a door barrier
  • Low-profile when left open

Against

  • Only works if someone closes it in time
  • Left shut, it will starve the floor of ventilation

AquaStop Door Guard

Best mid-price door option

AquaStop describes its door guard as custom fit, with a lever mechanism that makes installation quick. Made-to-measure matters: a flood guard fails at the gaps, and gaps come from sloppy measurement. The caution that applies to every door barrier applies here. Empty polypropylene sandbags

Best budget pick

Sandbags are the option everyone knows, and the government’s own guidance stresses that they must be filled about two-thirds full and laid overlapping like brickwork to work at all. They leak, they are heavy, and a pile of wet sand afterwards is a disposal job. They are on the list because they are cheap and available when an alert arrives. Treat them as the fallback, not the plan.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Flood water does not only come in at the door. Water presses on walls, and a brick wall is porous. If the water outside is deep or stays for hours, it will find its way through the masonry, through the floor and up through the drains. A door guard buys you time at best. This is why guidance usually describes these products as ways to reduce damage, not to keep a house dry.

The depth limit. Most domestic barriers are meant for shallow water, often around 60cm or less. Beyond that the load on a door or wall can cause structural damage, and the usual advice is to let water in rather than risk the building. Check the stated limit before you buy, and do not stack products to go deeper than they are rated.

Drains and backflow. In many floods the water arrives through the drains first. A non-return valve on the drain, fitted by a plumber, helps with this, and so does a bung over a toilet. It is not glamorous and it is arguably the most useful item in this guide, but it is a fitted job rather than something to order and drop at the door.

Resilience beats resistance. If you flood often, the cheaper answer may be to design for it: solid floors rather than timber, tiled finishes, raised sockets, lime plaster that dries out, and no chipboard kitchen units. Flood-resilient repairs cost less over ten years than replacing a ground floor every time. Raised electrics are a job for a qualified electrician.

What salesmen push that does not matter. “Instant” claims, military-style branding and kits with forty accessories. A cover plate that fits your air brick matters more than a case of gadgets.

The insurance angle. Insurers care about whether you have flooded before and whether you took reasonable steps. Flood Re, the scheme set up to keep premiums affordable for homes at high flood risk, covers many properties built before 2009. Ask your insurer whether fitted protection changes your terms, and keep receipts. After a flood, a damp meter and a dehumidifier are the tools that come out, and a hygrometer tells you when the building has dried enough to redecorate.

Questions I get asked

Are sandbags still worth using? They can help, but they leak and weigh a lot. Government guidance explains how to place them, and many councils now point residents to flood-specific alternatives. If you have to use them, treat them as a way to slow water, not to stop it.

Will a flood barrier stop my house flooding? Not by itself. It covers one route in. The water can still come through walls, floors, drains and the other doors. A barrier is one part of a plan that includes drains, air bricks and a ground floor that can cope with a wet spell.

Do I need permission for a flood door guard? A temporary, removable door guard generally does not need consent, but if the building is listed, or you are fitting something permanent to the outside, check with your local authority first. Listed building work is covered in the listed building consent cost guide.

Should I buy a house in a flood zone? It depends on the zone, the history and the insurance. I would want to see the flood history, the environmental search and an inspection of the ground floor before exchange. The environmental search cost guide covers the search.
