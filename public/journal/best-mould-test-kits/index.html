The best
mould test kits,
and when they’re not enough

A mould test kit tells you what a petri dish left out for an hour, or a swab posted off to a lab, can tell you — that spores are present, roughly what family, and not much more. That’s a genuinely useful answer some of the time and beside the point the rest of the time. Here’s what these kits actually measure, where that stops being enough, and six worth buying when a kit really is the right tool.

The call is usually the same shape: a client’s found a patch of black in the corner of a bedroom ceiling, or a smell that wasn’t there last winter, and before anyone mentions damp-proof courses or extract fans they want to know one thing — is it actually mould, and how bad is it. A mould test kit answers a narrower question than that promises. It tells you whether spores are present at a level worth noting, in the room or on the surface tested, and roughly what family they belong to. It doesn’t tell you why, and it doesn’t fix anything.

Inside the box is usually a petri dish of nutrient agar or a swab, left open in a room for an hour or wiped across a surface, then either read visually after 48 hours or posted off to a lab for a proper species count. A couple of the kits below skip the wait entirely with an immunoassay-style strip that gives a result in minutes rather than days. None of them measure concentration in any meaningful sense from a single sample — mould spores are in outdoor air everywhere, all the time, so what actually matters is comparison: how a sample from inside stacks up against a sample from outside, or against what’s normal for the time of year. A kit that ships one dish and calls that a diagnosis is only answering half the question.

Where that’s genuinely useful: a musty smell with no visible source, to decide whether it’s worth opening up a void or lifting a floor before spending more; a quick check before or after remediation work; documentation for a landlord–tenant dispute that needs something on paper. Where it isn’t: a wall that’s visibly black already — nobody needs a lab to confirm what they can see — or a house with a damp problem that keeps coming back after cleaning, which needs someone finding the moisture source, not another test confirming there’s mould growing in it. Six kits below, from a £17 petri dish to an £89 paired indoor–outdoor lab kit, and a clearer line on which side of that divide you’re actually on.

How I judge a mould test kit

What it actually samples. Air (an open petri dish exposed to room air for an hour), surface (a swab across a stain or suspect patch), or bulk (a physical piece of material). Matching this to the actual question matters more than the brand on the box — an air test tells you about spore load in the room, a surface swab tells you about that specific patch, and using the wrong one wastes the kit.

Whether the lab fee is already paid. Several of these kits are cheap to buy and then ask for another $30–$40 per sample once you actually want species identification rather than a vague colour-and-fuzz description. That belongs in the price comparison up front, not as a surprise once you’ve already committed.

What the result can and can’t tell you. Presence, and a rough family — Aspergillus/Penicillium-type, Cladosporium, occasionally the rarer Stachybotrys — is the ceiling. None of these give you a number that means anything on its own without a comparison sample, and none of them tell you whether the source is a leaking gutter, a cold bridge or an extract fan that’s never actually worked.

Turnaround. 48 hours for visible growth on a settle plate, up to a week once a lab’s involved, or minutes for an immunoassay strip. Match it to whether you need a same-day steer or can wait for a proper answer.

How far one kit actually stretches. A single dish covers one room or one patch. Testing a whole house properly means buying enough kits — or a multi-room pack — to cover it, and the real cost climbs fast once you do the arithmetic.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A kit tells you if, not why. That’s the whole honest-limits point of this guide. A positive result confirms mould spores are present at a level worth noting — it says nothing about whether the cause is a leaking parapet gutter, a cold bridge at a lintel, condensation from a bathroom with no working extract, or rising damp through a failed DPC. Fixing any of those needs someone finding the moisture, not a second test confirming there’s mould growing because of it. A damp meter reading against the affected wall is usually a faster, cheaper next step than a second mould kit.

One sample without a comparison means less than it looks like. Outdoor mould spore counts move constantly with season, weather and time of day, so a single indoor air sample read in isolation is a number without context. The kits that pair an indoor sample with an outdoor control — the Accurate kit above is built around exactly this — give you something to measure against; a lone petri dish left in a bedroom does not, whatever the number that comes back looks like.

Visible mould doesn’t need a kit to confirm it. If there’s already a black, fuzzy patch on a wall or ceiling, testing it just confirms what’s obvious — the £40–£90 is better spent finding out why it’s there and treating the cause. A test kit earns its keep on the cases where there’s a smell or a symptom with no visible source: inside a cold external wall, under a suspended timber floor, behind a bath panel.

Species identification rarely changes the fix. Marketing around “detects toxic black mould” leans on genuine anxiety about Stachybotrys, but it’s a rare finding next to the ordinary Aspergillus and Penicillium species behind most household damp and condensation problems — and in either case the remedial advice is the same: remove the growth, find and fix the moisture source, improve ventilation. Species ID matters for health-exposure cases and insurance disputes; for an ordinary damp patch, it’s interesting rather than actionable.

When a kit is the wrong tool. A problem that keeps returning after cleaning, ongoing respiratory symptoms in the household, a pre-purchase concern on a property, or anything that might end up in an insurance or tenancy dispute all need more than a DIY petri dish — a proper damp and ventilation survey identifies the cause and gives you a fix, which a test kit was never built to do.

Questions I get asked

Are mould test kits accurate? Within their limits, yes — a petri dish left in a room genuinely will grow whatever’s in the air, and a lab culture genuinely identifies genus. What they’re not reliably accurate about is severity: a single sample without an outdoor comparison, taken on one day, tells you far less about how serious a problem is than the number on the report makes it look.

Do I need a test kit if I can already see mould on the wall? No. Visible growth is confirmed by definition — testing it just proves what you can already see. That money is better spent on a hygrometer to check ongoing humidity, or on finding why the wall’s cold or damp in the first place.

What does a professional survey add that a kit doesn’t? A kit answers is-it-mould. A survey answers why, checking ventilation rates, thermal bridging, damp ingress and building fabric together, then sets out what actually needs to change — the practice’s retrofit assessments and building surveys cover exactly that ground.

Can a test kit tell me if mould is affecting my health? No consumer kit correlates a spore count to health risk — that’s not what any of these are designed or accredited to do. For symptoms, a GP is the right first stop; for a proper exposure assessment, that’s a job for an environmental health professional, not a £50 petri dish.
