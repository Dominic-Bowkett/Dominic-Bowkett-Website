Spray foam
insulation
removal cost
in 2026

Removing spray foam insulation from a loft typically costs £2,000–£4,500 in 2026, more where it's the harder-bonding closed-cell type — often as much as, or more than, it cost to install. The question that actually matters more than the price is whether it needs to come out at all.

Published 21 Sep 2026 Reading time · 10 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Spray foam insulation removal typically costs £2,000–£4,500 for an ordinary 3-bed loft in 2026, more where the foam is the harder-bonding closed-cell type and has to be mechanically scraped or ground off rather than pulled away by hand. That's frequently as much as, or more than, it cost to install the stuff in the first place — original installation runs to a broadly similar £2,500–£4,000 for the same size loft. Neither figure includes what often follows: repairing any roof timber the foam was hiding decay behind, and re-insulating with something a mortgage lender will actually accept.

The number that matters more than either of those, though, is whether you need to remove it at all. A good deal of the spray foam coming back out of British lofts right now is coming out because a mortgage application stalled, not because anyone found active rot underneath it. Those are two different problems with two very different price tags, and it's worth knowing which one you've actually got before a removal contractor's van is on the drive.

I don't remove foam myself — that's a specialist contractor's job, and by every account an unpleasant one once you're grinding cured closed-cell foam off timber in a confined loft. What I do, as a member of the Residential Property Surveyors Association, is assess it: RPSA co-developed the industry protocol surveyors now use to work out whether a given installation is a genuine risk or a fixable non-issue. It's the framework I already follow if I find foam in a loft during a building survey, not an optional extra bolted on afterwards.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 21 September 2026 against Screwfix's and MyJobQuote's cost guides, cross-referenced against Checkatrade- and MyBuilder-sourced figures and several specialist removal firms' own published rates. As with every trade in this series, quotes vary a lot — by loft size, access, how thoroughly the foam has bonded to the timber, and region; expect the South East to sit toward the top of every range here.

Per square metre, the same spread comes out as roughly £20–£45/m² for open-cell and £35–£100/m² for closed-cell — a wide range, and genuinely so, because there's no rate card for this job the way there is for, say, loft insulation fitting. Closed-cell costs more because it bonds rigidly to the timber and can't simply be pulled away; it has to be ground or scraped off in a slow, dusty process that eats far more labour time per square metre than hand-stripping open-cell ever does. Disposal is worth asking about as a separate line, typically £100–£500, because cured spray foam isn't recyclable and has to go as controlled waste rather than into a standard household skip.

What drives the price

Cell type. Open-cell is softer and more forgiving to remove; closed-cell is denser, more rigid, and bonds hard enough to timber that mechanical removal is usually the only option. This is the single biggest swing factor in any quote.

Loft size and access. A steep pitch, a small hatch, or a water tank and pipework in the way all add time — the same as almost every other trade in this series, but more so here, because the contractor is working overhead in a confined, unventilated space.

What's under the foam. This is the real wildcard. The timber might come up clean, or it might need treating or partially replacing where moisture's been sitting against it unnoticed. That's a separate quote from the removal itself, and not one the removal contractor is necessarily qualified to give.

Disposal. As above — controlled waste, not a skip, and priced accordingly.

Re-insulation. Stripping the foam out leaves a loft with effectively no insulation, which most homeowners then replace with mineral wool loose-fill or quilt — see my loft insulation cost guide for what that costs on its own account, without spray foam complicating the job.

Why it's coming out in the first place

RICS published a consumer guide on spray foam insulation in 2023, and it's worth reading before anyone signs a removal contract. Among its points: spray foam can hide a roof leak behind it for longer than a conventional loft would, because the felt or rafters aren't visible for inspection; it carries a Euroclass E fire rating when left uncoated, meaning it "will only resist a small flame for a few seconds"; and its presence is "a factor that could affect a surveyor's professional opinion of value" on a mortgage valuation. RICS's own guidance doesn't distinguish between open-cell and closed-cell foam when setting out these risks — both get the same caution.

The lending side backs that up. A BBC investigation found that five of the UK's top 20 lenders — TSB, Skipton Building Society, the Co-operative Bank, Principality, and equity-release lender Aviva — won't lend at all on a property with spray foam present. Metro Bank and Yorkshire Building Society said they'd "not usually" lend where there's a significant amount of it. Lloyds, Nationwide, Barclays, NatWest Group and Santander said they'd assess applications case by case. Every lender's stated concern was the same: poorly fitted foam can trap moisture against roof timbers, hiding decay that isn't visible without removing it to check.

The scale is genuinely large. The Property Care Association and RPSA have put the number of affected UK homes at somewhere around 250,000, much of it installed under the government's now-closed Green Homes Grant scheme — not a niche problem, and worth understanding properly rather than reacting to the first quote that lands.

Do you actually need it out?

This is where I'd slow down before anyone reaches for a full removal quote. Government-commissioned research into moisture risk in spray-foamed timber roofs, published on gov.uk in March 2024, found something more useful — and more reassuring — than the blanket "closed-cell bad, open-cell fine" story that gets repeated everywhere. Open-cell foam installed to BS 5250:2021, with a suitable underlay and an air and vapour control layer behind it, came out low risk in most of the scenarios the research modelled. Risk rose where the underlay had high resistance and no vapour control layer was present. But the one scenario that came out high risk in every case tested, regardless of cell type, was foam sprayed directly onto the underside of the roof covering or tiles with no ventilated gap behind it at all.

That's a more useful question than "open-cell or closed-cell": is there a ventilated gap behind the foam, or is it stuck straight to the tiles? A proper installation, to the relevant standard, with the right layers behind it, is not automatically the problem a stalled mortgage application makes it look like.

That's exactly what the PCA/RPSA Sprayed Foam Insulation Inspection Protocol, published March 2023 and developed jointly with input from lenders and policymakers, exists to establish. It gives a surveyor a standard, evidence-based basis for telling a lender "assessed, low risk" rather than the loft being blanket-flagged on sight — and it's generally what gets a stalled application moving again, not necessarily ripping the foam out. Get it assessed before assuming wholesale removal is the only route; it might turn out to be a "get a report" problem, not a five-figure remediation.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Bundled pricing that hides the real scope. Removal, timber repair and re-insulation are three different jobs. A single lump figure that doesn't separate them makes it hard to see where the money's actually going, or to get comparable quotes.
  • The contractor's own respiratory protection. Cutting or grinding cured polyurethane foam releases isocyanates. A standard FFP3 dust mask isn't rated for that under COSHH — it needs combined organic-vapour and particulate filtration, or better. Worth asking what they're actually wearing before they start.
  • High-pressure sales tactics. Several UK councils' trading standards teams — Devon, Plymouth, Herefordshire, Bromley, North East Lincolnshire and Torbay among them — have issued specific warnings about cold-calling spray-foam-removal firms targeting older or vulnerable homeowners with inflated urgency. A quote that needs signing the same day is a reason to get a second opinion, not a reason to sign.
  • Whether an assessment's been offered at all. If a firm goes straight to a removal quote without mentioning the PCA/RPSA protocol or an independent assessment first, that's worth questioning.
  • BBA or Kiwa documentation from the original install. Worth digging out and keeping regardless of what you decide to do — it's exactly what RICS recommends holding onto, and what a lender or future surveyor may ask for.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to remove spray foam insulation? In 2026, removal costs £1,000–£2,500 for a small property with open-cell foam, up to around £4,500 or more for a 3-bed loft with closed-cell foam needing mechanical removal. Specialist waste disposal typically adds £100–£500. Timber repair and re-insulation afterwards are separate costs again, taking a full remediation to £3,500–£8,000-plus.

Will my mortgage lender refuse a house with spray foam insulation? It depends on the lender, and increasingly on whether the installation's been professionally assessed. A BBC investigation found five major lenders, including TSB, Skipton and the Co-operative Bank, won't lend at all where it's present; several others, including Lloyds, Nationwide, Barclays and Santander, assess it case by case. A report to the PCA/RPSA protocol is often what unsticks a stalled application, and it's worth commissioning before assuming the loft has to be stripped.

Is spray foam insulation always a problem? No. Research published on gov.uk in March 2024 found open-cell foam installed to BS 5250:2021, with a proper underlay and vapour control layer, carries low moisture risk in most cases tested. The consistently high-risk case, in every scenario, was foam applied directly onto the roof covering or tiles with no ventilated gap behind it — regardless of whether it was open- or closed-cell. That's closer to the real question than cell type on its own.

Can I get a grant to remove spray foam insulation? No. ECO4 and the now-closed Great British Insulation Scheme fund installing insulation, not taking it back out, and there's no dedicated government support for removal. If the foam was fitted badly under an ECO- or GBIS-funded scheme, the installer may be obliged to put it right themselves; otherwise an insurance-backed guarantee or a TrustMark complaint is the usual route, not a grant.
