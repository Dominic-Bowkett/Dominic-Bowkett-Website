The best
hi-vis vests
for site work

Most of a survey happens somewhere visibility makes no difference at all — a loft, a bathroom, a back bedroom. But a fair share doesn’t: a part-renovated house with a skip on the drive and a plasterer’s van blocking the pavement, a snagging inspection on a site still being built around, roof access past scaffolding left up from the render job, or parking on a blind bend to reach the front door. None of that is roadside surveying, but all of it is a reason to be seen rather than not. Here is what is worth keeping in the van for that, from a £4 velcro spare to a padded winter bodywarmer, and what the certification labels actually mean.

A hi-vis vest is not glamorous kit, and most of the time it sits folded in the door pocket doing nothing. But the times it earns its keep are specific and recurring: turning up to a site where other trades are already working, walking a driveway shared with skip lorries and delivery vans, or standing at the roadside checking a boundary line while traffic passes closer than it should. On an active building site it is also simply what is expected — other contractors notice who is and isn’t wearing it, and it says something about how seriously the visit is being taken before a word is exchanged.

What separates a vest worth keeping from one that ends up left in a drawer is less about brightness and more about whether it actually gets worn. A vest that has to be wrestled on over a coat, or that has no pocket for a phone and ends up abandoned mid-visit, teaches a habit of skipping it. The picks below cover the two genuinely different shapes this kit comes in — a full waistcoat and an open-sided band-and-brace — plus a padded bodywarmer for anyone doing enough winter external inspections to justify one.

How I judge a hi-vis vest

Certification that actually applies, not a marketing badge. EN ISO 20471 is the standard that matters in the UK, and the class number on the label (1, 2 or 3) tells you how much fluorescent and reflective area is fitted. A vest just described as “hi-vis style” with no class marking is a fashion item, not PPE, however bright the fabric looks.

On and off over a coat, not just over a t-shirt. Half the point of a survey vest is wearing it over whatever else is already on — a fleece in autumn, a proper coat in January. A vest that only fits comfortably over a shirt gets left off the moment the weather turns.

Pockets that earn their place. A phone pocket and an ID window are genuinely useful. Beyond that, a vest starts competing with a tool belt or tool bag for the same job, and extra pockets just add bulk without adding anything carried in them.

Fastening that survives a full day, not just the first try-on. Velcro that has picked up fluff and dust from a few loft visits stops gripping properly; a zip has fewer ways to fail and doesn’t degrade the same way.

Packs down small enough to actually live in the van. A vest that has to be folded carefully to fit anywhere gets left on the passenger seat, then left at home. Thin, foldable polyester beats a stiff, bulky weave for anything meant to be grabbed on the way to the door.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Class 2 covers almost every residential survey. EN ISO 20471 Class 2 is the standard waistcoat spec and is enough for site visits, driveways and general external inspection work. Class 3 — more coverage, usually sleeves as well as a body — is a highways and lane-closure spec, built for people working within touching distance of moving traffic. Buying Class 3 for ordinary survey work is paying for coverage that will never be needed.

“Hi-vis style” is not the same as certified PPE. A fluorescent vest with no EN ISO 20471 class marking on the label is a fashion item, however bright it looks in a listing photo. For anywhere a site’s own safety rules require PPE, that distinction is the one that actually gets checked at the gate.

Pocket count is a competition with kit already carried elsewhere. A phone pocket and an ID window pull their weight. Beyond that, a heavily pocketed vest is trying to do the job of a tool belt or the pockets already built into a decent pair of work trousers, and mostly just adds bulk.

Band-and-brace suits layering; a waistcoat suits looking the part. Open sides go over anything without a fit fight, which matters more in December than July. A fitted waistcoat looks tidier and more deliberately “on a job” for a client-facing daytime visit, which is worth something even though it has nothing to do with visibility itself.

A padded bodywarmer solves a cold-weather problem, not a visibility one. The PW374 above is genuinely useful for someone doing regular external work through a UK winter, but it costs several times what a waistcoat does to solve a problem — warmth — that a coat worn underneath a cheap waistcoat already solves for most people.

Questions I get asked

Do I need Class 2 or Class 3 hi-vis for a residential survey?

Class 2 is enough for the overwhelming majority of survey work — general site visits, driveways, gardens and external inspections away from moving traffic. Class 3 is aimed at people working within touching distance of live traffic lanes, which is not where most residential surveying happens. Unless a specific site’s risk assessment asks for Class 3, a Class 2 waistcoat is the right spec.

Can hi-vis go over my own coat, or do I need a dedicated hi-vis jacket?

A waistcoat or vest over an ordinary coat is entirely normal and is what most of the picks in this guide are for. A dedicated hi-vis jacket only becomes worth buying separately if the outer layer needs to be waterproof and visible at the same time — for most survey work, a vest over whatever coat is already being worn does the job.

Is a railway-standard vest like the Scan RIS-3279-TOM overkill for ordinary site work?

For most jobs, yes. RIS-3279-TOM is the rail industry’s own visibility standard and exceeds what a typical building site or residential survey requires. It is worth having only if the work regularly brings someone near a live rail boundary or a site that specifically asks for it — otherwise a standard EN ISO 20471 Class 2 waistcoat is the more sensible buy.

How often should a hi-vis vest actually be replaced?

Reflective tape loses its retroreflective performance well before the fabric itself looks worn out — cracking, dulling or peeling tape is the sign to replace it, typically after a year or so of regular use, sooner if it has been through a washing machine repeatedly. A vest kept as an occasional spare in the glovebox will last considerably longer simply from being used less.

A hi-vis vest is one of the smaller decisions in a survey kit list, but it is also one of the few things that gets noticed by everyone else on site before a word is spoken. Getting the visibility right on a building survey or drone roof inspection matters more than which vest is worn to do it.
