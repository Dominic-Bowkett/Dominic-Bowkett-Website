The best
thermal curtains
and blinds

A fair few of the condensation call-outs I do trace back to the same evening routine: the curtains get drawn across a cold single-glazed window the moment it gets dark, and stay shut, sealed, all night. That's usually presented to me as a mystery — the room "just gets damp" — when the curtain is doing exactly what it was bought to do, and doing something else at the same time nobody warned them about. Here's what thermal curtains, honeycomb blinds and blackout lining actually do to a cold window, the trade-off that comes with them, and six products worth buying.

A single pane of glass loses heat far faster than the wall around it, which is exactly why a heavy curtain or a cellular blind in front of it feels like it's doing something — it is. Trapping a layer of still air between the room and a cold pane cuts the draught you feel sat near the window and slows how fast that heat escapes overnight. On genuine single glazing, or an old, poorly sealed double-glazed unit, that's a real, measurable difference for the cost of a curtain rather than a glazier.

What it doesn't do is make the glass itself any warmer. The pane behind a closed thermal curtain sits at roughly outside temperature all night, while the room side of that same curtain traps warm, moist air from breathing, cooking and drying washing against it. That warm air meets cold glass and gives up its moisture right there, on the sill and the inside face of the window, often for hours before anyone opens the curtains to find it. It's the same physics as a fogged-up car window, just slower and less obvious, and it's the single biggest thing missing from most "keep your house warmer" articles that push thermal curtains without qualification.

None of that means skip the curtains. It means treat them as one part of a room's moisture balance, not a free win, and know when the honest answer is a better window rather than a heavier curtain. How I judge thermal curtains and blinds

Layers and weight, not just the word "thermal" on the label. A triple-weave curtain or a genuine multi-pass lining traps air between its layers, which is where the insulating effect actually comes from. A single-layer fabric with a light coating printed on to make it opaque can block light just as well while doing far less for heat — blackout and thermal are marketed as the same thing and often aren't.

Fit against the frame. Gaps at the top, sides and floor let cold air circulate behind the curtain regardless of how good the fabric is — a curtain track that runs wall-to-wall and a drop that reaches the sill or floor does more for the insulating effect than upgrading the fabric alone. The same logic applies to a cellular blind: an inside-mounted recess fit with tight side channels traps air properly; a loose surface-mounted one barely does.

For honeycomb blinds — single cell against double cell. A double-cell (two honeycomb layers stacked with a gap between them) traps two pockets of still air instead of one, which is a genuine step up in insulating value over a single-cell blind of the same fabric — at extra bulk and cost. Worth it on a large or particularly cold window, unnecessary on a small one.

Cordless and child-safe mechanisms on blinds. Loose or looped cords are a strangulation risk around young children and pets, which is why cordless push-pull and spring-lift mechanisms have largely replaced them on new blinds — check this explicitly rather than assuming, especially on older stock still being sold through.

Whether it needs sewing, or genuinely doesn't. Thermal lining fabric sold by the metre is the cheapest way to add real insulating value to curtains you already own, but most of it needs hemming or hand-sewing onto the back of the existing curtain — iron-on and clip-on options exist but are fiddlier to fit well than the packaging suggests.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The condensation trade-off is real, and it's worth managing rather than ignoring. A closed thermal curtain or a tightly fitted cellular blind holds warm, moist room air against a cold pane all night, and that moisture has to go somewhere — onto the glass, the sill, and eventually into the curtain hem or the window reveal if it's not dealt with. On a genuinely cold, single-glazed window in a bedroom, leaving a small gap at the top of the curtain, cracking a trickle vent, or simply wiping the glass down in the morning costs nothing and heads off the mould that otherwise builds up on the sill and the curtain itself over a winter.

Thermal curtains don't move an EPC rating. RdSAP, the methodology behind a domestic EPC, doesn't assess soft furnishings — a curtain, however good, isn't a recognised measure the way loft or wall insulation is. Worth knowing before assuming better curtains will show up as an improvement on a certificate; the benefit here is comfort and a heating bill, not a rating.

"Blackout" and "thermal" aren't automatically the same thing. A single-layer fabric with a light-blocking coating printed on the back can hit 99 percent blackout while doing far less for heat retention than a genuine multi-layer weave of the same thickness. Where thermal performance is the actual goal, check for multiple woven layers or a separate lining, not just the blackout percentage on the listing.

Fit beats fabric. A wall-to-wall track, a floor-length drop and a pelmet or box header do more for the insulating effect than upgrading from a cheap to an expensive thermal curtain of the same size — gaps at the top, sides and floor let cold air simply flow around the barrier regardless of how many layers are in it.

None of this is a substitute for a window that's actually failing. If a "double-glazed" window mists up between the panes rather than on the room-side face, the seal's gone and no curtain fixes that — that's a glazing unit replacement, not a soft-furnishing upgrade. Questions I get asked

Will thermal curtains stop my windows misting up in the morning? No — if anything, a tightly closed thermal curtain over a cold single-glazed window can make morning condensation worse, because it traps humid room air against the glass overnight with nowhere to go. Leaving a small gap, or airing the room and wiping the glass in the morning, matters more than the curtain's thermal rating for this specific problem.

Do thermal curtains actually reduce heating bills? On genuine single glazing or an old, draughty double-glazed unit, yes, in a small but real way — less heat escaping through the window overnight is less heat the boiler has to replace. On modern, well-sealed double glazing the effect is much more marginal; the window's already doing most of the work.

Are honeycomb blinds better than curtains for condensation? Not automatically. A cellular blind sits closer to the glass with less bulk than a floor-length curtain, but if it's fitted with a tight seal on all sides it traps room air against the pane in much the same way. Either works better paired with basic ventilation — a trickle vent, an extractor, or just opening the room up in the morning — than left to do the job alone.

Can I add thermal lining to curtains I already own? Yes — blackout thermal lining sold by the metre is the cheapest option here, though most of it needs sewing or hemming to the back of the existing curtain rather than clipping on. It's worth doing on a curtain that's otherwise in good condition rather than buying a whole new pair.
