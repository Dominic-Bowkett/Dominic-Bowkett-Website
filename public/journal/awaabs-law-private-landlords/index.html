Awaab’s Law
is coming to
private rentals

The rules that force social landlords to investigate and fix damp, mould and other hazards to fixed deadlines are being extended to private renting. Nothing changes overnight, but the direction is clear — and the work that keeps you compliant is the same work that stops a home growing mould in the first place.

Published 3 November 2026 Reading time · 8 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

What Awaab’s Law is

The law is named after Awaab Ishak, a two-year-old who died in December 2020 after prolonged exposure to mould in his family’s social-rented home. The mould had been reported and put down to how the family lived. It wasn’t fixed.

Since 27 October 2025, social landlords in England have had to meet fixed deadlines once they become aware of a potential hazard, under the Hazards in Social Housing (Prescribed Requirements) (England) Regulations 2025:

StepSocial housing deadline
Investigate a potential hazard10 working days
Written summary to the tenant3 working days after the investigation
Make a significant hazard safe5 working days after the investigation
Deal with an emergency hazardWithin 24 hours

The first phase covers damp and mould plus all emergency hazards. A second phase, due from 30 November 2026 subject to Parliament, adds excess cold and heat, falls, electrical hazards, fire and explosions, structural collapse and domestic hygiene.

What’s proposed for private landlords

The Renters’ Rights Act 2025 (sections 60 and 61) gives the government the power to apply Awaab’s Law to private tenancies and to some licences, such as supported and temporary accommodation. On 9 October 2026 MHCLG opened a consultation on how it should work. It closes at 11.59pm on 18 December 2026.

Everything in this section is a proposal, not settled law:

  • Timescales. The consultation sets out “a case for” the same deadlines as social housing, but asks for views on shorter and longer options — for example 5, 10 or 15 working days to investigate, and 12, 24 or 48 hours for emergencies.
  • Hazards. Eventually every hazard in the Housing Health and Safety Rating System except overcrowding, starting with damp and mould and emergency hazards.
  • Start date. The earliest feasible date is given as 2027. The government’s own impact assessment assumes a phased roll-out in 2028, 2029 and 2030.
  • Tenant-caused damage. Hazards caused by a tenant’s breach of contract would be excluded — with guidance that this does not let landlords dismiss damp and mould as “lifestyle”.
  • Leasehold flats. Around 40% of privately rented homes are leasehold. The consultation asks whether hazards that are the freeholder’s responsibility should be excluded, or whether the landlord should have to take reasonable steps to get the freeholder to act.
  • Alternative accommodation. Social landlords must rehouse tenants if a home can’t be made safe in time. The consultation openly questions whether that is feasible for private landlords.

How it would be enforced

Awaab’s Law would become a term implied into every private tenancy. If a landlord doesn’t meet it, the tenant can take them to court for breach of contract, and the court can order repairs, compensation and some or all of the tenant’s legal costs. Councils keep their existing powers over hazards but won’t police the Awaab’s Law deadlines themselves, and the new private rented sector ombudsman (expected from 2028) will be able to order action and compensation.

Two points worth holding on to. First, the government doesn’t expect Awaab’s Law to create new repair duties — it puts a clock on the ones landlords already have. Second, a landlord who has taken all reasonable steps but been held up by things genuinely outside their control has a defence. Which is why evidence matters (more on that below).

What damp actually looks like

Most of the argument about damp and mould comes from naming the wrong problem. There are three main kinds, and they need very different fixes.

Condensation is by far the most common. Cooking, showers, drying washing and breathing put several litres of water into the air every day; when that warm, moist air meets a cold surface it turns back into water. Look for black spotty mould in the corners of external walls, behind wardrobes, around window reveals, and on top-floor ceilings where the loft insulation is thin or missing.

Penetrating damp is water getting in from outside — a blocked gutter, a leaking downpipe, slipped tiles, failed pointing, cracked render. It usually shows as a patch that grows after rain, often with a tide mark, in one place rather than throughout.

Rising damp is moisture drawn up from the ground. It’s real, but it is diagnosed far more often than it occurs. Before paying for injections, check whether outside ground levels, paths or render have simply bridged the damp-proof course.

Why “open a window” isn’t the answer

Condensation is controlled by three things working together: getting moist air out, keeping surfaces warm, and heating the tenant can afford to run. Take any one away and a careful household will still get mould.

Ventilation is where I find the biggest gap between what’s fitted and what works. On a recent survey, three bathroom extract fans measured 2.5, 6.6 and 9.2 litres per second against the 15 l/s minimum in Approved Document F. Every one of them was running, and every one of them failed. Door undercuts were 6–8 mm against the roughly 10 mm needed for air to reach the fans with the doors shut, and few windows had trickle vents. The owners were about to fit new windows — which would have removed the accidental ventilation the house relied on and made the mould worse.

The basics to check:

  • Kitchen and bathroom extract fans that actually extract (measured, not just switched on)
  • Trickle vents present, open and not hidden behind blinds
  • A gap under internal doors so air can move
  • Heating that works and is affordable to run
  • Loft and wall insulation, especially at the cold corners

Cold matters in its own right too. Excess cold is one of the rating-system hazards, it is in the next phase for social housing, and cold homes are linked to heart and lung conditions, particularly for older and very young occupants.

Five things to put in place now

  1. Log every report the day it arrives, with the date and time. Under a deadline-based regime, the clock starts when you become aware.
  2. Investigate the cause, not just the mould. Wiping it off a wall resets nothing.
  3. Photograph and measure, with moisture and humidity readings before and after the work.
  4. Record what you did, when, and why anything was delayed — access problems, contractor availability. That record is what a reasonable-steps defence rests on.
  5. Fix the building, not the surface: ventilation, heating, insulation and leaks, not just a coat of mould-resistant paint.
