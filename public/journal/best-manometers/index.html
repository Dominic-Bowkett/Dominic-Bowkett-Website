“Manometer” is a wider word than it sounds on a survey report. Two quite different site jobs both come down to reading a differential pressure accurately and writing it down: a Gas Safe engineer’s gas tightness test on an installation’s pipework, and a retrofit assessor’s ventilation commissioning check on an extract or MVHR system. Both use an instrument that does the same basic thing — measures the difference between two pressures — over completely different ranges, for completely different reasons.

I’m a building surveyor, not a gas engineer, and the tightness test itself isn’t something I run. What I see on a survey is what sits either side of it — a gas safety certificate with a date that’s quietly slipped past twelve months, or an installation that’s clearly had work done to it since the last inspection. The test that closes that loop, run under IGEM/UP/1, starts with a let-by check at 7–10 mbar and moves to a tightness test proper at 20–21 mbar for natural gas or 37 mbar for LPG, held and timed against a permissible pressure drop. The manometer is the instrument that produces the number a Gas Safe engineer signs against.

The other job is closer to my own work. When I measure ventilation on a retrofit assessment — checking a System 1 to System 4 setup is actually moving the air it’s supposed to — most terminals give a clean reading from a vane anemometer held against the grille. Not every terminal cooperates: an awkward duct run, a terminal too small for the vane, or a system being commissioned against Approved Document F’s flow targets, mandatory since June 2022, calls for a pressure-based method instead — a flow ring or orifice plate matched against a manometer reading, or a proper airflow-and-pressure instrument that does both. That’s the gap this guide covers.

How I judge a manometer

Range matched to the job, not a single headline spec. A gas tightness kit needs to comfortably span 7–10 mbar for the let-by check and 20–21 mbar (natural gas) or 37 mbar (LPG) for the tightness test that follows; a ventilation commissioning instrument is better judged on its Pa range and whether it reads airflow or velocity alongside pressure, not on a mbar figure built for gas work.

Resolution at the pressure the test actually runs at. A headline “±1% of full scale” figure is close to meaningless without checking what that works out to at 20 mbar specifically — the units here that step down to 0.01 mbar below 20 mbar are reading to a precision the tightness test needs, not just a precision that looks good on the box.

Min/Max or data hold, to catch a transient drop. A pressure drop during a tightness test can happen between glances at the display. A unit that records the minimum and maximum reading over the test period is doing the watching so the engineer doesn’t have to stare at a needle for the full duration.

Digital convenience against a manual gauge that can’t fail electronically. A U-tube manometer has no battery to go flat and no sensor to drift out of calibration between services. It’s still explicitly permitted under IGEM/UP/1, and worth keeping as a backup even where a digital instrument is the daily tool.

A stored or logged reading, for the paperwork. Since Approved Document F made ventilation commissioning mandatory in June 2022, a commissioning notice has to reach Building Control within five days of the work. A unit that stores or exports readings makes that record easier to produce than a phone photo of a display.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Airflow and cfm capability is wasted on a pure gas tightness kit. The Fluke 922’s velocity and calculated-flow readings earn their keep on MVHR and duct commissioning, where the question is how much air is moving, not just what the pressure is — on a routine domestic tightness test, that capability goes entirely unused.

A high pressure ceiling matters for airflow work, not for a domestic gas test. Every gas tightness test on this page runs well under 40 mbar; a unit rated to several hundred mbar or more is answering a question a domestic gas installation never asks.

Bluetooth and app logging is convenience, not compliance. A dated, saved reading from an app is a tidy record, but it isn’t the only acceptable one — a written figure and a photo of the display have done the job for years. It’s worth paying for if the paperwork process genuinely benefits from it, not as a default upgrade.

A U-gauge isn’t obsolete kit. It’s slower to read and can’t log a result, but it remains a legitimate instrument under IGEM/UP/1 and one that never needs a battery changing mid-job — worth keeping in the van even where a digital instrument is the daily tool.

Electrical CAT ratings don’t apply here. That’s the relevant safety marking on a clamp meter or multimeter working near live wiring; a manometer’s relevant reference points are IGEM/UP/1 for gas work and Approved Document F for ventilation commissioning, and neither has anything to do with electrical category ratings.

Questions I get asked

What’s the difference between a manometer and an anemometer?

A manometer measures pressure — the difference between two points, in mbar, Pa or inches of water column. An anemometer measures air velocity directly, usually with a spinning vane held in the airstream, and converts that to a flow rate. Both get used on a ventilation commissioning visit; a vane anemometer reads most terminals directly, while a manometer earns its place where the terminal geometry makes a direct vane reading unreliable, paired with a flow ring or orifice plate.

Does a Gas Safe engineer have to use a specific type of manometer for a tightness test?

IGEM/UP/1 doesn’t mandate a digital instrument — a calibrated U-gauge is still permitted alongside an electronic one, provided it reads accurately enough for the pressures involved and is checked or calibrated regularly. What matters is the reading’s accuracy and the engineer’s competence in running the test, not the specific model of gauge used.

Can the same manometer be used for both gas tightness testing and ventilation commissioning?

For the pressure reading itself, yes — the physics is identical, just at different scales. Where it gets awkward is range and resolution: an instrument set up for 0–40 mbar gas work reads a domestic ventilation pressure of a few hundred Pa (roughly 0–4 mbar) at the coarse end of its scale, which is why a dedicated commissioning instrument like the Fluke 922 pairs pressure with a direct velocity and flow reading instead of relying on pressure alone.

How often does a digital manometer need calibrating?

Commonly annually against a traceable reference, in line with how most gas test instruments are serviced — check the specific manufacturer’s guidance for the unit bought, since intervals vary. A manometer that’s been dropped, stored somewhere damp, or is giving a reading that looks obviously wrong is worth checking before it’s trusted again, regardless of when the last calibration fell due.
