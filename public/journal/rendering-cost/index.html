Rendering
cost
in 2026

Rendering a house typically costs £40–£100 per m² in 2026, or £5,000–£11,000 for a 3-bed semi before scaffolding. The right render for the wall matters more than the cheapest quote.

Published 3 Oct 2026 Reading time · 7 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Rendering a house in 2026 typically costs £40–£100 per m² supplied and fitted, which for a 3-bed semi means about £5,000–£11,000 for the render itself, plus £800–£2,500 for scaffolding. Sand-and-cement sits at the bottom of that range, monocouche and silicone in the middle and top, and lime straddles the lot. The spread is wide because the render, the wall behind it and the access all differ, and the cheapest quote is rarely the cheapest job.

The number I would worry about is not on the quote. Render has to suit the wall it is going on. A solid-walled house built before about 1919 needs to dry out through its walls, and the wrong render stops that. I diagnose buildings and don’t apply render, so I can say when the cheap option is the expensive one.

This is the 2026 price by system, what moves it, and what I would want settled before work starts.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 3 October 2026 against several UK cost guides: MyJobQuote (updated 1 October 2026), BestBuilders (7 September 2026), Trade2Base and Self Build. The sources agree on the order of the systems and differ on the numbers. MyJobQuote’s per-m² figures were much lower than the rest (£15–£20 for cement, £25–£30 for lime), so I have treated them as an outlier and used the overlap of the others. Kent, Sussex, Surrey and London usually run above the national average; I could not find a reliable uplift figure, so I have not invented one.

For a typical 3-bed semi (roughly 90–120 m² of wall), the guides put sand-and-cement at about £5,000–£8,500, monocouche at £6,500–£10,000 and silicone at £7,000–£11,000. One guide put a lime-rendered semi at £6,000–£12,000. Scaffolding is on top.

The breathability trap

Old solid walls have no cavity. Rain gets in and the wall dries back out, through lime mortar and, if there is one, a lime render. Conservation guidance repeats the same point: cement render is relatively impermeable, traps moisture, and once it cracks, water gets in behind it and cannot get out. The result is a cold, damp wall, often with spalling brick behind.

That is the pattern to watch for when someone has sand-and-cement rendered a Victorian house because it was the cheapest quote. It looks fine for a few years. Then the damp shows inside and gets blamed on a failed DPC. If that sounds familiar, rising damp treatment cost covers why much of it is something else, and damp survey cost covers getting the diagnosis first.

The fix is lime, which costs more. It is slower, goes on in several coats and needs curing time between them, so the scaffold stays up longer. That is where the premium comes from, and for the right wall it is cheaper than redoing the job. The same logic applies to repointing, which I have covered in repointing cost.

What drives the price

The system. See the table. Silicone and monocouche cost more per m² than sand-and-cement, but are through-coloured or thin-coat finishes that don’t depend on regular repainting in the same way.

Scaffolding. Roughly £800–£2,500 for a two-storey house, depending on whom you ask. It can be a fifth or more of the total. If the scaffold is also needed for a roof or window job, do them together; scaffolding cost covers how it is priced.

Removing what is there. Hacking off old render runs about £8–£25 per m² plus disposal, so £1,000–£3,000 for a typical house. One guide notes silicone and monocouche systems can take longer to remove than old sand-and-cement. A worked lime example from one guide on a 110 m² semi: roughly £1,320 removal, £7,150 lime, £1,500 scaffold and £500 repairs.

The wall behind. Preparation and substrate repair added £500–£2,500 in one guide. Once old render is off you find the brickwork. If it has been damaged, you pay to fix it before anything goes on.

Weather and curing. Lime in particular cannot be rushed, or applied in frost. Anyone promising a lime house in three days is not doing three coats.

Planning, listing and the building regulations

Planning Portal says that, outside protected areas, rendering is generally permitted development if the new finish is of a similar appearance to the existing walls, and replacing existing render with similar render is also fine. In a Conservation Area, National Park, Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty or the Broads you need to apply for planning permission first. Much of the High Weald around my own patch is AONB, so this comes up more than you might think. A listed building needs listed building consent, and the wrong render is exactly the sort of change that gets enforcement action.

The other catch is the building regulations. Planning Portal says that where 25% or more of an external wall is being renovated, meaning a new layer or the replacement of an existing layer, building regulations would normally apply and the wall’s thermal insulation would have to meet the Approved Document standard. Where that is not technically or functionally feasible, it should be improved as far as can be achieved within a simple payback of 15 years. So re-rendering a whole elevation can trigger an insulation requirement. That is the bridge to external wall insulation cost, where the render is a finish over insulation boards at about £100–£200 per m². Check with building control before you price.

What I’d watch for in a quote

  • The system named. “Render” is not a specification. It should say sand-and-cement, lime, monocouche or silicone, and the coat build-up.
  • A reason it suits your wall. If the house is solid brick or stone, ask why the render is breathable.
  • What happens to the old render. Removed, patched or overcoated, and who pays for what is found underneath.
  • Scaffolding, beads and flashings itemised, so you can compare like with like.
  • Whether the building regulations 25% point has been considered, and who deals with building control.
  • Weathering details. Drip beads, a plinth, sealed window reveals and roof overhangs. Most failed render fails at the details, not the wall.
  • A written guarantee and what it excludes. Read it.

Questions I get asked

How much does it cost to render a house? In 2026, roughly £40–£100 per m² depending on the system. For a typical 3-bed semi that is about £5,000–£11,000 for the render, plus £800–£2,500 for scaffolding. Lime on a period house runs about £6,000–£12,000 for a semi. Prices run higher in the South East.

How much does lime render cost per m²? Typically £45–£95 per m² for a three-coat system supplied and applied, in 2026. It costs more than cement because it goes on in several coats with curing time between, which also keeps the scaffold up for longer. One cost guide quoted far lower figures, which I have treated as an outlier.

Do I need planning permission to render my house? Often not, but check. Outside conservation areas, National Parks, Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty and the Broads, new render that is of a similar appearance to the existing walls is generally permitted development. Inside those areas you need to apply, and a listed building needs listed building consent.

Does render make a house warmer? Ordinary render adds almost nothing. Insulated render, which is insulation boards plus a render finish, does, and costs roughly £100–£200 per m² in one 2026 guide. If 25% or more of an external wall is being renovated, the building regulations normally require the thermal performance of that wall to be improved.
