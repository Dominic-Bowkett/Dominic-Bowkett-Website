A thermal imaging camera is the closest thing this trade has to X-ray vision, and also the tool most often used to reach confident wrong conclusions. On most retrofit and ventilation jobs it is the tool that earns its place: it finds the slumped loft insulation, the cold bridge behind the dot-and-dab, the radiator running at half temperature because of sludge, and the gap where the cavity batts stop two courses short of the wall plate. Ten years ago that capability cost several thousand pounds. Today a genuinely useful camera plugs into your phone for a couple of hundred.

What separates the good ones from the junk is not the brand on the front. It is native infrared resolution, thermal sensitivity, and — more than either — whether the person holding it understands what a thermal image actually shows. A thermal camera measures surface temperature. It does not see moisture, it does not see through walls, and on a mild afternoon with the heating off it sees almost nothing at all. The kit list below reflects that: cameras that produce images you can defend, at prices that make sense for the work you do.

If you are chasing damp specifically, note now that thermal is only half the diagnosis — a cold patch is a suspicion, not a verdict. You confirm it with a damp meter. More on that below.

How I judge a thermal camera

Native IR resolution first. The number that matters is the pixel count of the infrared sensor itself — 160×120 is the working minimum for survey imagery, 256×192 is comfortable. Manufacturers increasingly quote a “super resolution” figure produced by software upscaling; it makes the pictures prettier and tells you nothing new. Read the small print for the native figure before you buy anything.

Thermal sensitivity (NETD). Quoted in millikelvin: the smallest temperature difference the sensor can distinguish. Under 50mK and missing insulation shows up as a crisp shape; at 150mK the same defect is a vague smudge you will talk yourself out of. This is where cheap cameras quietly fail.

A visual camera for reports. A thermal image of a cold patch is meaningless in a report unless the reader can tell which wall they are looking at. Cameras that blend a normal photo with the thermal image — FLIR’s MSX, HIKMICRO’s fusion — earn their keep every time you write up a job.

Handling in real houses. Lofts, crawl spaces, the top of a stair with a torch in your teeth. One-handed operation, a screen you can read in a dark void, and a battery that lasts a working morning matter more than any laboratory spec.

What happens to the images afterwards. Radiometric files (where every pixel carries a temperature you can re-analyse later), sensible export, and reporting software that does not fight you. If the camera locks your data into a clumsy app, that is a running cost the price tag hides.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Conditions beat kit, every time. A thermal camera shows temperature differences, so there have to be some. For building work you want a decent delta-T — roughly 10°C between inside and out, held for a few hours. In practice that means a cold morning with the heating on since before dawn, curtains open, sun not yet on the elevation you care about. The same £1,200 camera that maps every stud and cold bridge at 7am in January will show you a flat grey nothing on a mild May afternoon.

Emissivity will lie to you. Every surface radiates heat with a different efficiency, and the camera assumes one value for the whole scene. Shiny surfaces are the trap: bare metal, gloss paint, foil-faced insulation and glass all reflect the thermal scene around them, so a foil-backed board can read as freezing while doing its job perfectly, and the classic embarrassment is photographing a “cold spot” that turns out to be the reflection of the sky. Matt building surfaces — plaster, brick, masonry paint — behave well; anything that would show your face in it does not.

Thermal shows temperature, not moisture. Damp patches often show cold because evaporation cools the surface — but so do cold bridges, voids, draughts and yesterday’s condensation. The camera raises the question; a moisture meter answers it. On damp jobs I use thermal to map the extent and pattern of a cold anomaly, then confirm with a damp meter and, where it matters, a carbide test. Anyone diagnosing rising damp from a thermal image alone is guessing in colour.

What the salesmen push that you can ignore: “super resolution” upscaling figures (marketing megapixels — only the native sensor counts), temperature ranges up to 550°C (houses live between about −10 and 60°C), the number of colour palettes (you will use two), and megapixel counts on the visual camera dressed up to look like thermal specification. One salesman-favourite that genuinely matters and rarely gets mentioned: whether the files are radiometric, so you can adjust level, span and emissivity back at the desk instead of living with whatever the screen showed on site.

Questions I get asked

Can a thermal camera see damp? Not directly — no thermal camera measures moisture. What it sees is the temperature signature damp often creates: evaporative cooling on a wet wall, or the thermal mass of saturated masonry lagging behind the room. That makes it an excellent screening tool for finding where to investigate, especially over large areas, but a cold patch has several innocent explanations. Confirm with a moisture meter before anyone quotes for tanking.

What resolution thermal camera do I need? For building survey work, treat 160×120 native infrared pixels as the floor and 256×192 as the sensible target — at typical room distances that is the difference between seeing that a wall is cold and seeing the shape of why. Ignore any resolution figure qualified by “super”, “enhanced” or “up to”; that is software interpolation. For casual homeowner use, 80×60 will find gross defects, but it will not support conclusions you would put your name to.

Are phone thermal cameras any good? The current 256×192 plug-ins — TOPDON TC001, InfiRay P2 Pro — are genuinely good, with sensors that embarrass dedicated cameras from five years ago. The compromises are practical rather than optical: they occupy your phone, the apps are workmanlike, and a dongle sticking out of a USB port is clumsy in a loft. For a homeowner, or a surveyor’s backup, they are the value of the decade. For daily professional use, a dedicated unit with its own screen and reporting chain still earns its price.

When is the best time to do a thermal survey? Early morning, in the heating season, with the house warmed overnight and before sun hits the walls — you want a steady 10°C or so between inside and out and no solar gain confusing the picture. Overcast beats bright, still beats windy (wind strips the tell-tale warm patches off an elevation), and dry beats wet. October to April is the realistic window in the South East; a thermal survey booked for a sunny June afternoon is a photography session, not a diagnosis.

Thermal imaging is a routine part of how the practice approaches PAS 2035 retrofit assessments and the heat-loss questions that sit behind EPC ratings — the camera finds the defects, the paperwork tells you what they cost.
