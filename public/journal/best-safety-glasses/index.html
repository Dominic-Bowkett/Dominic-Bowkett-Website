Half the debris that ends up near a surveyor’s eyes isn’t dramatic — it’s masonry dust from a cavity wall inspection hole, grit off an old lath-and-plaster ceiling, or fibreglass shaken loose pulling yourself through a loft hatch. A good chunk of a working survey involves putting a hole in something. A 10–12mm cavity wall inspection hole with an SDS drill, a bolster and hammer to lift a section of skirting, a loft hatch that’s been painted shut and needs persuading with a scraper — all of it throws small debris towards your face at exactly the moment you’re leaning in close to see what you’ve just exposed. None of it is dramatic enough to think about twice, which is precisely why it’s worth thinking about once, properly, rather than reaching for whatever pair is rattling around in the door pocket.

Most survey work doesn’t call for a sealed goggle — that’s a different job, for genuinely dusty strip-out or chemical exposure. An open-frame or wraparound pair of safety spectacles covers the actual risk on a typical survey: a chip of mortar off a drill bit, dust dislodged pulling back an old loft hatch above your head, grit falling as you probe a suspect area of ceiling. Worth having on any time you’re drilling, using a bolster, or working directly under something that could drop material on you — which on an older building is more often than the job description suggests.

What separates a pair worth keeping on your face from one that migrates to the glovebox and stays there is not much: a genuine dual coating rather than a single spray doing two jobs badly, a proper EN166 rating with the marking actually visible on the lens or frame, and a frame low-profile enough to sit comfortably alongside ear defenders, a bump cap, and sometimes a dust mask, all stacked on the same head. How I judge safety glasses

Anti-fog that’s actually two coatings. A cheap pair often has a single spray-on treatment trying to handle both fogging and scratching, and it wears off within weeks of being wiped on a sleeve. A genuine dual coating — anti-fog bonded to the inside, scratch-resistant bonded to the outside — keeps working after real use. It matters most exactly when you need it: bent over into a loft hatch, breathing hard after the ladder, in a cold void on a warm day.

EN166 rating, and what the letters actually mean. EN166 is the base standard for all safety eyewear sold as PPE in the UK; optional markings on top of it cover things like F (low-energy impact), B (medium-energy impact), K (resistance to surface damage from fine particles) and N (anti-fog tested to a standard, not just claimed). A pair with no visible marking on the lens or frame isn’t legally safety eyewear, whatever the online listing calls it.

How it sits with the rest of the PPE stack. Ear defenders, a hard hat or bump cap, and a dust mask or half-mask respirator often all need to share the same head at once. A temple arm that’s perfectly comfortable worn on its own can dig in painfully the moment ear defenders clamp down over the top of it — worth trying before you buy six identical pairs.

Lens tint for the job in front of you. Clear lenses for anything indoors, in a loft or in a void, where you need every bit of the available light. A tinted or mirrored lens belongs on a roof in bright sun, not in a dim crawl space where it just makes the job harder.

Weight and comfort over a full day. A pair worn for ten minutes while drilling a single inspection hole is a different proposition to one worn continuously through a loft survey. A few grams and a soft nose pad matter more than the spec sheet suggests once they’ve been on for two hours.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A dual coating beats a marketing sticker. “Anti-fog” printed on a listing can mean a genuine bonded treatment on the inside face of the lens, or it can mean a spray applied at the factory that lasts a few wipes. The manufacturers who describe their coating in two parts — anti-fog inside, anti-scratch outside — are telling you it’s a real dual-sided treatment rather than a single compromise coating. Worth reading the actual product description rather than trusting the headline word.

EN166 marking is non-negotiable, whatever the price. Any glasses sold as PPE for work in the UK should carry visible EN166 marking on the lens or frame, sometimes with additional letters (F, B, K, T) for the specific protection level. A pair with no marking at all — common among the cheapest unbranded multipacks — isn’t legally safety eyewear no matter how the listing is titled, and shouldn’t be relied on for anything more than keeping dust out of your eyes.

Buy for the PPE stack, not the glasses alone. A frame that’s comfortable on its own can become genuinely painful once ear defenders clamp over the top of the same temple arm for a few hours. If you regularly wear ear defenders or a dust mask alongside eye protection, try the combination before committing to six identical pairs.

Clear is the right default for survey work. Tinted and mirrored lenses look the part but make a dim loft, cellar or void genuinely harder to read. Save the tint for outdoor roof work in bright sun; keep a clear pair as the one that lives in the bag for everything else.

Prescription wearers have a real option, not just a compromise. Over-the-glasses (OTG) safety spectacles, such as Portwest’s PS24 Peak OTG, are designed to fit comfortably over an existing pair of prescription glasses rather than forcing a choice between the two. Worth knowing about before assuming a full prescription safety pair is the only route.

Questions I get asked

Do I actually need safety glasses for a routine building survey? Whenever you’re drilling a cavity wall inspection hole, using a hammer and bolster to open up a small area, or working under a loft hatch or anything else that could drop debris on you. The risk on most survey visits is low-level rather than dramatic, but a chip of masonry or a fall of old plaster dust in the eye is a genuinely avoidable inconvenience for the cost of a pair of glasses.

What’s the difference between anti-fog and anti-scratch coatings, and do I need both? Anti-fog stops condensation forming on the inside of the lens when your breath, body heat or a sudden change in temperature meets a cooler surface — the exact scenario climbing into a loft after exertion. Anti-scratch protects the outer face from the daily wear of being pocketed, wiped and knocked about in a tool bag. For anything worn regularly rather than kept as an emergency spare, both are worth having.

Can I wear safety glasses over my own prescription glasses? Yes — over-the-glasses (OTG) designs are a genuine category, built with extra depth and width to sit over an existing frame rather than replace it. They’re not as sleek as a standard pair but solve the problem properly, and are worth searching for by name if a standard safety spectacle keeps slipping over your own glasses.

Are the cheap unbranded multipacks from Amazon actually safe? Some are, some aren’t — the only reliable way to tell is checking for EN166 marking on the lens or frame itself, not just a mention of “safety” in the listing title. A named brand with a stated EN166 rating, even at the cheap end like the Portwest pick above, is a safer bet than an anonymous listing with no marking mentioned at all.
