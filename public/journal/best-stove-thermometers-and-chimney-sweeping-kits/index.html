A wood-burning stove turns up in a surprising number of the houses I survey. It is usually in an old inglenook or a Victorian chimney breast, usually installed by someone other than the current owner, and almost always with no paperwork. The buyer asks me one thing: is it safe? The honest answer starts with what I can see, which is the hearth, the clearances, the flue and, in the loft, the chimney stack. It rarely starts with a certificate.

Two small purchases make a real difference to how a stove behaves over the years. A thermometer shows whether the stove is being run in the band where it burns cleanly, and a sweeping kit, or a booked sweep, keeps the flue clear. Run too cool, a stove fills the chimney with tar and soot. Run too hot, it stresses the stove and the liner. Both are cheap to avoid and expensive to fix.

This is a guide for people who already have a stove, not for deciding whether to fit one. If a chimney is being taken out rather than swept, see the chimney removal cost guide. And before any of the kit below: a stove needs a working carbon monoxide alarm in the room. The carbon monoxide detector guide covers the choice, and in England the Building Regulations require an alarm where a solid fuel appliance is installed.

How I judge stove thermometers and sweeping kits

Does it suit the flue? This is the first question for both halves of the guide. A magnetic dial works on single-wall steel pipe and tells you little on double-wall, where the outer skin stays cool by design. A brush must match the liner: round brush for a round liner, polypropylene for stainless or flexible liners, wire only for masonry flues that can take it.

Is the reading a trend or a number? A flue pipe surface reading is a guide. The bands printed on most dials are broad, commonly around 100–250°C for the working zone on the pipe surface, with hotter indicating excess and cooler indicating smouldering. I would judge a dial on how clear it is to read, not on how precise it claims to be. Check the maker’s own bands for your stove, since they vary.

Can you reach the whole flue? A hand kit works on a straight flue. Offsets, a register plate, a long chimney or a liner with a cowl all limit what rods and a brush will clear. If you cannot get to the top of the flue or see the pot, do not guess. Book a sweep.

How safe is it to use? Sweeping is dusty, sooty work in a confined room. A dust sheet, an ash vacuum and a respirator are part of the kit. And a thermometer is only useful if the stove is lit properly with dry fuel, so measure the logs too.

Does it replace a professional? No. A sweep also looks for cracks, bird nests, liner damage and draught problems that a brush cannot find, and issues a certificate that insurers and buyers sometimes ask for.

The picks in detail

01

Stovax magnetic stove thermometer

Best overall

A bimetal dial thermometer on a magnet, the sort that clings to a single-wall flue pipe about 150mm above the stove. Stovax is a long-standing UK stove name, and the dial is marked with the usual banded zones: too cool, the working band, and too hot. Read it as a guide to trend rather than a laboratory number. Manufacturer information is clear that it is designed for the surface of a steel flue pipe, and it will read quite differently on a double-wall pipe or on the stove top.

03

Non-contact infrared thermometer, -50 to 550C

Best for spot checks

An infrared thermometer is the tool for the questions the dial cannot answer: how hot is the wall behind the stove, how hot is the hearth, what is the surface of a double-wall pipe doing. It reads the surface it is pointed at, so shiny black enamel and bare steel give different answers, and it will never tell you what the flue gases are doing. Treat it as a surveyor treats one on a cold bridge: good for comparing one place with another, poor for absolute figures. More on that in the infrared thermometer guide.

For

  • Reads hot surfaces from a safe distance
  • Useful for clearances to combustible walls, hearths and ceilings
  • Has plenty of uses beyond the stove

Against

  • Emissivity errors on shiny or painted surfaces
  • Not a flue gas reading, and not a substitute for the dial

04

Flexible nylon chimney sweeping rod and brush kit, 6m

Best sweeping kit

The standard hand kit: a polypropylene or steel-wire brush head and a run of flexible screw-together rods, working up from the stove or down from the pot. Match the brush to the flue. A round brush of the right diameter suits a round liner, and a wire brush on a plastic or flexible liner is the common way to wreck one. If the chimney has a bend, a kinked offset or a stainless liner, say so before buying a kit. Rods that are too stiff or too short are the usual complaint in owner feedback.

For

  • Complete starter kit, brush and rods in one box
  • Works from inside the room with a dust sheet
  • Replacement brush heads are widely stocked

Against

  • Bends and offsets limit what a hand kit reaches

05

Rotary chimney sweeping kit for cordless drill

Best for heavier deposits

A flexible rod with a brush head that spins when driven by a drill. It clears heavier soot and glazed tar more thoroughly than pushing a brush up and down by hand. It also puts more stress on the flue, and the maker’s guidance on drill speed and liner type needs reading properly. Used on a flexible liner it can damage it. If there is thick, shiny, tar-like glaze on the flue, that is a sign the stove has been run too cool for too long, and a professional sweep is the better call than a drill.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Dry fuel beats any gadget. Wood seasoned to below about 20 per cent moisture burns hot and clean; wet wood smoulders, coats the flue with tar and does most of the damage this guide is trying to prevent. A cheap moisture meter pushed into a freshly split log tells you more than a flue dial will. Kiln-dried or properly seasoned wood is what stove manufacturers specify.

Sweeping frequency is not a guess. Sweeping trade and HETAS guidance is generally at least once a year for smokeless fuel, and more often, commonly twice a year, for wood burnt through the heating season. Check the stove manual and your insurer, because some insurers ask for a recent sweep certificate.

Look up the flue. You can check a lot with a head torch and a borescope: glazing on the liner, a cracked flue joint, light coming through where it should not. Anything unusual means a sweep or a HETAS registered installer, not a DIY fix.

Protect yourself. Soot and old ash are not nice to breathe. A respirator mask and a dust sheet cost far less than redecorating a living room.

A measurement is not a safety test. Combustion analysers belong to the engineer, as the flue gas analyser guide explains. A flue thermometer will not tell you whether a stove is producing carbon monoxide, and nor will sweeping it. The alarm does that.

What salesmen push that doesn’t matter. Stove-top fans do move warm air, but they are an accessory, not a fix for a stove running badly. Chimney cleaning logs and powders sold as an alternative to sweeping, and digital thermometers with alarms and apps that cost many times the price of a dial, are the other common upsells. A good dial and a booked sweep cover nearly everything.

Questions I get asked

What temperature should a wood burning stove run at? Most stove makers and sweeps describe a working band on a single-wall flue pipe of roughly 100–250°C surface temperature, with hotter than that a sign of overfiring. Dial markings vary, so follow the one printed for your stove and thermometer.

Where do you put a stove thermometer? On the flue pipe, usually 150mm or so above the stove top, where the pipe is single-wall steel. On a stove with a double-wall pipe, place it on the stove body or use an infrared thermometer instead, and follow the stove’s manual.

How often should I sweep a chimney with a wood burner? Typically at least once a year, and twice a year if the stove is used heavily on wood through the winter. Smokeless fuel is usually once a year. Do it before the season starts, not in the middle of it.

Can I sweep my own chimney? If the flue is straight, accessible and you have the right brush for the liner, yes, and many people do. A professional sweep also inspects the flue and gives you a certificate, so I would still book one at least every other year, and always if there is glazing, a smell or smoke coming back into the room.

If you are buying a house with a stove or a chimney that has seen better days, a building survey will look at the hearth, the flue route, the stack and the pots and say what needs sorting before you light it. Get in touch to arrange one. For the rest of the kit, there are more field-kit guides.
