Snagging survey
cost in 2026

A snagging survey on a new build typically costs £250–£600 in 2026, depending on the size of the property. It’s one of the cheapest ways to protect yourself on the single biggest purchase most people make — and one of the surveys buyers skip most often, usually to their cost.

Published 28 Aug 2026 Reading time · 8 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

A snagging survey on a new build typically costs £250–£600 in 2026, rising for larger houses and falling for a small flat. Against the price of the property itself, and against what a developer's own snagging list tends to miss, it's a small outlay. The bigger question isn't really the fee — it's timing, and whether you use the report while the developer still has a reason to act on it.

New-build defects are the norm, not the exception. Survey after survey of new-home buyers finds the overwhelming majority report at least some problem after moving in, and a meaningful minority report a long list. A professional, independent snagging inspection catches far more than the walk-round most buyers do themselves, because it's done by someone who does this for a living, isn't emotionally invested in finally getting the keys, and knows exactly where builders cut corners under time pressure.

I offer new-build snagging inspections as part of my building survey work, so I've a professional interest here — but the figures below are researched independently across current 2026 UK snagging providers, cross-checked against each other, not pulled from my own price list. What follows is what a snagging survey actually costs, what drives the price, and why the timing matters more than almost anything else about it.

The short answer

Figures below were checked on 28 August 2026 against current 2026 pricing from UK snagging inspection providers, cross-referenced against each other. As with the rest of this series, London and the South East, Dom's patch included, trend above the national average, and RICS-regulated inspectors typically charge more than non-regulated alternatives for a comparable property.

Those figures are for a standard pre-completion or early post-completion inspection with a written photographic report. A RICS-regulated inspector will often sit toward the top of each band, or above it; a non-regulated specialist snagging firm typically undercuts that but should still be able to show you sample reports and insurance before you instruct them.

What drives the price

Size and layout. More rooms, more elevations and more fittings simply take longer to check properly — a two-hour visit on a flat can stretch to four hours or more on a larger detached house.

RICS regulation. HomeSnag is currently the only RICS-regulated snagging provider in the UK; RICS regulation brings professional indemnity insurance and a formal complaints route, and inspectors who carry it price accordingly.

Report depth. A basic visual snag list costs less than a report with thermal imaging added, which can pick up missing or poorly fitted insulation that a visual check alone would miss.

Re-inspection. Most providers charge separately to come back and check the developer's fixes actually held — worth budgeting for from the outset rather than treating as an afterthought.

Location. As with most trades in this series, London and the South East commonly run above the rest of the UK for a comparable inspection.

Turnaround. An expedited report, useful if you're up against a tight completion date, sometimes carries a premium over the standard 24-48 hour delivery.

Timing matters more than the fee

This is the part buyers get wrong most often, and it costs them far more than the survey fee ever would. The best time to have a new build snagged is in the window between the developer telling you the property is ready and your legal completion — while they still hold the keys and still have a straightforward financial incentive to fix what you find before they get paid in full. Once you've completed, that leverage doesn't vanish, but it weakens: you're now chasing a company that's already been paid, through a warranty process rather than a pre-completion sign-off.

If pre-completion timing wasn't possible, the next best thing is to move fast afterwards — ideally within the first few weeks of moving in, while the defects are fresh, easy to document, and still squarely within the developer's early-stage obligation to put things right. Most new builds in England and Wales carry a structural warranty (commonly NHBC Buildmark, though there are several providers) that puts the developer on the hook for workmanship and materials defects for the first two years, with structural cover running for up to ten. A snagging report is only as useful as what you do with it: it's the evidence that turns "the floor creaks" into a dated, photographed, itemised list a developer is contractually harder to wave away.

Independent inspectors typically find anywhere from 30 to 150 or more individual snags on a freshly built home — from the trivial (a scuff, a gap in sealant) to the genuinely consequential (a poorly sealed roof junction, a door that doesn't close, evidence of rushed insulation work). Buyer satisfaction survey data has repeatedly shown the large majority of new-build purchasers report at least some problem after moving in, and a meaningful proportion report well into double figures. None of that is an argument against buying new — it's an argument for checking before you rely on the developer's own sign-off, which is written by the people who built it, not for you.

Do you actually need a professional, or can you do it yourself?

You can absolutely do a version of this yourself, and I'd encourage every new-build buyer to walk every room slowly with a torch and a notepad regardless of whether they also book an inspector. What a professional adds is pattern recognition: they've seen the same developer's finishing standards across dozens of plots, know which junctions and details tend to be rushed, and check things a first-time buyer typically wouldn't think to — loft insulation continuity, damp-proof course detailing at door thresholds, whether trickle vents were actually fitted, whether the boiler was commissioned correctly rather than just switched on. A good snagging report also carries more weight with a site manager than a homeowner's own list, simply because it's dated, itemised and comes from someone with no reason to exaggerate.

Where I'd say it's genuinely optional: a small, simple flat from a developer with a strong local reputation for finishing quality, where you're also planning your own careful walk-round before completion. Where I'd say book one without much hesitation: anything larger or more complex, any developer you don't already have a read on, and any purchase where the completion timetable is tight enough that you won't have time to do a thorough job yourself.

What I'd watch for in a quote

  • Insurance and accreditation confirmed — ask whether the inspector is RICS-regulated or carries professional indemnity insurance, and what happens if something significant gets missed.
  • Report turnaround stated upfront — most reputable providers deliver within 24-48 hours; if you're against a completion deadline, confirm this before booking, not after.
  • Re-inspection priced separately and clearly — know what it costs to check the developer's fixes before you need it, so it isn't a surprise add-on.
  • Sample report available — a genuine specialist will happily show you an example; if they won't, that tells you something.
  • Thermal imaging included or optional — useful for catching insulation gaps, worth asking whether it's bundled in or an extra.

Questions I get asked

How much does a snagging survey cost in 2026? For a 1-2 bed flat, typically £250–£400. A 2-3 bed house usually runs £350–£500, and a 4-5 bed detached £450–£650 or more. A re-inspection after the developer has attempted fixes usually adds £100–£250.

When should I book a snagging survey? Ideally between the developer notifying you the property is ready and legal completion, while they still hold the keys and still have an incentive to fix things. If that's already passed, book as early as possible after moving in.

Is a snagging survey worth it on a new build? For most buyers, yes. Independent inspectors typically find 30 to 150 or more snags on a freshly built home, and most new-build buyers report at least some problem after moving in. A few hundred pounds spent finding them while the developer is still obliged to fix them is cheap against the alternative.

Does a snagging survey replace my new-build warranty? No. The survey generates the defect list; the warranty (commonly NHBC Buildmark) is what obliges the developer to fix it, typically for the first two years, with structural cover running for ten. The survey only works if you use it before the early fix period runs out.
