The best
immersion heater
timers

A fair few of the all-electric flats and cottages I survey — no gas main, hot water from a cylinder and an immersion element — have the immersion switched to permanently on at the wall, timer bypassed years ago and never reconnected. Someone's paying full daytime rate for water that's been hot since two in the morning. A proper timer, matched to whatever off-peak tariff is actually on the meter, is about the cheapest genuine saving going in a property like that: no rewiring, no new cylinder, usually a same-afternoon swap on the existing backbox. Here's how I'd judge one, and seven worth buying.

An immersion heater timer does one job: it makes sure the element only draws current during the window you actually want to pay for, instead of whenever someone last left the wall switch. On a straightforward single-rate electricity tariff that's mostly about convenience — not leaving three kilowatts running all day for a bath nobody's had yet. On an Economy 7, Economy 10 or comparable time-of-use tariff, it's the whole economic case for the cylinder: the cheap overnight rate only pays off if the immersion is actually heating water during that window, and switched off — or on a proper boost — the rest of the time. I see the same finding often enough to flag it here first: a working timer on the wall, dial or display dead, immersion left on the manual override because nobody could work out how to reset it after the last power cut or the clocks changing. That's not a fault, it's just a timer nobody's used properly in years, quietly costing peak-rate money on every tank.

Horstmann is the name most people over forty still say when they mean this category, but the brand itself is now Secure — Horstmann became part of Secure Meters some years back, and the familiar Electronic 7 controller was discontinued and replaced by the E7+ below. Timeguard remains the other name you'll see on the wall in most UK homes, usually a plain mechanical dial rather than anything branded for a specific tariff. Between the two brands and a scattering of generic WiFi switches now selling on the back of the smart-plug trend, there's a genuinely useful range here from a £15 mechanical dial to a £90 app-controlled unit, and the right one depends far more on what's already on the wall and what tariff is actually running than on how much the box costs.

Worth saying plainly before the picks: none of this works without a real off-peak tariff behind it. A timer on a single-rate meter saves whatever it saves by not leaving the immersion on longer than needed, which is real but modest. The bigger saving only exists if the property is actually on Economy 7, Economy 10 or a similar time-of-use arrangement, and the timer's on/off window is set to match the hours that tariff is actually cheap — not a generic midnight-to-seven default that may or may not line up with the supplier's real off-peak period.

How I judge an immersion heater timer

Load rating matched to the element, not just the price bracket. Most single-element immersion heaters draw around 3kW; some twin-element cylinders run closer to 3.68kW when both elements are on together. Check the timer's resistive rating — 13A/3kW and 16A/3.68kW both turn up here — against the rating plate on the immersion heater itself, not just whichever unit happens to be cheapest.

Genuine off-peak alignment, not a generic default. A timer set to a textbook midnight-to-seven window is worth nothing if the actual tariff's cheap period runs 01:00–08:00, or varies by supplier. Confirm the real off-peak hours on the bill or with the supplier, then set the timer to match — every unit here can be reprogrammed, but none of them know the tariff without being told.

A boost function that doesn't mean leaving it on override. Every household needs hot water outside the scheduled window occasionally. A proper 30, 60 or 120-minute boost button covers that without switching the whole thing to permanently-on and forgetting to switch it back — which is exactly the fault I find on survey.

Memory that survives a power cut. A mechanical dial can't lose its programme — the dial just sits where it was left. A digital unit needs genuine battery backup, or a short outage resets the clock to midnight and quietly stops saving anything until someone notices the display flashing.

What's actually on the wall already. A straight swap for an existing timer, on the same backbox and pin spacing, is a same-afternoon job for a competent person. A change in format — dial to digital, switched spur to a different footprint — is more likely to need an electrician, and matters more than any feature on the spec sheet.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Off-peak tariff eligibility is the whole economic case. None of this saves serious money on a standard single-rate tariff, where electricity at 3am costs the same as electricity at 6pm. Confirm Economy 7, Economy 10 or a comparable time-of-use tariff is actually active, and check the real off-peak hours on the bill rather than assuming a textbook window — every timer here needs telling, none of them know on their own.

Matching the load rating is a safety check, not just a spec comparison. A 13A/3kW timer on a genuinely 3.68kW twin-element load is a real fault, not a rounding error. Check the rating plate on the immersion heater itself before ordering, and if there's any doubt, have an electrician confirm it.

“Plug-in” is misleading for most immersion circuits. Most UK immersion heaters are wired through a fused connection unit or a 20A double-pole switch, not a standard 13A socket. A genuinely plug-in-style unit only suits the minority of installations where the existing arrangement is already a socket outlet — check what's actually on the wall before assuming a product description applies to the property.

What it does to the EPC is smaller than what it does to the bill. RdSAP records the water heating control type, and a proper timer is recognised over no control at all, but the effect on the certificate itself is modest next to the effect on the running cost — see what an EPC costs if the property's due one anyway.

Smart control is a convenience, not the saving itself. An app is genuinely useful on a property nobody occupies every day, where a remote boost avoids a cold shower on arrival. But the actual saving comes from the schedule matching the real tariff window, which a £15 mechanical dial does exactly as well as a £90 app-controlled unit — check the schedule matches the tariff before paying extra for the interface.

Questions I get asked

Will an immersion timer really save me money? Only if there's a genuine off-peak tariff behind it and the timer's window is set to match it. Without Economy 7, Economy 10 or similar, the saving is limited to not leaving the element running longer than needed — real, but modest next to what the tariff itself delivers when the timer's actually aligned to it.

Do I need an electrician to fit one? A straight swap for an existing timer, on the same backbox and wiring, is within reach of a confident DIYer for some models, but always isolate the circuit first and check the connections match. Anything involving a new circuit, a change in format, or any doubt at all should go to a qualified electrician — this is a fixed wiring job, not a plug-in gadget for most properties.

What's the real difference between a mechanical dial and a digital timeswitch? Precision and memory, mostly. A mechanical segment dial like the Timeguard NTT01 gives fixed steps — typically 15 minutes — and never loses its programme because there's nothing to reset. A digital unit can be more precise and adds a proper boost function, but needs genuine battery backup to survive a power cut without reverting to a default schedule.

Can I just use a smart plug instead of a proper immersion timer? Not usually. Most immersion heaters aren't wired to a socket at all, and even where one is, a generic smart plug is rarely rated or certified for a continuous, resistive load that size on a wet-area appliance circuit. A purpose-built control — mechanical or smart — rated for the job is the safer route, not a repurposed plug meant for a lamp.
