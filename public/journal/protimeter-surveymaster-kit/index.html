The Protimeter SurveyMaster is the instrument the UK damp industry standardised on, and it shows up in this practice’s work constantly — not carried by me, but referenced in the paperwork: damp reports from other surveyors, insurance loss-adjuster assessments, retrofit PAS 2035 condition reports, all quoting readings from “the Protimeter” as if the brand name were the unit of measurement. It more or less is. So the question I get isn’t “which damp meter should I buy”, which is what the standalone damp meters guide answers — it’s narrower and more specific: what does a SurveyMaster reading of 22 actually mean, what’s the difference between the two buttons on the front, and why does the shop also sell an Aquant, a Mini and something called an MMS3.

This page answers that. The SurveyMaster is not one meter but the centre of a small system: a dual-mode instrument that takes a family of plug-in probes, sits alongside cheaper and more expensive siblings built from the same sensing technology, and reports everything on a scale that only makes sense once you know what it isn’t claiming to measure.

Everything below is drawn from Protimeter’s own published specifications and documentation, plus the wider feedback from people who use these professionally — features and functions, not a lab comparison. How the SurveyMaster actually works

Two modes, one instrument. Flip the SurveyMaster over and the back is a flat search pad; the front carries a pair of pins. Search mode uses the pad to sense moisture non-invasively through paint, plaster and coverings like tile and vinyl to roughly 19mm (¾in) deep, without marking anything — Protimeter’s own material calls it a way to find higher-than-normal moisture, not to diagnose it. Measure mode is the pins: press them to the surface, or drive them in for a proper reading in timber, for a direct percentage. The whole instrument is built around that workflow — scan wide, then confirm the spots that matter.

The WME scale, honestly. On timber, the pins report actual moisture content. On masonry — brick, plaster, mortar, block — there is no equivalent physical quantity, so the display reports wood moisture equivalent (WME) instead: a number calibrated against timber that tells you how wet a wall is relative to a dry one, not a moisture percentage it can’t actually know. The colour bands do the same job as the numbers, at a glance: broadly, a low double-digit reading sits in the green (dry) zone, high teens into the twenties is amber (borderline), and sustained readings above that turn red (wet) — Protimeter’s own examples cite 14% as a green-zone timber reading and 25% as a red-zone one. Search mode’s non-invasive reading uses a related but separate 0–1000 scale, “loosely based on” WME rather than reporting it directly, which is one more reason a Search hit always needs confirming in Measure mode before you trust it.

Calibration you can actually check. Protimeter sell a small accessory called the Calcheck: clip it across the pins and a correctly functioning instrument should read 18.1% ±1%. It also tests the hammer electrode and other plug-in accessories, which matters if a reading is ever going to be quoted as evidence — being able to show the instrument was verified on the day is worth more than the accessory’s £20 price tag.

Bluetooth and the Connect app. The current model pairs to Protimeter Connect to save readings with a time and date stamp, rather than relying on a paper record written up later from memory. Useful for anyone building a formal damp report or a before-and-after drying record; entirely skippable if you just want a number on a wall.

Built to take probes. The pins on the front are removable, and the same body accepts a family of accessory probes — deep wall, extension, hammer electrode, and more specialist attachments like an EIFS probe for rendered insulation systems. That’s the real difference between “a meter” and “a system”: the SurveyMaster on its own answers most questions, and the accessories below answer the ones it can’t reach.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

A Protimeter reading is a screening tool, not a diagnosis. This is true of every meter in the range, flagship included. It tells you where moisture — or something that conducts like it — is. It does not tell you why. Rising damp, penetrating damp, condensation, a leak and hygroscopic salt contamination can all produce a similar number, and they have entirely different remedies at entirely different prices. The pattern of readings across a wall, the building’s construction and ventilation, and what’s actually visible do the diagnosing; the meter narrows down where to look.

The probe you need depends on the job, not the budget. A deep wall probe answers “is this at depth in the cavity”; an extension probe answers “what’s happening in that crawl space or cavity fill”; a hammer electrode answers “how wet is this slab or hardwood floor”. None of them are more or less “professional” than the others — buying the one that matches the actual question in front of you beats buying the whole accessory case on spec.

Don’t buy the MMS3 for what the SurveyMaster already does. The extra sensing modes — relative humidity probing, infrared, deeper non-invasive reach — only earn their keep on work that specifically needs them: concrete-slab RH testing before flooring goes down, or restoration work logging hundreds of readings across a flooded building. For a general damp inspection, all that extra capability sits unused.

Calibration is the thing people skip and shouldn’t. A Calcheck costs less than a round of drinks and takes ten seconds to use. If a reading might ever be quoted back at you — in a dispute, a report, an insurance claim — being able to say the instrument was verified that day is worth far more than its price.

What the range doesn’t need: buying every accessory “just in case”. The probe family exists because different materials genuinely need different contact methods, not because more attachments make a better surveyor. Match the probe to the material in front of you.

Questions I get asked

What does a WME reading of 20 actually mean on a Protimeter? On timber it’s close to an actual moisture content and worth taking fairly literally — into the amber zone, worth watching. On masonry it’s relative, not absolute: compare it against a reading from a known-dry part of the same wall, of the same construction, rather than treating the number on its own as a verdict. A uniform reading of 20 across a whole house is a different story to one patch reading 20 against a background of 12.

Should I buy the SurveyMaster or the Aquant? The SurveyMaster if you want one instrument that both scans and confirms — which is what most damp inspection work actually needs. The Aquant only if your work is genuinely non-invasive-only: listed interiors, finished surfaces you can’t mark, or you already own a pin meter and want a dedicated scanning tool alongside it.

Is the MMS3 worth the extra money over the SurveyMaster? For a general building or damp survey, no — you’re paying roughly three times as much for sensing modes you won’t use. For flood or fire restoration work, concrete-slab relative-humidity testing before flooring is laid, or any job that needs hundreds of logged, timestamped readings, yes: it’s a genuinely different instrument for a genuinely different job, not a status upgrade.

How do I know my Protimeter is still reading correctly? Use a Calcheck device: clipped across the pins, a correctly functioning instrument reads 18.1% ±1%. It also verifies the hammer electrode and other plug-in accessories. Protimeter’s current SurveyMaster additionally runs an automatic calibration check roughly every 50 power cycles, but a physical Calcheck is the only way to get a number you can point to and say the instrument was verified.

Whatever the reading says, what it means for the building — and what, if anything, needs doing about it — is the actual survey.
