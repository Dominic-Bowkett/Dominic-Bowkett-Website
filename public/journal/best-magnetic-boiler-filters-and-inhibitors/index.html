Every wet central heating system is quietly corroding itself. Steel radiators react with the oxygen in the water and produce magnetite — a fine black iron oxide sludge that settles in radiators, the heat exchanger and every low point in the pipework. A little is normal. Left unmanaged, it shows up on a survey with predictable regularity: radiators cold at the bottom despite bleeding, a boiler pump working harder than it should, and eventually pinholes in radiators thinned from the inside over a decade. None of it is dramatic on the day. All of it is expensive by the time someone notices.

The fix has two parts, and this is the bit people miss. A magnetic filter is a mechanical trap plumbed into the return pipe near the boiler that pulls circulating magnetite out of the water before it reaches the heat exchanger, ready to flush out at a service. A chemical inhibitor is dosed into the system to slow the corrosion producing new magnetite, and often manages limescale too. The filter catches what’s already there; the inhibitor stops more being made. Fit one without the other and it’s half a job.

It’s also increasingly not optional. Boiler manufacturers routinely make a system filter and an approved inhibitor a written warranty condition, naming specific brands — fit something off their list and a claim can be refused on exactly that technicality. Worth reading your own boiler’s warranty booklet before buying anything here. What follows is the filter I’d fit as standard, the inhibitor I’d pair with it, and the honest alternatives either side on price. How I judge a filter and an inhibitor

Named on the manufacturer’s approved list. This matters more than any spec sheet. Worcester Bosch, Vaillant, Ideal, Baxi and the rest each publish which filters and inhibitors satisfy their warranty conditions, and it isn’t always the same brand as the boiler. Buying whatever’s cheapest and assuming it’s fine is how warranty claims get refused on a technicality years later.

Genuine filtration, not just a strainer. A magnetic filter has to actually catch magnetite — fine, black, and mostly invisible until it’s built up. Reputable brands publish independent capture-rate data; unbranded budget filters mostly don’t, which tells you something.

Serviceable without draining the system. Good filters have isolation valves either side and a drain point, so an engineer can empty the sludge chamber at a service in minutes rather than draining the whole system. A filter nobody can be bothered to clean is dead weight within a year.

Simple, correct dosing for the inhibitor. One bottle, one system size, treats-to-X-litres on the label, compliant with BS 7593. Measuring cylinders and multi-stage dosing regimes belong in commercial plant rooms, not a semi-detached with a combi boiler.

Built to stay in the system for years. This is now a permanent fitting. Housing quality, seal life and spares availability matter more here than on almost anything else in a field-kit guide.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

You need both, not either/or. This is the single most common mistake I see: a filter fitted with no inhibitor dosed, or an inhibitor topped up into a system that’s never been filtered or flushed. The filter deals with what’s already circulating; the inhibitor stops more being produced. A filter in untreated water fills with sludge fast; an inhibitor dosed into a system already thick with years of magnetite is treating water that’s already lost the fight.

Read the actual warranty wording before buying anything. Boiler manufacturers name specific approved filters and inhibitors — not always the same brand as each other, or of the boiler itself. A claim can be, and has been, refused because the fitted filter wasn’t on the approved list. Five minutes with the warranty booklet before ordering saves that argument entirely.

A filter doesn’t reverse existing damage. If a system is already knocking or radiators stay cold at the top despite bleeding, a filter and inhibitor won’t undo pinholed radiators or a heat exchanger already partially scaled. That’s a power flush and a conversation with a Gas Safe registered engineer — a filter and inhibitor are what you fit afterwards to stop it happening again, not instead of the flush.

What gets pushed that doesn’t matter as much as it sounds. Wi-Fi monitoring is genuinely useful for landlords, and largely decorative for a household already on an annual service — the filtration underneath is usually identical to the non-smart version at half the price. Dosing pots are a convenience, not a performance upgrade over a bottle poured in properly at commissioning.

Most Gas Safe engineers do this at a service or a flush anyway. Fitting a filter and dosing an inhibitor correctly is straightforward for a competent DIYer with the right tools, but most people have it done as part of a service, a power flush, or a new boiler install — ask for it by name if it isn’t already quoted.

Questions I get asked

Do I need a magnetic filter and an inhibitor, or is one enough? Both, ideally, and most boiler warranties now require both as a condition of cover. The filter is mechanical, trapping magnetite already circulating; the inhibitor is chemical, slowing the corrosion that produces new magnetite and protecting against limescale. Fitting just one is a common shortcut that undermines the point of doing either.

Will a magnetic filter fix noisy pipes or cold radiators? Not on its own, and not immediately. A filter stops the problem getting worse and, over time, reduces circulating sludge. But a radiator cold at the top because of a solid layer of sludge already sitting in it needs bleeding, and possibly power-flushing, to actually clear that radiator — the filter protects the boiler and prevents re-contamination afterwards, it doesn’t retroactively clean radiators already blocked.

How do I know what my boiler’s warranty actually requires? Check the warranty booklet or manufacturer’s website — most publish an approved products list or state the requirement plainly. If a Gas Safe registered installer did the work, ask what they fitted and whether it satisfies the warranty; it should be on the benchmark commissioning checklist left with the boiler.

How often does the inhibitor need topping up? Check every five years, or immediately after any drain-down (a radiator replacement, a leak repair, a power flush) since draining removes the inhibitor with the water. Manufacturers sell simple test strips that check concentration in minutes; an engineer can also check it at a routine service.
