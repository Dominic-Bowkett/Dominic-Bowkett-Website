The best
smoke pens,
for tracing draughts and air leaks

A smoke pen answers a question a hand held near a window never quite settles: which way is the air actually moving, and from where. A thin, controllable trail of smoke bends toward or away from a gap the second you hold it there — cheaper and faster than a blower door test, and usually the first thing worth reaching for before anyone starts sealing gaps on a guess. Here’s what these tools actually show you, what they don’t, and seven worth carrying, from a £6 tub of matches to a £90 professional kit.

A fair number of “draughty room” call-outs turn out to have nothing to do with the window everyone’s pointing at. The cold edge is real, but the air’s coming up through a suspended timber floor at the skirting, or down a loft hatch that’s never sealed properly, or being pulled backwards down a chimney by an extractor fan three rooms away doing exactly what it’s meant to do. A hand held near the frame tells you almost nothing reliable — body heat and draught both read as “a bit cold”. A thin trail of smoke settles it in seconds: it bends, stalls, or streams, and there’s no interpreting required.

On site that means holding a smoke source near a skirting board, a loft hatch edge, a service penetration, a boxed-in soil pipe, or the reveal of a window that’s been draught-proofed twice already and still isn’t right, and watching which way the trail moves. It’s also the quickest way to check whether ventilation is actually doing its job under Part F 2022’s whole-dwelling strategy — does that trickle vent draw, does the continuous extract fan in the bathroom actually pull air rather than just spin, is a newly-fitted MVHR unit pressurising the rooms it’s meant to. Paired with a thermal camera, smoke turns a cold patch on the image into an answer — cold bridge or moving air — in the time it takes to hold the trail up to the wall.

What a smoke pen won’t give you is a number. It tells you where air is moving and roughly how freely; it doesn’t tell you the air change rate, and it’s not evidence for a Part L or PAS 2035 air permeability sign-off — that comes from a blower door test with a calibrated fan and a manometer, where smoke’s role is only to help whoever’s holding the door rig see where the air the fan’s pulling is actually entering from. Seven worth having below, reusable pens through to disposable matches, and which end of that range actually suits the job in front of you.

How I judge a smoke pen

How controllable the trail is. The job is pinpointing one gap, not fogging a room. A thin, directable stream you can hold an inch from a skirting board tells you something specific; a dense cloud that fills the room in five seconds tells you a draught exists somewhere in it, which you probably knew already, and it can set off a smoke alarm halfway through the survey.

Running cost over a year of surveys. A reusable pen or pencil costs more upfront but the wick or fluid refill is pence per test; a tub of matches or pellets costs less to buy but each one is gone after a single 20–30 second burn. Do the arithmetic against how often it’ll actually come out of the bag.

What’s actually in the smoke. The products worth buying now use a glycerine or glycol-based vapour, food-grade and non-toxic, rather than the older chemical smoke compositions some trade suppliers still sell for gas spillage work. Worth checking before use in an occupied home, especially with children, pets or anyone with respiratory sensitivity present.

Whether it needs a naked flame. A wick or a match needs lighting; a battery-heated smoke pencil doesn’t. That’s a genuine consideration near loft insulation, timber, or a client who’d rather you didn’t strike a match indoors, even briefly.

Burn time against the size of the job. A single loft hatch check needs seconds of smoke; a slow pass around every window and service penetration in an older house needs a source that lasts. Match the total smoke output on offer to how much of the house you’re actually covering that visit.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

Density isn’t the point, control is. Marketing around these products leans on the biggest, thickest cloud a source can produce, as if more smoke were automatically better. On site the opposite is usually true: a room-filling plume tells you a draught exists somewhere, which you likely knew before lighting anything, while a thin, directable trail held right at a specific gap tells you something you can act on. A dense cloud also has a habit of tripping the nearest smoke alarm, which nobody thanks you for mid-survey.

Reusable pays for itself fast if it comes out often. A pen costing £35–£40 with hundreds of tests in it is cheaper within a few months than buying matches every week; a £6–£13 tub of matches makes more sense for occasional use where a pen would mostly sit in a drawer. Worth doing that arithmetic against how often smoke tracing actually features in the work, rather than buying the most capable option by default.

BS 5440 matters for gas work, not for chasing a draught. The compliance stamped on the Fumax pellets is specifically about flue spillage testing under gas safety protocol. It’s a genuine spec worth knowing exists, and irrelevant if the job in hand is a sash window or a loft hatch — no need to pay for a certification that isn’t answering the question being asked.

A smoke pen finds gaps, it doesn’t quantify them. For a Part L compliance figure or a PAS 2035 whole-house air permeability number, smoke on its own is the wrong tool entirely — that number comes from a blower door test with a calibrated fan and a manometer, sustained pressure difference and all. Smoke’s job there is to help whoever’s running the test see where the air being pulled through the building envelope is actually entering, not to put a figure on how much of it there is.

Where it earns its keep day to day. Loft hatches, suspended floor edges at the skirting, service penetrations, boxed-in soil pipes, and confirming whether an extract fan is genuinely extracting rather than just running — cheap, fast, visual confirmation before anyone spends money sealing the wrong gap. Pairing a positive result with an anemometer reading turns “air is moving here” into an actual extract rate worth writing in a report.

Questions I get asked

Do I need training or a licence to use a smoke pen? No. The products in this guide are unrestricted retail items built around food-grade glycerine and glycol vapour, not the genuinely hazardous chemical smoke generators — some using titanium tetrachloride — that older trade suppliers still sell for specialist gas work. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions on ventilation and disposal and there’s nothing else to arrange beforehand.

Can a smoke pen replace a blower door air-tightness test? No. A smoke pen is qualitative and directional — it shows where air moves, not how much. A Part L or PAS 2035 air permeability figure needs a calibrated blower door and manometer; smoke is useful during that test to help the operator see where the pressure difference is pulling air from, not as a stand-alone substitute for it.

Will it set off the smoke alarm? It can, particularly with a dense plume held close to an optical alarm head. Warn anyone in the property beforehand, keep the trail thin and away from the alarm sensor, and test the smallest amount that answers the question rather than the biggest cloud the source can produce.

What’s the actual difference between a smoke pen and smoke matches? Reusability. A pen or pencil takes a refillable wick or fluid reservoir and can run hundreds of individual tests before it needs restocking; matches and pellets are struck or lit once, burn for 20–30 seconds, and that’s the end of that one. Which suits better comes down to how often smoke tracing actually features in the work, covered above.

Chasing a draught back to its actual source, rather than the wall it happens to be felt against, is standard groundwork for the practice’s ventilation assessments and retrofit assessments, where Part F compliance and post-improvement airtightness both get checked properly rather than guessed at.
