The best
trickle vents
and what Part F actually asks for

Half the trickle vents I find on survey have been painted shut, filled with mastic, or removed altogether by someone chasing draughts. The other half are doing their job quietly and nobody notices. Most of what's written online about how big a trickle vent needs to be is a decade out of date — here's the current Approved Document F figure, the difference between a vent you can retrofit and one that needs the glass replacing, and six worth buying.

A trickle vent is the small, permanently-open slot built into or bolted onto a window frame that gives a room its background ventilation — the slow, constant trickle of air that stops moisture building up between the times an extractor fan runs or a window gets opened wide. It doesn't do the heavy lifting after a shower or a pan boiling over; that's the fan's job. What it does is stop a well-sealed, well-insulated modern window from turning a bedroom or living room into a sealed box that quietly steams up its own corners over a cold winter.

The figure most homeowners and even some installers still quote — 5,000mm² for a habitable room, 2,500mm² for a wet room — was the 2010 edition of Approved Document F. The 2021 edition, in force since 15 June 2022, raised those minimums substantially: 8,000mm² for a habitable room or kitchen in a multi-storey dwelling, 10,000mm² in a single-storey dwelling, and 4,000mm² for a bathroom either way. There's no minimum window-to-floor-area calculation that decides whether background ventilation applies at all — if the room has an external wall, Table 1.7 applies regardless of how big the window is. A single small retrofit vent bought off a shelf rarely clears the higher figure on its own, which is worth knowing before you assume one vent per window is automatically enough.

If the problem is condensation running well beyond the window reveal — landing ceilings, cold corners, wardrobes on an external wall — a trickle vent on its own won't fix it; that's usually a whole-house ventilation question, and a PIV unit or a proper MVHR installation is the more likely answer. If you're weighing up new windows anyway, double glazing costs covers what a full replacement runs to, since the vent question usually gets decided at the same time as the frame. With that context, here's the trickle vent I'd fit first.

How I judge a trickle vent

Equivalent area against the current Table 1.7 figure, not the old one. 8,000–10,000mm² for a habitable room, 4,000mm² for a bathroom, is the 2021 figure. Most of what's written online still quotes the smaller 2010 numbers.

Independently tested area, not just the box figure. The mm² quoted is measured to BS EN 13141-1 at 1Pa, in theory a level playing field — but as one pick below shows, a published third-party test found a real gap between a claimed figure and what was actually measured.

Frame-mounted retrofit versus glazed-in. A surface-mounted or routed slot vent bolts onto the existing frame with no glass work. A glazed-in vent runs through the sealed unit itself and can only be fitted when the glass is being replaced — a fabricator-specified item, not something you buy and fit yourself.

Acoustic attenuation where it's actually needed. A plain open slot does nothing for road noise. Worth the extra cost on a busy-road elevation; unnecessary on a quiet one.

Colour, slot size and fixing centres matching what's already there. A vent that doesn't match the existing routed slot or screw centres means more cutting, not a clean swap.

Frame-mounted, glazed-in or acoustic — and when a shelf-bought vent isn't enough

Every product above is a frame-mounted retrofit vent: it bolts onto or routs into the existing frame, and any competent DIYer can fit one in minutes. That's not the only kind. A glazed-in vent runs through the sealed glass unit itself — Titon's own Maxiglaze system gives 12,000mm² per metre of extrusion, well clear of the habitable-room figure in a single vent — but it can only be specified when the glass is being re-manufactured, so it's ordered through your window installer, not bought off a shelf. There's also an acoustic tier: Titon's SF Xtra Sound Attenuator is rated up to roughly 44dB attenuation open, built to actually cut road noise rather than just admit it with the air. Neither is a retail SKU I could verify in stock on Amazon at a fixed price, so neither gets a buy button here — ask your installer to price one in if the plain vents above don't fit the job.

Which is the honest answer to "when isn't a shelf-bought vent enough": a single 4,000–4,400mm² retrofit vent doesn't clear the current 8,000mm² (multi-storey) or 10,000mm² habitable-room figure on its own. Two vents per window is the common practical fix; a glazed-in strip is the other. If you're replacing windows rather than retrofitting, Part F's own retrofit rule (paragraph 3.14–3.15) says the new window's vents must be at least as large as whatever was there before, or hit the Table 1.7 figure where none existed — one more reason to check what your installer is actually specifying.

What actually matters — and what doesn’t

The number on the box versus the number independently tested. The Greenwood 5000EA pick above is the clearest example: a claimed figure and an independently tested one aren't always the same thing. Trust third-party test data over the marketing spec where you can find it.

One vent is often not the whole answer any more. The habitable-room figure nearly doubled between the 2010 and 2021 editions of Part F. A small retrofit vent that cleared the old rule comfortably often falls short of the current one.

Closing a vent to stop a draught just moves the problem indoors. Most of these have an adjustable or closable flap, tempting to shut in winter. Do that permanently and the moisture it was clearing has to go somewhere — usually the cold corner behind the wardrobe.

Colour matters more than it looks like it should. A white vent on a woodgrain or anthracite frame is a small thing I flag on survey purely because it looks like an afterthought — check the finish before ordering, not after fitting.

On a listed building or in a conservation area, a routed slot is an alteration. Cutting a new vent slot, or fitting one to the front elevation, is exactly the kind of small change a conservation officer can have a view on. Check before you cut.

Questions I get asked

Do I still need trickle vents if I already have an extractor fan? Yes — they do different jobs. An extractor fan clears a burst of moisture after cooking or showering; a trickle vent provides the constant low-level background ventilation Part F expects a habitable room to have all the time, fan running or not.

Will fitting trickle vents make my house colder or hurt my EPC? The heat loss through a trickle vent is genuinely small next to what's lost through the glass and frame around it, and RdSAP doesn't penalise a compliant vent. Blocking existing vents to save heat is a false economy that shows up as a damp or mould finding on the next survey, not a real saving.

Can I fit a trickle vent myself? A surface-mounted retrofit vent like the ones above is a straightforward job for a confident DIYer — mark, drill, screw. Routing a new slot into a frame that's never had one, or anything glazed-in, is a job for whoever's doing the glazing.

Is my installer required to fit trickle vents when they replace my windows? In substance, yes: if the old window had vents, the new one must have vents at least as large; if it didn't, the installer should get as close to the current Table 1.7 figure as feasible. A FENSA or CERTASS-registered installer should build this into the spec unasked, not treat it as an optional extra.
