Surveyor’s notes
8 October 2026:
Flats at a loss

A flat national price picture with the South slipping, a repeat-sales study on flats, and the first detail on what landlords must hand over to the new database. Here is what each means on the ground.

Published 8 October 2026 Reading time · 5 min By Dominic Bowkett · MRPSA

Today is about the housing market and the paperwork around it. Prices have stopped moving, the South is doing worse than the rest, and flats are carrying most of the pain. Alongside that, the landlord database has acquired a fee, a document list and a privacy question nobody has answered.

Flat prices nationally, falling prices in the South

Landlord Today reports Lloyds Bank’s latest index, the former Halifax series, putting the average UK price at £298,441, with no change on the month and no change on the year. Knight Frank’s Tom Bill is quoted warning that “downward price pressure will continue during the final months of the year” because of higher mortgage rates. Garrington Property Finders’ Jonathan Hopper says prices are rising in Scotland and northern England but falling in London, southern England and East Anglia, the places with the worst affordability.

That regional split matters more than the headline. A zero national figure hides a South East where buyers have the leverage. In practice that shows up as longer sales, price reductions and offers conditional on a survey. I would also treat any single month’s index with caution, since these are mortgage-based figures and the sample is not the whole market.

If you are selling a Sussex or Kent house this autumn, price it where the buyer’s mortgage lets it sit, not where last spring’s neighbour sold. If you are buying, a falling market is when a survey earns its fee, because you have room to renegotiate on what it finds. A damp chimney breast, tired roof coverings or a wet-room that was never tanked properly are all worth a reduction, and a surveyor’s report gives you the evidence to ask for it rather than just a feeling.

A third of South East flats resold at a loss, and the survey is the point

Also via Landlord Today, e.surv Chartered Surveyors looked at flats resold after five to ten years. In the 12 months to August 2026, nearly 40% in London sold for less than the previous purchase price, about a third in the South East and East of England, roughly 26% across Great Britain, and under 10% in Scotland. e.surv’s head of research, Rob Owens, points to building safety, tenure and service charges as the factors lenders and buyers should weigh.

This is a repeat-sales study of flats that changed hands, so it says nothing about flats that nobody can sell, and the real picture is probably worse. I read it as the cladding, lease-length and service-charge problem finally showing up in prices. The flat that looked fine at purchase can carry a major-works bill, a short lease or an unresolved fire-safety question that only appears in the management pack.

A flat survey has to look past the front door. Ask for the last three years of service charge accounts, the planned works, the lease length and any building safety paperwork before you exchange, and have the communal roof, balconies and windows looked at. An EPC and a good survey will not fix a flat with a bad lease, but they will tell you what you are buying.

The landlord database now has a price tag, and a privacy hole

Property118 reports that landlords will have to register each property on the new private rented sector database for £65 a year, supplying the address, number of residents, number of bedrooms and copies of gas, electrical and EPC documents. Empty properties must be registered before marketing. Propertymark wants a redacted version of the landlord’s address published rather than the full detail, and the article says the Ministry of Housing has only promised to follow data protection law, with specifics to be announced. It says the rollout starts in the West Midlands in December 2026.

The EPC requirement is the line I would underline. The database turns the EPC from something filed in a drawer into something the regulator can check, and it makes an out-of-date or missing certificate a registration problem as well as a letting one. Landlords whose last EPC is nearly ten years old, or who have never looked at the recommendations, will find that out at the worst moment.

My bet is that the document list is where small landlords will trip first. Gas and electrical certificates are routine for most, but EPCs are the one people forget, and a certificate that expired or never covered a converted loft or extension is a problem you can fix cheaply now and expensively later. If you have made changes to the building since the last assessment, say so to your assessor before they visit.

On privacy, Propertymark is right that a small landlord’s home address should not be searchable by anyone. Until the government says what is public, assume nothing and keep your records tidy.

What I’d actually do

  1. Sellers in the South East: price to the current market, not last spring, and have a recent EPC ready.
  2. Flat buyers: get service charge accounts, lease length and building safety information before exchange, and commission a survey that covers the communal parts.
  3. Landlords: check the date and recommendations on every EPC now, and keep gas, electrical and EPC certificates together in one folder.
  4. Landlords with void properties: budget for the £65 registration and the pre-marketing step when the database reaches you.
